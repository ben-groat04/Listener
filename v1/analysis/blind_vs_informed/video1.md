# video1: blind run against informed run

Compared: BLIND (`v1/analysis_blind/`: music_reading, interpretation, dossier; no context, research or web) and INFORMED (`v1/analysis/`: music_reading, interpretation, REPORT, dossier). Disputed points were checked against `v1/data/video1/` (frames f_0013, f_0025, f_0168, f_0188; `lyrics/` Whisper nl transcribe and translate; `music_views/per_second_levels.json`; `context.md`). I cannot hear audio; "music" claims below rest on the same measurements both runs used. British spelling. Labels: CHECKED (I verified it in the data), RELAYED (from a run's text, not re-verified).

## Verdict in brief

The two runs are far closer than the presence or absence of context would suggest. Both read the same stems, the same level file and the same Whisper text, and they reach the same music structure almost to the second. The blind run reached the correct subject, date, place and emotional register from the screen and the transcript alone. What it lacks is provenance (who, why, when made, how it was received). What the informed run adds is mostly framing, not new perception, and in two or three places that framing made its reading of the pictures slightly worse.

## 2. What the blind run got wrong or invented, and why

Fewer errors than expected, and most were hedged.

1. **The 0:12 discs.** It called them unidentifiable and let a panellist hear "eyes". CHECKED f_0013: a blown-out round lamp over two glowing round objects with curved rims. Still not identifiable from the frame. The informed run is no better (it calls them lamp reflectors or lanterns), so this is a data limit, not a blindness effect. The "eyes or projector" idea was later withdrawn.
2. **"Heartbeat" for the sub.** Withdrawn by the blind run itself (ten per minute is too slow). Cause: a human-feel word chosen before checking the rate. The informed run avoids it (a "second clock") but its own summary text still speaks of a "heartbeat" for the flutter.
3. **A "screening notice" reading of the 3:07 banner.** CHECKED f_0188: an indoor hall with strip lights, a pillar, hat-backed heads and hanging cards, under a banner "...uitvoerige Reportage / ELFSTEDENTOCHT / op de parterre". So the blind run's observation (indoor, a showing notice, medium confidence) was right and better than the informed run's flat "banner reading Reportage ELFSTEDENTOCHT". Its overreach was building a "who is this film for" theme on it, which it did label speculation and offered the cheaper alternative (just the next shot of a source reel).
4. **"Cinema report would not be cut to a click, so picture was re-set".** Reasoning from style with no outside fact; lucky rather than demonstrated, and it said so.
5. **Over-weighting of the newspapers as "cost on the record".** The headlines are real, but the blind run leaned on the press-negativity count to settle "neither celebration nor warning". Without the context (a famous brutal race, 127 finishers) it could not know how typical the headlines were, so the confidence in "bill attached" was higher than the evidence for it. The informed run's explicit "stakes from the band, content from the picture" is more cautious.
6. **Timing details from 1fps data.** Several early sub-second claims (the banner "held", cut positions around 2:47.8) were corrected in later rounds. This is a method issue (frames vs the mp4), not a blindness issue.
7. **Music minutiae.** Bass entry given as "about 0:04" against the level file's -44 dB at 0:03 (CHECKED: bass is -66 dB at 0:02, -44.2 at 0:03). A second off, within the tolerance both runs quote.

Nothing here is a fabricated fact about the event. The blind run's dangerous zone was interpretive vocabulary (heartbeat, eyes, ghost) rather than invention of facts.

## 3. What the informed run got wrong that the blind run got right

Context can bias reading of ambiguous pictures.

1. **The 2:47 whip-pan.** The informed interpretation says it shows "a skater with a raised fist, possibly a celebration", and carries that into its summary ("no one crosses a line... a raised fist"). CHECKED f_0168: a hooded person in a white knitted helmet and dark glasses, hand raised, in a motion-blurred crowd. Nothing marks a skater, a fist, or a finish. The blind run's "a hooded person in dark glasses raises a hand" is what the frame shows. The informed run's "raised fist" seems to have been pulled towards the "arrival at the finish" story the context supplied.
2. **"R. Paping op kop".** The informed dossier reads a headline fragment on the 0:23 newspaper as naming Paping, the winner given in the research notes, and the REPORT repeats "apparently R. Paping". CHECKED f_0025: the smudged byline fragment is not clearly a name at all (the legible words are "Tal van uitvallers en vele gewonden", "Cracks staken wedstrijd"). The blind run did not attempt it. This is classic priming: a known name found in unreadable print. The informed run did hedge it ("apparently"), but then used it as "the only name in the frames".
3. **The 3:07 banner.** Informed run did not notice or did not use the indoor setting and "parterre" line, which change what the ending is (a notice for a showing, not a title card). It had its own research (Fries Film Archief, source films unconfirmed) and could have used it to ask "what kind of reel is this?".
4. **Closing sentence as "the line that began the piece returns to frame it".** Fine in both, but the informed run adds a "reportage" framing and commission story that pushes towards "a half of a two-part release, so the missing finish is in Part 2". That is a plausible excuse that blunts the observation that nobody is seen finishing; the blind run kept the observation open.
5. **Reception framing.** The informed run states a documented reception ("drive, physical effort, image and sound fused") and a method ("archive voices carry the story") from outside sources; these can pull the feeling words (drive, relentless) towards the press line. The blind run's words (dread, held breath, awe, one patch of real warmth) came from the material.

None of these is large. They are the places where research tells you what the answer "should" be before the eyes have finished.

## 5. Human feel: which reads more like a person responding?

The blind interpretation reads more like a person. It writes the emotional journey as lived moments ("dropped in, uneasy", "a held breath", "awe with no one in it", "like weather one hears before one feels"), gives the first-timer's attention lapses their own weight, records a change of mind ("the same sentence felt like pride at 0:28 and dread at 2:44"), and picks one moment, the hinge at 2:47.8, that nearly every panellist names. It admits what it felt and what the film did not do. The informed interpretation is more organised and better caveated, and its REPORT is clear and readable, but it leans on a table of "feeling / cause" rows and on the sentence "disciplined urgency, a heavy step with a fast heartbeat", which is an apt phrase but a summary of the evidence rather than a response. Its best human moment (the tea table, the half-second hole) is also the blind run's best moment. In short: the blind run feels like a conversation among people who watched the film; the informed run feels like a briefing about it.

Caveat: both are produced by the same panel method, and neither can hear audio. "Feel" here is how convincingly each simulates a viewer.

## 6. Lessons for the toolkit

For prompts:
1. **Keep the blind lane as a standard first pass, then run the informed lane against it.** Blind runs give a bias-free read of faces, signs, text and feeling; the informed lane should be asked to find what the blind lane missed, not to rewrite it. The blind run's weak points (provenance, intent, reception) are exactly what research fills.
2. **Make unreadable print a "do not name" zone.** Add to viewer and moderator prompts: never resolve a smudged word to a name you learned from research ("R. Paping" case). Quote only what is legible at full size, and list the rest as unread.
3. **Add a "frame check before sentence" rule for contested figures.** For any claim about what a person is doing at a pivotal moment (raised fist, looking into lens, banner shape), open the full-size frame and record the literal description first, then the reading. The informed run's "raised fist" would not have survived this.
4. **Ban emotive nouns for sound until a rate or measurement is quoted** (heartbeat, bell, breath), or label them metaphor with the figure beside them.
5. **Ask for the viewer's own preference and boredom**, as the blind First-Timer did (attention drift at 1:30). These feel-first observations were the strongest human signals in the blind run.

For tools and checks:
6. **Always extract sub-second picture events from the mp4, not from `frames_1fps`.** Labels can be about 0.5 s out (f_0187 versus the mp4). Add a script that extracts frames at a named time and records the time with the mp4's own PTS.
7. **A better cut detector is overdue.** `shots.json` under-detects (snow, whip-pans, white-outs); both runs spent rounds retracting cut-rate statistics. Use a pixel-difference or histogram detector with a low threshold and a manual verify on the file's top 10 boundaries.
8. **Whisper as hint, not text.** Both runs correctly treated "MUZIEK", "Bedankt voor het kijken" and "Dalmatians" as hallucinations. Make this a rule: only digits, the language ID and phrases that repeat in both passes are quotable; place names need an on-screen sign. Consider a larger model or a Dutch-specific model for the town list.
9. **Run OCR-like "sign and headline" extraction** (a crop at full resolution of any frame with text) and store it in `data/<video>/text_on_screen.md` with legibility tags; both runs hand-read headlines and disagreed on fragments.
10. **Pre-register a hold-out claim set.** Before the informed lane, freeze a short list of "what we believe" from the blind lane so the later comparison is automatic: subject, date, place, structure times, three moments. This comparison took one read of two files because the runs already shared a section-by-section layout; keep it.

For workflow:
11. **Do not give the informed lane the blind interpretation to polish.** Keep them independent (as here); the independence is what made this comparison informative.
12. **Treat music identical-by-construction.** Since both lanes use the same stems and levels, music agreement says little; for real independence, give the blind lane a different feature view (for example a different separator or a raw-spectrogram-only task) or a different analyst model, and check whether structure survives.
13. **Context file as priors, not facts.** When context is supplied, split it into "identity" (safe) and "interpretation" (reception, intent) and tell the viewer agents to use only the first when reading pictures.
14. **Report the "unknown" list as an output.** The blind run ended with a list of what it could not determine (source films, narrator, whether the voice is archival). That list is the research agenda for the informed lane and should be auto-generated.
