## What worked

- Leading with a verdict, then walking through the piece in the order it is felt. The old reports led with a disclaimer and a file list.
- Plain similes ("a door being kicked open", "feet hitting the ground", "a machine clearing its throat", "a slight limp") in place of dB, p-values and OBS/INT tags.
- Moving the evidence into a sidecar and keeping numbers out of the prose.
- Saying what stays in memory, and being honest that nobody here heard it.

## What is still not human (all six judges agree on most of these)

1. It is a prediction about "named kinds of listener" ("cold first-timer on a phone", "who hears what"). People say what they felt; they do not sort themselves into personas.
2. No first person and no body: no goosebumps, boredom, tension, urge to move. No "this reminds me of". The refusal to compare ("I would not name a comparison") is the checker beating the listener.
3. Theory of conditioning ("taught, denied, paid", "notice what you are being taught"). A listener feels a gap and then a hit.
4. Clock times still carried everywhere ("about 4:37", "1:24"). People say "near the end", "when the voice stops".
5. Review-site frame ("Worth five minutes if...", "Best on headphones", "Not for you if...") and survey endings ("Two questions for you", "Which sentence rang falsest?").
6. Constant hedging and an apology paragraph; "we" and "you" wobble.
7. Craft vocabulary ("on the one", "no fill", "held sound", "subtraction", "the wall").
8. Committee scaffolding still visible: tables, confidence grades, withdrawn lists, "What nobody here could hear".
9. The 127-finishers fact is used as a decoding key, not as something that lands emotionally; the Dutch voice itself is barely described.

## Accuracy faults in B (fix first, A had already caught them)

- video2: "the picture cuts from an empty road to a crowd on the same instant" as the drop (and the same in the picture section and sidecar). The static shot runs 1:22.1 to 1:30.3; the cut is about 1.7 s after the drop.
- video2: "two drum hits and three bass notes" stated as fact; the stem shows one tone with a dip.
- video2: "no fill, no rise, nothing to announce it" contradicts its own point that the drums thin beforehand; sidecar lists this as withdrawn but the page keeps it.
- video1: "nobody smiles" and "sounds like longing" are stronger than the evidence (faces too small; pitch track on a polyphonic stem).
- Small slips: "a couple of seconds" ring-out (about 1.5 s), "about ten seconds later" (12 s), "twenty-five seconds" (about 24 s), "wake" for the race.

## Next changes

1. Rewrite as one voice. Drop the persona section, "who hears what", the conditioning frame, the survey endings and the review-blurb opener. Put a one-line, plain disclosure up front ("I could not hear this; this is built from analysis") and then stop apologising.
2. Reaction first, evidence second: have the writer state a felt response (tension, boredom, a lump in the throat) and name only one or two moments, not a timeline. Allow comparisons ("like a march with a stuck engine", film-score or krautrock references) marked as comparisons, not claims.
3. Replace clock times with relative landmarks (when the voice stops, the last third) and strip craft jargon; a banned-words lint for "on the one", "no fill", "held sound", "subtraction", "taught".
4. Describe the Dutch voice as a sound (old, dry, flat, reading out figures) and let the 127-finishers fact land as feeling.
5. Make the accuracy pass mechanical: run A's corrected shot list and sync facts against B before publishing, and delete withdrawn claims from the page rather than only the sidecar.
6. Keep the sidecar out of the human file (separate path) and publish B alongside A as a reference, not instead of it. Re-run the three blind judges after the rewrite and add a fourth question: "does this sound like one person who listened?"
7. Honest limit: a text-only pipeline can predict, not hear. Say so once; the aim is a piece that a person could plausibly have written after listening, with no claim beyond what the evidence supports.
