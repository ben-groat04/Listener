# Round 1: the critic

*A music journalist's reading of our own reports. British English, no apologies.*

## Why these reports are not how anyone listens

I have read `video2/REPORT.md`, `video1/REPORT.md` and the diptych comparison. They are careful, honest and, as writing about music, almost useless. Not because the facts are wrong. Because a report like this is a lab sheet, and music is not a lab sheet. Seven faults.

**1. It is a timeline, not a point of view.** Every paragraph is a timestamp and a state change: "The stride begins (0:28)", "The hole and the drop (1:24 to 1:28.6)", "The unmaking (4:36.8 to 5:04)". Nobody remembers a record like that. After a track you remember three things, perhaps four, and you have an opinion about it. A review that tells you what happened at every wall-clock second has no opinion, because it has not decided what matters. Our "moments that stay with you" list gives six entries, each of them again a timestamp. That list is the report in miniature.

**2. The unit of attention is wrong.** The reports treat a feeling as something to be derived from a measurement: "the overall level steps up about 7 dB", "the drums about 50 dB louder", "One layer brightens by roughly 22 dB in its top frequencies (about 3 dB in the full mix)". A listener does not hear 22 dB in a top end. A listener hears that it got *brighter, tighter, more urgent*. The decibels are the evidence we needed because we have no ears; they are not the experience, and they should not be what we write down.

**3. Hedging in place of commitment.** "It feels like a held breath, or a cold morning heard through glass (INT)." The best line in either report, and then we stamp it INT as if it were a guess. Further on: "The best-supported word is withdrawal; 'mourning' is weaker." "Whether you feel grief, pride or a quiet leaving depends on what you bring." That is a committee declining to say. A critic says "this is a funeral march for a way of winter" or "no, it is a pub singalong in a cold coat", and defends it. Being wrong in an interesting way beats being unfalsifiable.

**4. It describes the mechanism and calls it the sound.** "The bass plays a figure grouped in threes against the kick, so it lands with the kick only on beat 3 and never on beat 1. That gives a steady foot-tap with a slight limp." This is a real finding, and the last clause is lovely, but it is buried inside theory. And "It also fools an automatic beat tracker, which reports 161 BPM instead of 120" is a fact about our machine, not about the music. A reader does not care what fooled the tracker.

**5. Facts about the tool crowd out facts about the track.** "How this music was heard" opens with a disclaimer; the cold-read revision section is a page of our own bug fixes; the Part 1 header is a paragraph of file names ("Checked against: v1/data/video1/music_views/per_second_levels.json..."). The reader asked about a song and got our audit trail. The cold-read note "'tonal centre hauntological' line removed" is a sentence in which the one phrase with any imagination was deleted for being unmeasurable.

**6. Comparison is missing where it matters most.** Not one line says what this sounds like. Is it Mogwai? Steve Reich on a skating rink? Kraftwerk's "Trans-Europe Express" with the radio on? Is the Part 2 kick-drum drop a rave moment? Every listener files music against other music within ten seconds, because that is how we know whether to care. We offer a table whose rows are "Music: kind", "Music: loudness", "Sync". "Loop-built electronic piece" is a catalogue category, not a description. There is no band either: Public Service Broadcasting's whole conceit (found archive voice, modern instruments, the bravery of the past and a slight irony in the polish) is the thing, and the reports keep it in a context.md footnote.

**7. It is not written for a person.** "The drama is made by layers, loudness and the human voice, not by harmony or tempo." "Cut rate and image motion rise and fall loosely with loudness (correlations about 0.2 to 0.3...)." "p about 0.09." A *p*-value in a piece about a skating race is a category error. The comparison table is the clearest case: a 20 KB document, forty-odd bracketed confidence tags (H, M-L, OBS, INT, SPEC), and nowhere does it say whether it is any good.

### Lines that ring false, and what a listener would say

- *"The sudden brightening at that instant is the narrator's own consonants, not a new instrument."* True, and a nice catch, but it is a correction aimed at our own pipeline. The human: "A man's voice walks into a room that has been all weather and no people. That is the first moment you feel somebody is there."
- *"It is the biggest single event in the piece."* Which is a ranking from a meter. Human: "At 1:28 the floor comes up under you and everyone in the room nods." (The report does get this, in "Its force comes from the gap before it and the lack of any warning". That is the real insight and it is one clause long.)
- *"It sounds like a sudden clearing in which the music must carry the scale of the crowd alone (INT)."* Good. Then spoiled by the tag. Delete "(INT)" and the sentence stands as writing.
- *"Mostly D by the chord detector, with a gritty, circling low figure (chromatic friction around D) and a cymbal-led kit... Mostly the riff and the payoff."* Human: "It is a riff that grinds like boots on hard snow and then the cymbals open like the sky lifting."
- *"A calm Dutch voice gives the date... mentions a distance of about two hundred (the noun is unclear)."* Nobody notices the noun is unclear. They notice a calm old voice reading out terrible figures in the tone of a station announcer, over a band that will not let him be calm. That *tension* is the piece and neither report states it as the headline.
- *"The track ends by taking itself apart, in this order"* followed by a bullet list. Human: "It ends like a coat coming off in a warm room. The big machine stops; two knocks; then you hear the building."
- *"A fair short description is an unspoken afterword..."* It takes one hundred and four words to say what a person says in five: "a march that slowly stops."

### What is missing

Surprise, memory, humour, body, taste, and the *other music in your head*. And, critically: the order of knowing. We never separate "what I felt the first time" from "what I understand now". We flatten both into one analytical present tense.

## How a human takes this music in

I will describe it as I would to a friend on the way out of the room, then slow it down.

**The first seconds.** Before any analysis you form a verdict in about five seconds: genre, mood, "is this for me". With Part 2 that verdict is a long, cold, nearly silent pad: *oh, this is going to be patient.* With Part 1 it is a hit and a grinding riff: *oh, this is going to be loud.* You also read the visuals instantly. Colour or black and white. Old film or new. That sets the century. You are not counting, you are placing yourself.

**The voice is a hook.** A foreign-language old man reading the numbers of a long-ago event is the most memorable thing in either piece, and it hooks you before the beat does. Most listeners will not follow the Dutch, and that matters: it becomes *a sound of authority and period*, grain and cadence and the faint crackle, not information. You feel history first and parse it second, if ever. Our reports spend their effort on which words are "trusted" ("16 degrees below, over 200 km, and 575 and about 9,500"). Real viewers hear a mood and catch two numbers and a year.

**Body before mind.** By thirty seconds you are in your feet and your breathing. Part 2's 120 BPM is a walking pace and that is why it feels like walking; nobody needs a beat tracker to tell them. Then you wait. The attention in this music is about *waiting for the thing you can feel coming*: the held-back kick, the return of the bass at 1:40 in Part 1. You do not experience those moments as measured dB but as relief, or a lift in your chest. Anticipation, not information, is the engine.

**Memory and comparison.** Listeners place the track at once: post-rock, Reich, a Kraftwerk pulse, a documentary soundtrack, a band that does this with archive films (the person who knows PSB's other records hears this as the same trick again, and judges it against *The War Room* and *Inform-Educate-Entertain*). The ones who have never heard the band hear something like a Radio 4 documentary that has joined a band. Either way it is placed on a map of what they already know.

**Repetition is not boredom.** A 47-second trance at the plateau is, in our report, "for about 47 seconds nothing new happens". To a listener this is the point: the loop is the endurance, the skater's rhythm. You are not waiting for a change; you are being carried, and noticing small shifts is the pleasure. People will say "I zoned out and then realised it had got louder", which is exactly our finding in the most accurate and least technical form.

**The picture and music marry loosely, and that is fine.** Our sync section proves the cuts are not on the beat (p about 0.09). A viewer never asks. What a viewer feels is the *difference in age*: the footage is 1963, the sound is 2013, the colour is a bit faded and the kick drum is crisp. The collision is the work. Whether a cut lands within 80 ms of a beat is irrelevant when the thing you notice is that the picture refuses to react at the drop. Our own report found this ("the picture ignores the biggest moment in the music"), and it deserved to be the headline, not point three.

**The face.** People look at faces. In Part 2, 4:10 to 4:12, a young man glances at the lens. That is where a human watcher leans in: someone *looked back*. A fifty-year-old film makes a living person out of a dead one for half a second. It is on our list at number five and given one flat sentence.

**The ending, and afterwards.** What you leave with is a handful of images and one feeling. Probably: *the bass coming back*, *the old man's voice*, *the empty road with the tiny walkers*, *the two knocks and then silence*. The rest dissolves, as it should. Afterwards you tell someone "it is that thing about the skating race, you need to see the one where the drums stop and then come back". A day later you remember the feeling of being cold and watched by the past. If you know the history (127 finishers, the 2012 cancellation, the last true freeze) the whole thing re-reads as an elegy for something that now barely exists; if you do not, it is a bleak, beautiful march. Both readings are legitimate, and they arrive in *different order* for different people: feeling first, facts second, or facts first, feeling re-coloured.

**Taste is not optional.** A human listener has a verdict: *this is good, this is a bit cold, this one is better than Part 1.* We refused to rank anything. A reader wants to know whether to bother.

## Three questions for the panel

1. **To the psychologist:** When a listener first meets this track, what do they actually process in the first ten seconds, and in what order: genre, emotion, body, words? Which of our measured features (the 0:28 step, the missing drums, the held-back kick) correspond to something the research says people reliably *feel*, and which are our own artefacts?

2. **To the fan (someone who knows Public Service Broadcasting and the rest of the catalogue):** What do you hear on first play that our reports cannot hear: the PSB trick, the old voice against new machines, the jokes and the lump in the throat? Is this one of their better tracks, and does Part 2's drop work for you?

3. **To the devil (advocate), and for the musician in the room:** Is it fair to say a machine can never write about music? If not, what is the *minimum* we can do: what one-paragraph rewrite of the 1:28 drop, using only what we can measure, would sound like a person to you? And, musician, how would you play it to someone to make them hear the gap before the drop?
