# Round 1: the devil's advocate

British English. I read v1/analysis/video2/REPORT.md, v1/analysis/video1/REPORT.md and the first part of the diptych comparison. My job is to argue for what the current approach protects, so I will say first that the user is right, and then say what we must not throw away.

## 1. Why the reports are not how a person listens

The user's complaint is fair, and it is not about tone alone. The reports are built the wrong way round. A person starts with a feeling and, if asked, goes looking for the reason. We start with a measurement and, if allowed, attach a feeling to it.

**Examples that ring false.**

1. *"No one has listened to this audio with ears. Everything about the sound comes from measurement... the feelings are a reconstruction."* (video2, "How this music was heard"). Honest, yes. But it sits at the top of a piece about a song, so the reader is told before bar one that nobody was there. A listener's first report is "it starts very quiet and cold". The disclaimer belongs once, at the end, as a note on method.

2. *"Then everything returns on one downbeat at 1:28.6, with the drums about 50 dB louder, a four-on-the-floor kick and no fill."* No one hears "50 dB". They hear the floor come back. The true human sentence is: "it's been holding its breath for four seconds and then the kick comes in and you nearly stand up." The number is the evidence, not the experience.

3. *"The bass plays a figure grouped in threes against the kick, so it lands with the kick only on beat 3 and never on beat 1... It also fools an automatic beat tracker, which reports 161 BPM instead of 120."* The tracker's failure is irrelevant to a listener. What a person gets is that it walks and it rocks. We spent a sentence on what the machine got wrong, not on what the music does.

4. *"One layer brightens by roughly 22 dB in its top frequencies (about 3 dB in the full mix)... how it was made cannot be recovered."* A listener says: "it keeps getting more urgent and I couldn't tell you when it started." The report does arrive at that, but only after the decibels.

5. *"36% of the 86 cuts fall within 80 ms of a beat, against 32% expected by chance."* A viewer says: "the pictures don't hit the beats, they just drift along, and that's why it feels like a documentary and not a video." Same finding, and the human version tells you what it does to you.

6. Video1: *"Loudness barely changes (about 0.2 to 0.5 LU on a later ebur128 measurement...), but the bass itself leaps from almost nothing to full weight."* The section is saved by "You feel weight and relief, not volume. It works because of the 25 seconds you waited." That last sentence is exactly right, and it is the only sentence of the paragraph a person would say. It is buried behind the LU figures.

7. Video1: *"The composer here is, in effect, the mixer."* A clever line a critic might write after listening. Here it is a conclusion drawn from stems, and it flattens the band into a fader.

8. The header lines. *"Checked against: v1/data/video1/music_views/per_second_levels.json, ... frames_1fps/f_0024.jpg..."* is a lab notebook. It is not a response to a song.

9. The comparison: *"Nobody on the team has heard the audio. Every 'feels' below is a reasoned expectation, not a report of hearing."* and the OBS / INT / SPEC and H/M/L tags on nearly every line. This is the audit voice. A reader who wanted to know what it is like to sit through these two pieces gets a table of confidence levels.

**What is structurally wrong, in plain terms.**

- **The order.** Facts first, feeling as an afterthought tagged "(INT)". Humans do it the other way.
- **Every feeling is on trial.** Each one has to cite a number before it may speak. Real listening is mostly not like that: you feel the drop, then, if you are a nerd, you check the waveform.
- **The wrong unit.** We describe events (a layer, a hole, a stab) and levels. People describe a shape over time: waiting, giving way, something arriving, something being worn out.
- **No body, no memory, no before.** Nobody says what the first ten seconds promise, what they expect next, or how the middle feels once you have stopped noticing it. Expectation and its breaking is most of how music works, and there is little of it here beyond the "hole and drop".
- **No "what's it about".** The viewer of a film of a frozen race in 1963 is moved, or bored, by *people*: the small figures, the muffled voice from another age. Our reports have that in the picture section, but the music section never lets the voice be a voice from the past. It is "a dry, band-limited Dutch archive voice".
- **Hedging as a style.** Every other sentence says what cannot be decided. The result is a person who will not commit, which is the opposite of listening.
- **A committee voice.** "A panel" settled it. A human reaction is one person's, and can be wrong, and that is allowed.

## 2. How a human actually takes this in, from first second to afterwards

I am building this from what the music is (our own measurements are fine for that) and from how listeners generally behave. Where I describe an experience I mean *typical*, not "I heard it".

**Before you press play.** You know the band, or you don't. A fan comes with a picture of PSB already: archive voices over drum-and-bass-driven electronics, a history lesson with a beat. Someone new has the thumbnail, the title (a word they cannot pronounce), and a length. Part 1 is 3 minutes, Part 2 is five. You have already guessed "short, then long".

**The first seconds.** Within about two seconds you have decided: quiet or loud, warm or cold, human or machine. Part 2 opens quiet, dark and cold, and nothing is moving. Part 1 opens with a snap and is moving at once. That is more or less the whole of the first impression, and it does not need a spectrogram. Lines that matter here: Part 2 tells you to settle in and wait. Part 1 says "keep up".

**The first minute.** You are working out what kind of thing this is. A voice arrives in Dutch. For most of us (this includes the viewer in Britain) it is not words but a sound: old, flat, a bit crackly, sure of itself. The listener feels "this is from the past, and someone is telling me something that matters", and the subtitles, if any, add the date and the cold. In Part 2 this is the first human sound after 42 seconds of pad, and that is a feeling: *someone has come into the room*.

**Middle: the habit and the break.** Once the beat settles you stop hearing it. You nod. Then the music does something to the habit. Part 1's bass goes missing for 25 seconds and everything feels light and unsafe; when it returns it feels like coming home. Part 2's four-second hole before 1:28 and the huge return. A person would say these are "the bits you wait for". Nobody counts the seconds. They remember *that it went away and came back*.

**The long stretch.** In Part 2 the loop goes on and on. A human does not take it as "47 seconds of nothing new". They drift, lose time, and half a minute later notice it has got more intense. The fragments of voice, the brightness, the picture of backs and bibs: this is what is called trance or grind, and it is a feeling of effort, of a crowd doing something hard together. It is not a story about a layer opening a filter.

**The end.** This is where listeners disagree, and that is a finding too. Part 1 stops while still going. Some people feel cheated: "where is the finish?" Others feel the point. Part 2 takes itself apart and stops, then two hits, then nothing. Many people will find it strangely moving or frustrating, not "withdrawal not arrival". The picture's ducks and floes make it gentle. The two ends are the same sort of unresolved, and one person's "haunting" is another's "that's it?".

**Afterwards.** What stays is not a map of the track. It is: a cold morning, a voice from the past, the bit where the bass came back, the ending that doesn't finish. People hum the bass line or the rhythm (not the "pitch set B C# D E F# G A"). They might tell a friend "it's about a skating race but it feels like a march". They might look up the race. They might replay the drop. They will confuse which part had which feature. All of that is normal, and it is allowed.

**Things a human does that we cannot, and should say so briefly.** Body response (the foot tapping, chill at the return of the bass), the sound of the voice (age, warmth, the Frisian accent), and the surprise of the first time. Those we can only estimate. The rest, such as the shape of expectation over time, we can model very well from the stems.

## 3. What would be lost by going "human"

I am the devil, so here is the case for the current approach, stated fairly.

1. **Accuracy.** A free human voice invites invention: "the bass sounds like a heartbeat", "the choir swells". Our own ERRORS log exists because agents did this. Unconstrained feeling is where hallucination lives. We cannot hear, so "it sounds warm" is a guess made to look like testimony.
2. **Honesty about what an AI can perceive.** If the report reads like "I felt the drop", a reader may think Claude has ears. That would be a false claim about the evidence. The audit voice, ugly as it is, stops that.
3. **Checkability.** Someone can open per_second_levels.json and test "the drums are 50 dB louder". They cannot test "it nearly made you stand up".
4. **Reproducibility across videos.** The toolkit is meant to be video-agnostic. Measurements generalise. Style does not.
5. **The corrections already made.** Several earlier errors (tempo octave, "calm" for the second half, the beat tracker's 161) were caught only because every claim had a number attached. Drop the number and the corrections go.

## 4. How to keep those without the audit voice

- **Two layers, one document.** The first page is written as a listener would speak: shape over time, one feeling per passage, plain words, committed. After it comes a short "what this rests on" section for each claim, written once, with timestamps. The reader chooses how deep to go.
- **Say the feeling as an expectation, not a testimony.** "Most listeners would feel the floor drop away here" is honest and human. "I felt..." is not honest and "(INT)" is not human. A grammar of "you'd hear / most people would" keeps both.
- **Numbers move to footnotes, not out of the report.** "The drums come back about 50 dB louder" can sit beside a link. The sentence in the body is "the kick comes back".
- **Hedge once, not every line.** One honest paragraph at the end names what we could not perceive: timbre and warmth, the voice's sound, the body response. Then we write with confidence elsewhere.
- **Keep the failure log.** The mistakes list stays. The reports can say less about method.
- **Test the feeling against a person.** The only real check on "this is how it feels" is people. A cold read by a human who has heard the track, or a listener panel (from different backgrounds), is a better test of "is this human" than another fact-checker. The panel role should be: "does this ring true?", not "is this sourced?".
- **Write about the arc, not the events.** Expectation, deferral, release, and what is left. These can be derived from the measurements without being quoted from them.

## 5. Where I will still push back

- The user's complaint could be solved by removing the audit voice and putting nothing in its place. That would be worse. A smooth, confident, ungrounded description of music nobody could hear is the exact failure the project exists to prevent.
- "Human" is not one thing. A fan hears the reference to the band's other work. A musician hears the bass pattern. A casual viewer hears a mood. The report cannot be all of them at once. Pick one reader, state it, and write for them.
- Beware of making the language beautiful instead of right. Fine prose can hide a wrong claim better than a table can.

## 6. Three questions for the panel

1. **To the psychologist:** What are the few things that most reliably shape how people describe music in the first thirty seconds and afterwards (expectation, surprise, arousal, memory), and which of those can we infer from structure alone without pretending to have heard it? Which feelings are shared across listeners, and which vary so much that a single "human reading" is misleading?
2. **To the fan:** When you first played these two tracks, what did you actually say about them, to yourself or a friend? Which bit would you point at, and what would you never say aloud, like "the stem brightens by 22 dB"? What do our reports leave out that a fan would add (the band, the history-lesson trick, the voice)?
3. **To the critic, musician, viewer and (for me) the devil, one each:** (critic) Does this report say anything a review would, or only describe? (musician) Which of our technical observations would you actually say to another player, and which are noise? (viewer) Is there a single sentence in these reports that sounds like you? And the devil's own: if we strip the evidence from the front, what would stop the next agent inventing a choir?
