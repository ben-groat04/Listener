# Human listening, round 1: the fan

*British English. I read video2/REPORT.md and video1/REPORT.md properly, and skimmed the comparison. I have not heard the tracks either, so I am speaking as a fan about how listening works, not about what these particular songs sound like.*

## Why these reports are not how anyone listens

**1. They are a lab notebook, and nobody tells a mate about a song that way.** Look at the hedging: "The tonal centre (B or E) is a hypothesis." "How the late build is made, and what the late voice fragments are, are open." A fan hears the thing and says "it's cold, it plods, and then it goes". We don't doubt that there's a key; we just don't care what it's called.

**2. Numbers stand in for feelings.** "Loudness barely changes (about 0.2 to 0.5 LU...) but the bass itself leaps from almost nothing to full weight." A real listener says: *when the bass comes back at 1:40 it's like the floor returning.* The report actually says that too, in the middle of the decibels, but buries it. Likewise "the drums about 50 dB louder" and "a 6 to 8 dB dip at about 5:02.60". Nobody hears 6 dB. People hear "it stops, then two thumps".

**3. It forgets that listening happens in time, with memory and with a body.** The report treats the track as a set of events to audit. A human is carried along: you tap your foot by 30 seconds, you stop noticing the pulse, and you only notice when it vanishes. The 120 BPM and the "kick on beat 3, never beat 1" detail is the sort of thing a drummer might notice on the fifth play, if ever. The first-listen experience is "it's steady and it sort of marches".

**4. It spends words on things no listener perceives.** "Fools an automatic beat tracker, which reports 161 BPM instead of 120." "36% of the 86 cuts fall within 80 ms of a beat against 32% expected by chance." A viewer does not run a Rayleigh test. What a viewer notices is simply whether the picture and music feel married, and the report's honest finding (they mostly are not cut to the beat) is lost under the statistics. Also: a human forgives loose sync completely. Cut-to-beat precision is an editor's craft, not a listener's experience.

**5. It declines the obvious response.** Quote: "Elegy or celebration is genuinely open... Whether you feel grief, pride or a quiet leaving depends on what you bring." That is the committee talking. A fan would say "it's a bit sad but proud, like a war film for ice skaters". Not choosing is the least human move available. People are opinionated and are happy to be wrong.

**6. No comparison, no taste.** Fans place music instantly: this is PSB doing what they did on "The War Room" and "Every Valley", archive voice over motorik krautrock, a bit of Explosions in the Sky, a bit of Kraftwerk's "Tour de France" for the ride and the machine. Neither report reaches for a single other song. A human reads new music through old music; that is the main way meaning gets made.

**7. The cold open: "No one has listened to this audio with ears."** Honest, and correct to say once. But the whole of Part 1 and Part 2 is then built as a ruling on a measured record, with 3,000-word reports, "OBS/INT" tags and cold-read fixes. The effort goes on being right about the ending (two drum hits or three bass notes?) instead of whether that ending lands. The second cold-read revision spends a bullet on "the pad rings alone for about 1.5 s (not two)". That is not what stays with someone after the song.

**8. "The moments that stay with you" are not the moments that stay with you.** Item 4 in Part 2: "5:02.33 and 5:02.58 over a bass tone to about 5:02.86". A person remembers: *the bit where it all drops out, then it comes back mental; and the way it just stops at the end.* The report has those (1:28 and the end) but wrapped in timecodes to the hundredth of a second.

## How a person actually takes this in

**First ten seconds.** Gut verdict: genre, mood, era. Something like "oh, atmospheric, cold, slow-building, a bit film score". I do not know what the pad is. I know it is not going to be a pop song.

**First minute.** I decide whether to stay. A human voice arriving in a foreign language at 0:42 is the hook: I cannot understand "18 januari 1963", but I can hear a date, and the crackly archive sound tells me it is old and real. I will not catch the words (most listeners can't speak Dutch), so the voice works as a *texture and a feeling of old*, not as information. The reports lean heavily on the transcripts, which a fan would not have.

**Minutes two to four.** I stop analysing. I am either hooked or drifting. The steady pulse becomes the floor I stand on. I notice change only as relief or tension: "it's all getting a bit more intense", "something's missing". I definitely do not notice the layer "brightening by 22 dB at the top". I might think "is it louder?" and be right.

**The single big moment.** The drop in Part 2 at 1:28 (and Part 1's bass at 1:40). It is the thing everyone will mention. Hairs-up moment: the gap, then it all slams in. That is the song, as far as I'm concerned. And the picture staying on a quiet snowy road through it is, for a viewer, *interesting*, not a failure: it feels like the film being calm while the music goes off.

**What I would skip.** The long plateau in Part 2 from about 2:48 to 4:36. Nearly two minutes of the same loop. For some people it is the trance and they love it; for others it is a long drive. A human admits this. The report calls it "hypnotic" and "straining", which is the polite version.

**The picture.** It is old colour film of ordinary Dutch people on ice. I find it charming, a bit sad because they are all dead or old now, and I am drawn to the odd face that looks at the camera (the report notices this at 4:10, good). I watch faces, not cut rates. When the film is a bit grainy and the music is modern and crisp, that contrast is the whole point of the band.

**Afterwards.** What I remember: it started cold and empty, a voice told me a date, it marched, there was a huge moment, it faded into ducks and water and then went black. I would say "it's like a funeral and a party at once, on ice". I would not remember it is in B minor. I would probably look up the race, which is where the real feeling hits: 127 finishers out of nearly 10,000. That fact does more to the music than any chord does.

**Reminds me of.** PSB's own "Every Valley" and "The Other Side", Kraftwerk, Boards of Canada for the faded-film warmth, Mogwai for the long climb and sudden quiet, Godspeed You! Black Emperor for the slow gathering.

## What a better report would sound like

It would open with a verdict ("A cold, dignified march that builds for five minutes and then leaves you alone with some ducks"), name the single moment, say what to skip, say what it reminds you of, and then put the measurements in a footnote. The facts are not the problem. The ordering and voice are. We have written a defence of evidence when the job was to describe an experience.

## Three questions for the panel

1. **To the psychologist:** What do people actually retain from one listen of a five-minute instrumental piece, and how much of it is the *shape* (quiet, then big, then stop) against specific events? Could we build the report around that memory shape and relegate everything else?
2. **To the musician:** Which of our measured details (the 3-against-4 bass, the missing third, the 120 BPM grid) would a trained ear actually hear and be moved by, and which are noise to everyone but us? Please be ruthless.
3. **To the devil:** If we cannot hear, is it dishonest to write "it feels like" at all? Where is the line between reconstructing a listener's reaction from evidence and just guessing what the typical fan would say, and which one should the toolkit aim for?
