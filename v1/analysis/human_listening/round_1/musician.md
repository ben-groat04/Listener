# Round 1: the musician

British English. I have read v1/analysis/video2/REPORT.md, v1/analysis/video1/REPORT.md and the diptych comparison. I have not heard the audio either, so what follows is what a player would say hearing these two records cold, using what the reports measured as a map, not as the experience.

## 1. Why these reports are not how anyone listens

**They describe a spreadsheet and call it a song.** A player hears a track in about five seconds: what's the groove, what's the riff, where does it go. Video2 opens with "A single dark, hushed pad, with isolated low thuds and long tails at 0:00, 0:08, 0:12, 0:20 and 0:24". Nobody counts thuds. A musician says: "it's a pad and a few low hits, ambient intro, nothing happening on purpose." The timestamps are accurate and useless; the listener's experience is "it's ages before it starts, and then it starts".

**They never say what the music is.** Video2 says it is "a loop-built electronic piece" with "no sung vocal, no tune and no cadence". A band musician would say it is a *build track*: a four-on-the-floor kick under a locked bass loop and a lead layer, doing what a dance record does. They never use the word that matters to a listener, which is *groove*. "The kick on every beat. The bass plays a figure grouped in threes against the kick, so it lands with the kick only on beat 3" is a good observation, and it is the best sentence in the report. But it ends with "fools an automatic beat tracker, which reports 161 BPM". The player's version: "it's a three-against-four bass line, so it rolls and never sits down. That's why it feels like walking rather than marching." The tracker being fooled is a fact about the tool, not the music.

**The feelings are decorated measurements.** "The sudden brightening at that instant is the narrator's own consonants, not a new instrument." True and fine, but a listener doesn't hear "brightening"; they hear a man start talking. "One layer brightens by roughly 22 dB in its top frequencies (about 3 dB in the full mix)" is a filter sweep. Every producer knows it: you open the filter on the synth over a minute and the track tightens like a spring. Say that. "Brightness, loudness and fragment length rise together" is a build. Call it a build.

**They hedge where a musician would commit.** "How it was made cannot be recovered" about the late layer; "no banjo or guitar is claimed". A player wouldn't worry. A distorted wall of sound in video1 is a wall of guitars, or guitars and synth stacked, and in a band like this the answer is: probably both, played through pedals. Nobody gets marks for refusing to say "guitar". The reports treat a naming uncertainty as if it were uncertainty about the *experience*, which it isn't.

**They list; players tell a story of the room.** "The drama is subtraction. Three things are switched on and off: the bass, a dense, distorted 'wall' layer... The composer here is, in effect, the mixer." That is a clever line and it is half right, but it is written as an analyst's discovery. A musician says "it's all arrangement: layers in, layers out, nobody changes chord", which is the normal way this band writes, and everyone in the room knows it.

**They make the voice a data point.** "A free-timed human voice sits over a rigid modern grid and lines up with it only where its sentences end." That is a nice idea. But the human fact is simpler and the report buries it: the narrator is the *vocalist*. In a PSB record the sampled voice is the lead, the tune and the hook. You listen to the voice the way you listen to a singer who can't carry a note and doesn't need to. Part of why it lands is that the Dutch is unintelligible to most English listeners, so the voice becomes pure texture and tone, a calm old radio man, with only a few words ("1963", "Elfstedentocht", numbers) snagging.

**They treat a drop as a statistic.** Video2's 1:28.6 is called "the biggest single event in the piece" and explained as "drums about 50 dB louder, a four-on-the-floor kick and no fill. Its force comes from the gap before it". The facts are right and a drummer would say it in a breath: "four bars of nothing, then the kick comes in on the one with no fill. That's the drop." Fifty dB is a measurement artefact from a near-silent gap; no one hears fifty dB, they hear "oh, there it is". The sentence "Its force comes from the gap" is the right claim and the number is noise.

**They overlook what the ear does with repetition.** "For about 47 seconds nothing new happens and the loop is hypnotic" is accurate. But a human doesn't register 47 seconds as "nothing". They go under; they stop counting; the track becomes a place. The report notes the hypnosis and then spends paragraphs on what it measured within it.

**They separate music and picture by test, and then conclude the feel from the tests.** "36% of the 86 cuts fall within 80 ms of a beat, against 32% expected by chance". A viewer doesn't test; they feel that the film "goes with" the music or not. For a musician, editing to the beat is a choice made on a feel for the *phrase*, not the 80 ms window. The relevant human point is that a four-on-the-floor kick at 120 BPM is a 2-second bar, and cuts on that grain would feel mechanical. The reports' finding (the picture ignores the drop) is the interesting part and it is told as if it were a null result. A player would say: "the picture refuses to cheer for the drop. That's a decision."

## 2. How a human takes this in, from the first seconds

I'll take Part 2 mainly, Part 1 where it differs.

**The first few seconds.** You decide what kind of record this is before anything has happened. Video2 opens with a quiet pad and a few low hits and no pulse: "this is going to be slow, atmospheric, a long way in." Video1 hits you at 0:00.7 with a swell, a crash and drums already going: "right, rock band, loud, here we go." That difference matters more than any later fact. You set your patience by it.

**The first minute.** What a player hears is *waiting*. In Part 2 nothing rhythmic for 28 seconds; then the bass and sparse drums start (0:28) and you feel the walking begin: not a beat yet, a gait. Then a voice, dry and radio-small, says a date. You don't understand the Dutch. You understand it's old, it's official, it's a story told by someone who was there or is reading what was written. The pictures say 1963: colour film, winter, snow. You are already in a mood: documentary, cold, a little solemn. Nobody has told you the plot and you don't need it.

**The first drop.** At about 1:28 the whole thing locks. This is what every listener of this kind of record is waiting for and it comes after four seconds of nothing. You feel it in the body: shoulders drop, you nod. And then, crucially, the record settles into a groove, and the groove has a limp in it, the bass sitting against the kick. You settle into the pulse like walking in step with someone.

**The long middle.** Here the human experience is not "47 seconds of loop" but *being carried*. You stop analysing. You watch the skaters' backs, the bibs, and the music is simply the rhythm they move to. Small changes (a new bass note every 12 seconds; the top end opening on a synth) work below attention; you notice, if at all, that it's getting bigger. By 4:00 you are leaning forward without knowing why. That is the build doing its job, and it is the part a measurement-led report is worst at, because the whole point is that it is not an event.

**The ending.** You feel the thing come apart: the layers drop, the bass chops into stabs, the groove stops, two drum hits and silence. A listener says "it just stops", then "it feels unfinished, like they've gone on without us". The report's phrase "withdrawal and a stop, not arrival" is, in fact, right and is the best bit of human reading in it. It needs to be said in those few words, not wrapped in 25-second times.

**Afterwards.** What you remember is not the 575 racers or the 120 BPM. You remember: the quiet start, the waiting, the *slam* when it all comes in, the long trance, and the voice. You remember the one thing you would hum: the bass line, rolling. And you carry a mood: cold, determined, a bit sad, a bit proud. If you are a drummer you remember the kick; if you are a bassist you remember the three-against-four. And you might go and look up what the race was.

**Part 1 in the same terms.** Loud, grinding, a riff on one note (D) that never lets up; then at 1:15 the bass drops out and you float; at 1:40 it comes back and it is the payoff of the whole record. That is a classic rock arrangement move: strip the bottom, let it hang, slam it back. A band would call it "pulling the floor". The report gets it right ("lands harder than its loudness suggests precisely because it was withheld"), and that is human. Then it spends the next lines on LU differences between measurement methods.

## 3. What the reports got right but said wrongly

- **The 1:28.6 drop** in video2 and **the 1:40 bass return** in video1 are the two true centres. Say: "a held-back kick that arrives with no fill", "the bass is pulled and slammed back".
- **The 3-against-4 bass figure.** Right and valuable; say "it rolls and never sits down", not "fools the tracker".
- **The subtractive ending.** Right; say "the track takes its clothes off one layer at a time and then stops".
- **"The composer is in effect the mixer".** True of a build record; say "it's all arrangement".
- **The sync finding that the picture ignores the drop.** Right and interesting; say it as an artistic choice, not a p-value.
- **"The voice carries the feeling"**; in fact the voice is both the hook and the texture, and half the audience doesn't understand it.

## 4. What we leave out that a musician would add

1. **Is it good music to play along with?** Nothing about playing it: the riff, the feel, the hook. A person hears something they could tap or hum.
2. **The production trick.** PSB are a sample band: a vintage voice cut over a modern machine. The tension between the old man's voice and the new grid is the whole idea, and the reports mention it only as a sync statistic.
3. **Tension and release as a bodily thing.** Nothing says "your body leans".
4. **Genre.** Part 1 is post-rock/rock; Part 2 is electronic/dance-adjacent. Listeners hear the genre first and everything after is coloured by it.
5. **Loudness as mastering, not drama.** A flat, loud Part 1 is partly a mastering choice; a listener hears "loud and tight" not "drama is subtraction".
6. **Nothing about attention:** when you stop listening, when you drift, when something pulls you back.

## 5. Three questions for the panel

1. **To the psychologist:** What does a listener actually register of a 47-second unchanging loop (Part 2, 2:48 to 4:36)? Do they feel "nothing happens", or do they feel a rising tension they cannot date? Which of our "feelings" are memory of the *whole* shape and which are felt in the moment?
2. **To the fan:** When you play this record, what is the one thing you wait for, and what do you tell a friend about it in a sentence? Is it the drop, the voice, the bass, the ending? And does knowing the 1963 race change what you hear, or do you just hear a cool record?
3. **To the devil's advocate:** If an agent can never hear, is "how a human feels it" the wrong target for this toolkit? Would it be more honest to describe the music in the working vocabulary players use (groove, build, drop, filter sweep, pulled floor), flagged as *inferred from the stems*, and stop dressing each claim in decibels?
