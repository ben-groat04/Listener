# Round 1: the music psychologist

British English, plain speech. I have read video2/REPORT.md, the top of video1/REPORT.md and the comparison table in the diptych file.

## Why these reports are not how a person listens

The reports are careful, and I would trust their numbers. That is the trouble. They describe a recording as an engineer would audit it. Nobody listens like that, and a reader can tell within a paragraph.

**1. They sort by clock time and by layer. People sort by moments and by what happens to their body.**
Quoted from video2: "The bass plays a figure grouped in threes against the kick, so it lands with the kick only on beat 3 and never on beat 1." And: "the drums about 50 dB louder, a four-on-the-floor kick and no fill." A listener does not count beats against a bass figure. They feel a steady foot-tap, maybe a bit of lilt, and they nod. What they would say is "it suddenly kicks in and I wanted to move." The report has that thought in half a sentence ("a slight limp", INT) and buries it under the arithmetic.

**2. They treat feeling as something that must be justified by a measurement.**
"Brightness, loudness and fragment length rise together, so the intensity climbs without anyone being able to say when." Real listeners feel the build first and never find the cause. Research on emotion in music (Juslin's BRECVEMA mechanisms) is clear that most of it comes from things the listener cannot name: brain-stem response to loudness and attack, rhythmic entrainment, expectation, memory, imagery. The report treats "I can't say when" as a defect. For a human it is the normal state.

**3. They count surprise as a measurement of the music rather than an event in a mind.**
"Its force comes from the gap before it and the lack of any warning (INT); it is the biggest single event in the piece." That is correct, and it is Huron's ITPRA story (imagination, tension, prediction, reaction, appraisal): a four-second hole creates tension, the return is a big reaction response, then appraisal. But the report never says who is surprised. A first-time listener is surprised. By the second listen the drop is anticipated, and the pleasure moves from shock to anticipation (Salimpoor's dopamine work: the peak of anticipation comes before the peak moment). The reports treat "the drop" as one fixed fact, and never ask whether it is the first listen or the fifth.

**4. They write as if the listener attends to everything for five minutes.**
"The sudden brightening at that instant is the narrator's own consonants, not a new instrument." "For about 47 seconds nothing new happens and the loop is hypnotic." Real attention wanders. People drift in a loop, come back on a change, and only retain a few moments (a peak, an ending, perhaps one image). Memory for music is schematic: you remember "it started quiet and cold, then a big beat came in, then it kept going, then it stopped" and your own feelings at the peaks. You do not remember "the bass changes note every 12 seconds (B, A, D)". Nobody does, including trained musicians, who are no better at this without a score.

**5. They use vocabulary nobody uses.**
"Pedal piece", "tonal centre", "mode not settled", "root", "gated", "chromatic friction", "D-centred drone", "B minor or E-centred hearing". Ordinary listeners say loud, quiet, dark, driving, build, drop, "like a march". Even many musicians cannot name a key by ear. The reports use this vocabulary to sound credible and then hedge it ("a hypothesis"), which is the worst of both: the reader gets neither the feeling nor the certainty. Video1's "third of the chord is almost never played, so the mode is not settled" is a note to a mixing engineer, not a listening experience.

**6. They assume the video is a separate object checked for sync.**
36% of cuts within 80 ms of a beat, p = 0.093. People do not test for this. They sense whether picture and sound "go together", and they do so loosely: audiovisual binding is generous (the "unity assumption"), and a shot that lingers while the music drops can feel deliberate and moving rather than a failure. The report says "the picture ignores the biggest moment in the music". A viewer may feel the opposite: the empty road holding still through the silence is the point. Calling it "ignores" is a judgement imported from the measuring.

**7. They flatten what is known and what is felt.**
The report lists "what we could not settle" as the narrator's identity, the instruments, the tonal centre. A person's open questions are different: "Is this sad? Who were these people? Did they finish? Why does it stop like that?" The reports do ask the last (the ending) but answer with a measurement of two drum hits at 5:02.33 and 5:02.58.

**8. They make everything equally weighty.**
Every claim has a tag and a timestamp. Listeners do not weight in that way. A few things dominate: the opening mood, the first human voice, the drop, the ending, and one or two pictures. The report's list "The moments that stay with you" is closest, but it is six items, and item 5 (a glance within a flurry, 4:10 to 4:12) is an observation, not something that "stays". Memory research (the peak-end rule; Kahneman's work, replicated for music) says people keep the peak and the end and lose the middle. The report spends most of its words on the middle.

**9. The tone is a committee's.**
"Best-supported word is withdrawal; mourning is weaker." "Elegy or celebration is genuinely open." No listener decides between those words. They feel something mixed (cold, communal, effortful, a bit sad) and may later find a word.

## How a person actually takes this in

I will work from first seconds to afterwards. This is a reconstruction from the research and from the facts in the reports (which I trust as facts); I have not heard the track either, and I flag where I am extrapolating.

**The first ten seconds.** Almost nothing happens in Part 2: a quiet dark pad with a few low thuds. The listener forms a first impression in a couple of seconds (thin-slice judgements of valence and arousal are made very fast): cold, slow, quiet, serious. The picture adds "old film, winter, Dutch title". Expectation sets: this will be moody, probably slow. Many people will also check whether they like it enough to stay. A quiet opening of 28 seconds is a test of patience; a casual listener on a phone may leave, and a fan will lean in.

**The first voice (0:42).** A human voice is salient (we are tuned to voices) and a foreign one with crackly archive quality signals "documentary, history, this is real". A non-Dutch speaker will not follow the words but will catch "1963" and perhaps "Elfstedentocht" and will take the tone: calm, authoritative, a bit old-fashioned. This is when most viewers decide what the piece is about. Note that the meaning arrives from context (a man's voice, snow, old film), not from the measured spectrum.

**The first steady beat.** Entrainment: the foot starts to tap by about the second bar. At 0:28 in Part 2 the bass and drums enter, and what a listener feels is "now we are going somewhere", a walk. The pull to move (groove) is strongest at medium syncopation, and a steady four-to-the-floor kick at 120 BPM is a walking, almost marching pace. That matches skaters on ice and the film's "pack". That match is probably the most universal response and is under-reported in the reports.

**The drop (1:28).** For about four seconds the beat is gone. The brain has built a strong prediction of the next kick and is denied it; tension rises (physiological arousal can be measured). Then the kick comes back at full volume. This is what many people describe as a "thrill", and some get a physical response (goosebumps, tingling) at moments like this: a loud return after a gap, a new instrument or voice, a sudden swell. Chills are individual: roughly half to two-thirds of people report them, linked to openness to experience and absorption, and they are more likely on a familiar piece. So I would not claim it works for everyone, only that it is the likeliest place in the track.

**The long middle.** Here attention does what attention does. The loop repeats for minutes. Some people fall into a pleasant trance (absorption, mind-wandering with the music in the background), some get bored, some start to watch the pictures instead. Repetition makes music feel more familiar and more liked up to a point (mere exposure, "repetition makes speech sound like song"). The reports say "hypnotic" for 47 seconds and are right, but then treat the next ninety seconds as a measured build. A listener does not notice a 22 dB increase in one layer. They notice that "it's getting more intense somehow" and may not notice at all while looking at faces in the pack. The picture does heavy lifting here: close, shaky, anonymous bodies make the repetition feel like effort. Audio-visual interaction is strongest when attention is split, so the pairing is where the feeling comes from, as the report itself half admits ("any sadness comes from the pairing").

**The ending.** Peak-end: the way it stops probably shapes how it is remembered. The track unwinds, goes quiet, stops dead, adds two small hits, then silence, while the picture goes to ducks, floes and black. A person feels a letdown or an unresolved loss, or relief, possibly a lump in the throat. Because it never resolves, some will feel unsatisfied (an expectation of a final cadence is denied), others will find that moving. I would not state which. The report's "withdrawal" is a good word but a person would say "it just trails off, and I was left looking at water."

**Afterwards.** What remains is schematic: a quiet cold start, a human voice reading a date, a steady march, a sudden surge, a long push, and an unfinished end. Plus one or two images (the empty road, a skater, black). Most will remember a mood (cold, communal, strained) and not a structure. If they know the 1963 race or have skated, memory and association flood in: personal stories (autobiographical memory, the "reminiscence bump") matter more than any analysis. Many will want to know what happened in the race (the human interest), which the reports barely serve.

**Emotion felt versus recognised.** Listeners often recognise "this is sad music" without feeling sad. The report's "withdrawal" and "mourning" are recognised categories. Felt emotion is mostly arousal and movement (excitement, tension, calm) plus nostalgia, absorption and a vague sadness from the pictures. I would put the felt emotion of Part 2 at: anticipation, a lift at the drop, a slow-burning strain, then a wistful emptiness. Not "elegy".

**Differences between listeners (the reports assume one).** A fan with the album knows the arc, the "samples of archive broadcasts" signature, and listens for how this one compares; a skater, or a Dutch speaker, hears the words and is moved or amused by the narrator; a casual viewer on a phone may be half-watching; a musician hears the pulse and the drone, notes the lack of melody and may find it a little monotonous. The reports collapse all of these into one analytic voice.

## What I would change in the reports

- Start with what a first listen feels like, in plain sentences, with at most a handful of moments (opening, voice, beat in, drop, strain, ending). Put measurements in a separate appendix.
- Say who is feeling it: first listen, fan, Dutch speaker, someone who knows the race.
- Separate "this happens in the audio" from "this is likely to happen to a listener", and say how likely (many, some, few).
- Drop the vocabulary of keys, modes, gates and stems from the main text.
- Use the measurements only to check a feeling after it has been stated (is the drop really loud? yes), not to generate it.
- Admit what cannot be known: we have no listener data; the best proxy is the research literature and real people's accounts.

## Three questions for the panel

1. **To the fan:** When you first heard Part 2, what did you notice and feel in the first minute, and what did you forget by the end? In particular, did the long middle bore you, carry you, or disappear, and what did the narrator's voice do for you if you do not speak Dutch?
2. **To the critic:** If you reviewed this in two paragraphs for a magazine, which one or two moments and which one word of feeling would you give, and which of our report's findings would you simply leave out as irrelevant to a reader?
3. **To the devil (and the viewer, if they wish):** Suppose a listener felt nothing special at the drop and nothing at the ending. Would our whole account of "how it feels" fall apart, and how would we know any of our feelings were real rather than guessed from a spectrogram?
