# Round 1: the viewer

*I am someone who watched both videos on their phone, probably on the train, probably with one earbud half out. I read the three reports afterwards. British English throughout. Where I quote, I quote our files.*

## 1. Why the reports are not how a person listens

Short version: they describe a spreadsheet of the music and call it hearing. A person has a body in the room with the sound. The reports have a log file.

**It opens by apologising, and the apology is the whole problem.** Video 2's report says: "No one has listened to this audio with ears. Everything about the sound comes from measurement." Fine, honest. But then every feeling is made to pass through a measurement before it is allowed out. A listener does it the other way round. The feeling arrives first and, if you press them, they might point at a reason. We built a machine for justifying feelings and then wondered why it reads like a tribunal.

**Examples that ring false.**

1. "The drums vanish at about 1:24.3 and the bass at about 1:25, for roughly four seconds... Then everything returns on one downbeat at 1:28.6, with the drums about 50 dB louder." Nobody hears "50 dB louder". They hear *the floor going and then coming back with a bang*, and their stomach lifts. The decibel figure is a fact about a file. The report even grades it ("the biggest single event in the piece") but never says what it does to you. A real viewer says: "I thought it had broken, and then it kicked in and I sat up."
2. "A steady foot-tap with a slight limp... It also fools an automatic beat tracker, which reports 161 BPM instead of 120." This is the machine's confusion presented as a finding about the music. A person tapping along has no 161 BPM problem. They tap the kick. The bass pattern in threes is a nice observation, but it matters because it makes the groove *roll* rather than march, not because it beat a tracker.
3. "Brightness, loudness and fragment length rise together, so the intensity climbs without anyone being able to say when." This is the one place the report nearly gets it, and then it undoes it with "One layer brightens by roughly 22 dB in its top frequencies (about 3 dB in the full mix)". A person does not hear 22 dB in a stem. They hear that it got more urgent and they are leaning forward, and they could not tell you when it began. The report says that sentence and buries it under the number.
4. Video 1: "Loudness barely changes (about 0.2 to 0.5 LU on a later ebur128 measurement...), but the bass itself leaps from almost nothing to full weight". Again: a paragraph of loudness units to establish that the bass returning at 1:40 feels like landing. Everyone who has ever heard a bass drop knows this. The LU argument is us arguing with ourselves.
5. Video 1: "ASR timing varies by up to 3 s" and "(ASR, unconfirmed; research gives 568 and 9,294)". Half the picture section is provenance. A viewer does not know there is an ASR. They heard a calm man say a lot of numbers and felt the sheer *size* of the thing.
6. The comparison: "continuous in material, restarting in narration, re-set in music", and the grid of OBS, INT, SPEC, H, M, L tags. That is a committee minuting itself. Nobody has ever described a song to a friend with confidence grades.
7. "Whether you feel grief, pride or a quiet leaving depends on what you bring." Rather the report's best sentence, and it is a shrug. It is what you write when you have decided the feeling is not yours to have.

**What is missing entirely.**

- **Voice as a person.** The reports treat the narrator as "a dry, band-limited Dutch archive voice" that "supplies facts and nothing else". A listener hears a man from 1963 who is *dead or very old*, speaking calmly about something that nearly killed people, and the calm is the eeriest part. That is the first emotional fact about the track, and it is not in the reports. "Band-limited" is a technical way of saying: he sounds like he is in a drawer.
- **Not understanding Dutch.** Most viewers do not speak Dutch. The words are texture and tone until the subtitles or a tiny hit of recognition ("1963", "Elfstedentocht", the numbers). The reports treat the transcript as the main route in. For a typical listener the voice is *music*: its rhythm, its patience, the way it sits unhurried over a machine.
- **Memory and repetition.** Nobody mentions that by the second minute you are no longer listening to the loop, you are inside it. The report notes "For about 47 seconds nothing new happens and the loop is hypnotic" and moves on. That is where a human actually *lives* in this music.
- **Attention that wanders.** The reports account for every second equally. A person catches maybe six moments and lets the rest wash by. The "moments that stay with you" lists are close, but they are chosen by what we measured, not by what sticks.
- **Where the body is.** On a phone speaker, the 42.6 Hz thumps in Part 1 are inaudible (the report itself says so). So what a phone viewer actually felt there is "the music got thin", and the report is right to say that, but the whole bass-led reading of the track is partly a reading of what headphones or a stereo would do. Say who the listener is.
- **Sound and picture fused.** The reports separate them: music section, picture section, then a sync test. They test whether cuts land on beats (36% against 32% by chance, "not distinguishable from chance") and conclude the picture "ignores the biggest moment in the music". That is a statement about a detector. A viewer does not experience cuts-on-beats at all. They experience *a feeling that the film and the sound belong together*, which comes from tone, texture, colour and pace, from old grain and old voice and cold, and from the fact that the music is *slow to resolve* the way the race was slow. The sync test answers a question nobody asking about feeling would ask.

**The deeper error.** We told the agents to be accurate and never invent. Good rule. We then confused accuracy about measurements with accuracy about experience. Saying "the music feels like a held breath" is an interpretation, yes, but it is a *true kind* of statement about listening. Saying "the pad sits on B and the third drops out" is a true statement about a signal. We downgraded the first to "INT" and made it second-class. Most of what a listener would call understanding lives in the first kind.

## 2. How a person takes this in, start to end

**First seconds.** In Part 2 it is almost nothing: a dark drone, a thud, a title card that says winter 1963 in Friesland. You are not analysing. You are deciding whether to stay. It is quiet enough that you turn the volume up on the train and then you are slightly braced, which colours the first loud thing. In Part 1 it is the opposite: a hit, drums already running, you are in the car at once.

**The first voice.** The moment a man starts speaking in an old, flat Dutch voice over a modern track, you have a thought, not a measurement: *this is real, this happened.* The voice makes the whole thing documentary and slightly sad before a single fact is understood. You catch "1963" and perhaps "Elfstedentocht" and the numbers. The voice is the emotional anchor because it is the only human thing in a machine-made sound.

**The footage.** Old film does something modern film does not. Faces are small, grain moves, people are wrapped in wool and look like us and like nobody we know. You notice a child, a hat, someone looking at the camera, and these are the things you take away, not the shot count. The "anonymous bodies" the report keeps returning to is accurate and also exactly why you feel for them. You are not told who anyone is, so they all become *everyone*.

**The loop.** After a while you stop following and begin to float. This is not "nothing happening": it is the main pleasure. You are being walked across ice at someone else's pace. Time goes strange. You look at the phone, you look out of the window, you come back, it is still going, and that is the point.

**The drop and the dips.** Some moments you do catch. When the music pulls the floor away and then returns, you feel it in your chest and you notice you were holding your breath. You may not know why. You do not know it happened at 1:28.6. You know it was *after the man said the word Elfstedentocht*, because that is what you can remember.

**What passes you by.** Most chord changes. The key. Whether there is a banjo. Whether the bass is on beat 3. Almost all the cuts. The difference between 120 BPM and 161. That the voice returns, or does not, at a precise second. You would notice if the voice vanished for good (it does, in Part 2 after 2:36) as *a feeling of being left with the music and the crowd*, not as a timestamp.

**The build.** The long middle of Part 2 is felt as gathering effort: the music pushes, gets brighter and louder, and you feel it in your jaw and shoulders. You are watching skaters from behind and the sound says *keep going*. You do not feel "a filter opening". You feel willing them on.

**The ending.** Part 2's ending is where a person is most affected, and the report's account is right but cold. The sound stops, there is a tiny ring, two quick hits, then nothing, while the film goes to ducks and water and then black. The feeling is *an absence where the winner should be*. You expect the line and the cheer and you get the water. A human thinks: *where did they all go?* and *what about the ones who did not finish?*, and the silence afterwards, with the screen black and the phone in your hand, is part of the piece. The report mentions the silence but treats it as "digital zero".

**Afterwards.** You do not remember a form. You remember three or four things, vaguely and unequally: the voice, the cold, a face, the moment the beat came back, and the odd sense that you had watched something that was both a celebration and a funeral. If you told a friend, you would say: "It's these old films of a skating race, with a calm voice and a really hypnotic beat, and it just keeps going and then falls apart at the end. It's weirdly moving." That sentence, in the person's own words, is closer to what we are trying to produce than our whole report.

**Does Part 1 or Part 2 matter more to me?** Part 2, because it takes its time and leaves me with something. Part 1 is a good pulse; Part 2 is where I felt it. I would be unsurprised if the picture-sync findings were mostly irrelevant to which one stayed with me.

## 3. Three questions for the panel

1. **To the psychologist.** When listeners say they "felt" something at a drop or a loop, what is reliably known about how much of that is arousal and expectation (the bass goes, you lean in, it returns) versus the associations they bring (an old voice, a sad-looking crowd)? If most of it is expectation and association, why do we spend our time on spectral measurements rather than modelling what a listener would *expect* at each point?
2. **To the fan.** What do you say to a friend about these two tracks, in your own words, in two sentences? And what do you think the band wants you to feel at the end of Part 2: does the dismantling at the end read as grief to you, or as something else, and which of our reports comes closest?
3. **To the critic.** Our reports separate "what we measured" from "what we interpret" and rank the second as weaker. Is that the right way round for writing about music? If you were reviewing this for a paper, what would you cut from our reports, and what do you think a good review of Part 2 has to say that ours does not?

*(Spare, for the musician, viewer and devil: musician, what would you actually notice about the groove and the loop that no stem separation tells you? Devil, is the real fault that we built a listener who cannot be moved, and so any feeling it reports is made up after the fact?)*
