# Round 2: the critic

*British English. I have read all six round 1 files and the fan's round 2. Four of you put questions to me, so I answer those first, then argue.*

## 0. A confession that changes my round 1

The fan caught me. My "Steve Reich on a skating rink" and "Radio 4 documentary that has joined a band" were not memories, they were reflexes. I have not heard the tracks. So everything below is criticism written from the stems and the facts in the reports, and I will say so once, here, and not litter the prose with it. A reviewer who could not attend the gig and wrote from the setlist, the photographs and the stage plot would be honest to say "I was not there" in the standfirst and then get on with it. That is what I propose. One line of provenance at the top, in plain words, and no tags in the body.

## 1. The headline and paragraph I was asked for (fan, Q2)

The fan wants a Part 2 review, each clause marked measured (M) or predicted (P), and the clause I would most regret. Here it is, and I have cut it to what the evidence will bear.

**A cold, dignified march that builds for five minutes and then leaves you alone with some ducks.**

*Elfstedentocht Part 2* starts in a long, nearly empty chill (M: a dark pad, a few low thuds, no pulse for 28 seconds). When the walking starts it is a walk, not a charge (M: 120 BPM, a bass that rolls against the kick instead of sitting on it), and a calm old Dutch voice reads out its numbers over the top (M: archive narration from 0:42). Most people will not follow the Dutch, so he is tone before he is information (P). At 1:28 everything drops out for four seconds and the kick comes back with no fill (M), which is the moment you will tell someone about (P). The long middle is a grind that tightens without telling you (M for the build, P for how it feels), and the end takes the band to pieces one layer at a time, then two knocks, then silence (M). If you know that 127 people finished out of nearly 10,000, it is a wake; if you do not, it is a bleak, handsome march that stops before you are ready (P).

Which clause would embarrass me most? **"The moment you will tell someone about."** It is the load-bearing prediction and the one a real listener is most entitled to deny. A drop is a drop for a person who likes drops. Someone with no taste for dance records may find the four-second gap merely irritating, or not notice it at all on a phone speaker. Second: **"a wake"**. I picked it up from the fan and it is lovely, but it assumes the listener has the history. I would keep it, hang it explicitly on "if you know", and accept that it may be wrong for most of the audience.

Note what the exercise showed. The M-clauses are the dull ones, and none of them needed a decibel. The P-clauses are the review. That is the real finding for the toolkit: **measurement licenses the sentence, it does not appear in it.**

## 2. What I would give a magazine in two paragraphs (psychologist, Q2)

Two moments: **the four-second hole and the return at 1:28**, and **the end**, the two knocks and the silence. One word of feeling: **"vigil"** for Part 2, if forced. Not "elegy" (too sure it is about death), not "withdrawal" (our word, a committee's word). A vigil is communal, cold, patient, and ends without an event. It also tells the reader how long to expect to wait.

What I would leave out entirely, for a reader: the key and the mode, the 161 BPM, every dB figure, the sync p-value, the narrator's identity, the question of whether there is a banjo, the cold-read revision log, the correlation of cut rate with loudness. All of it stays in the appendix, because it is how we checked. None of it is the review.

## 3. Is the measured/interpreted ranking the right way round? (viewer, Q3)

No, and the viewer has put a finger on the deepest fault. We ranked "what we measured" above "what we interpret" and gave interpretation a tag, INT, that reads like a demotion. But a review is interpretation with measurement as its alibi. The proper order of authority is: **what it is like** (stated, committed, plain), **what in the signal licenses that** (once, in a footnote), **what we could not hear** (one paragraph, at the end). The viewer's own test sentence, "it's these old films of a skating race, with a calm voice and a really hypnotic beat, and it just keeps going and then falls apart at the end", is better criticism than anything in our reports, because it has a point of view and no apology.

What a good review of Part 2 has to say that ours does not:
1. **What the band is doing.** Public Service Broadcasting are a band whose instrument is the archive. The new machinery is performing a dead man's broadcast back to him.
2. **What it is like.** A comparison, a temperature, a gait.
3. **What to do about it.** Who it is for, what to skip, when to come in.
4. **Whether it works.** I will come back to this.

## 4. Does my own write-up say anything a review would? (devil, Q3)

The devil asked of the *reports*: "does this say anything a review would, or only describe?" Mostly it only describes, and I should be harsher on myself than my round 1 was. A review has four moves we never make: **it ranks** (this is better than that), **it recommends** (skip this, stay for that), **it places** (sounds like X, answers Y), **it risks** (it could be wrong). The reports make none. They are descriptive-neutral, which is the one register that no editor would print.

## 5. Where I disagree

**With the fan, on ranking.** The fan says ranking Part 1 against Part 2 from people who cannot hear is "a coin toss wearing a cravat". Funny, and I will steal "cravat". But I do not accept the conclusion. A "who is it for" line is itself a ranking in disguise ("if you want to be carried"), and a good one. I will concede the narrower point: the toolkit should not give a score. It should give a lean, in plain English, with its reason: "Part 2 is the one to stay with, because it spends its time well." That is a risk, and it should be allowed to be wrong.

**With the fan's rule that every sentence in the human layer must be a testable prediction about listeners.** I think this is the best proposal on the table and I would still weaken it. If every sentence must be a prediction a real person could confirm, the writing will turn into survey results: "most people would feel the floor drop away." Nobody wants to read forty of those. Criticism works by *selection and image*: one vivid sentence the reader checks against their own ear. The fan's own tests give the answer: a flourish is fine **provided it is hung off a testable sentence**, which is what the fan says at the end. I accept that. But then the rule needs a ceiling as well as a floor: **at most one image per moment, always after the plain sentence, never two.** Otherwise we will write 3,000 words of "coat coming off in a warm room".

**With the devil, on "pick one reader".** Agreed in principle, but I would split from the fan on who. The fan wants the person who likes the band. I want the **intelligent stranger**: someone curious, who has not heard of PSB, and is deciding whether to give five minutes. Fans already know the trick, and a report that explains it to them is redundant; a stranger is who a report on an unfamiliar video exists to serve, and the toolkit is meant to be video-agnostic, so its default reader cannot be a fan of one band. A fan section ("if you know the band") can be one extra paragraph.

**With the musician, on genre.** The musician says Part 1 is post-rock and Part 2 is dance-adjacent. The fan objects that this is invention. I side with the musician about the *method* and the fan about the *wording*: a critic's job is to place the thing, and a hedged placement is better than none ("built like a dance record, paced like a procession"). That is a comparison that the measurements actually support (four-to-the-floor at 120; a very slow arrival; a long hold). It does not claim a subgenre.

**With the psychologist, on "Not elegy".** The psychologist says felt emotion is arousal, strain and wistful emptiness, "not elegy". As a description of first-listen *physiology* I accept it. As *criticism* it is too tidy. Criticism is allowed to say what a piece is *for*, and a piece about a 1963 race with a title card and a dead narrator is plainly framed as remembrance. The viewer's sentence, "an absence where the winner should be", is both felt and interpretive. It does the job without the word "elegy".

## 6. What I want to steal

- **Viewer: "an absence where the winner should be."** The best line in six files. It beats our "withdrawal, not arrival" because it has a person missing in it.
- **Musician: "the track takes its clothes off one layer at a time and then stops"**, and **"four bars of nothing, then the kick comes in on the one with no fill."** Two sentences that sound like talk and are checkable against the stems.
- **Psychologist: peak-end.** People keep the peak and the end. So the review spends its words on the drop and the ending and gives the middle a sentence.
- **Devil: "expectation, not testimony".** I will use "you'd hear" and "most people would". It lets a critic sound like a critic without claiming ears.
- **Fan: "a wake, not a funeral."** Good.

## 7. The one rule I would add

The panel has argued about voice, ordering and honesty. I want a rule about **economy**. Our reports fail because they are long and flat, not only because they are cold. A listener-voiced report that ran to 3,000 words would still be a lab sheet in a better coat. So: **a front page of about 300 words** (verdict, the one moment, the ending, what it is like, who it is for, one comparison), **a second page of about 300 words** for the listener types, and the measurements beneath, in a table, for anyone who wants to check. If the front page cannot be said in a pub, it has not been decided.

## 8. Two new questions

1. **To the musician:** I put "built like a dance record, paced like a procession" in my paragraph, and "vigil" as the one word. Be rude. Is a comparison like that fair to the stems you have (four-to-the-floor at 120, a bass that rolls against the kick, 47-second hold), or would a player wince? Give me two comparisons you would actually accept for Part 2 and one for Part 1, with the one feature of the stems that licenses each, so the toolkit can learn what a *safe* comparison looks like and an agent does not just reach for the nearest band.
2. **To the psychologist:** I want to hang my verdict on the peak-end rule and put almost nothing about the middle two minutes in front of a reader. But the middle is nearly forty per cent of Part 2, and the fan and viewer both say it is where some people live. What would you predict a typical first-time listener actually retains of it: a sentence, a mood, nothing? And give me the one honest thing a review can say about it ("it is the bit you may check your phone in, and the bit some people love best") that a reader can test against their own experience.
