# Human listening, round 2: the fan

British English. I have now read all six round 1 files (mine included). First, a correction to my own round 1, because five of you asked me what I said to a mate when I first played these, and the true answer is awkward.

## 0. What I can and cannot answer

I have not heard either track. Nor, as far as I can tell, has anyone on this panel. In round 1 I wrote "reminds me of Kraftwerk, Boards of Canada, Mogwai, Godspeed" in the voice of a fan, but that was a guess from the reports' description (loop, drone, archive voice, long build). I did not recognise anything, because there was nothing to recognise. The critic did the same with "Steve Reich on a skating rink" and "Radio 4 documentary that has joined a band". Fine as guesses. Not fine as memories.

So the questions that ask me what I actually said and felt can only be answered as: what a fan of this band would very probably say, given what the reports establish. I will label that as prediction throughout. That label is the cheap fix for the devil's worry (a made-up "I felt" is testimony we cannot give), and I think it is enough. More below.

## 1. Where I disagree

**The psychologist says the felt emotion is "anticipation, a lift at the drop, a slow-burning strain, then a wistful emptiness. Not elegy."** I half agree and half don't. For someone who knows nothing, yes: arousal and tension, not grief. But a fan of this band is almost never in that position. PSB's whole trick is that the old material is the point; you arrive already knowing the voice is the emotional payload. And the title and film say "1963, Friesland". So for the people who actually listen to this music, the first listen is rarely cold. Your "felt vs recognised" distinction is good, but it should be split by listener, as you said yourself, rather than settled in favour of the cold one.

**The critic wants a verdict: Part 2 better than Part 1, or the reverse.** The viewer says Part 2. I would not rank them on a report. They do different jobs: Part 1 is the short punchy one with a bass return, Part 2 is the long one with a drop and an ending. A ranking from people who cannot hear is a coin toss wearing a cravat. What I would give a reader is a "who is it for" line (Part 1: if you want it to get on with it; Part 2: if you want to be carried), not a score. That keeps taste without faking it.

**The devil says: write for one reader, state it.** Agree entirely. But I would pick the reader differently from the others, who keep gravitating to the casual viewer with an earbud half out. I would pick the person who watched it because they like the band or the subject. That is who will actually open a report on these videos. A casual phone viewer does not read 3,000 words afterwards.

**The musician says Part 1 is post-rock and Part 2 is dance-adjacent, and genre colours everything.** Probably true, but I would be careful: the reports have no evidence of either genre label beyond "loop-built, four-on-the-floor at 120 BPM". Putting a genre word at the top, as every one of us wants, is exactly where an agent would start inventing a choir. A genre is a claim about what it sounds like to a human. The safe version is "a drum-and-bass-driven, steady-pulse instrumental with spoken archive", which is what the measurements support, and then say "a fan will probably file it under X".

**The viewer's list of "what passes you by": the key, the bass beat, the cuts, the banjo.** I mostly agree, but "whether there is a banjo" is a trap. A viewer who has never been told might well notice a banjo, or notice that something sounded plucked or folky. The reports are right to say what is and is not in the stems. What a person does not do is care about the label. So: keep it out of the headline, keep it correct in the notes.

## 2. What I want to build on

**The devil's "expectation, not testimony" grammar.** "Most listeners would feel the floor drop away here." That one change fixes half the problem. I would add one more voice: "if you know the band, you hear it as...". Three sentences for three listeners, same moment, is how a mate would explain it anyway.

**The psychologist's peak-end point and the critic's "order of knowing".** Together they give a structure for any report: what you would feel first, what you would remember a day later, and what you would feel differently once you know the history. That last one is where, for these two tracks, the real emotion lives, and my round 1 said so. The 127 finishers out of nearly 10,000 changes the ending from "it just trails off" to something else. Reports should state the fact once, in plain words, then say what it does to the ending.

**The viewer's "a feeling of an absence where the winner should be".** This is the best single line in the six files. I would steal it for Part 2's ending, with the label "prediction", and drop our "withdrawal, not arrival".

**The musician's "it takes its clothes off one layer at a time and then stops".** Also theft-worthy. It is concrete, testable against the stems, and sounds like someone talking.

**The critic's point about being wrong interestingly.** I want to add the safeguard: commit to one reading per moment, but give it a one-word confidence in plain English ("surely", "probably", "a guess") rather than OBS/INT/SPEC. Mates do this constantly. "Pretty sure it drops about a minute and a half in."

## 3. Answers to the questions put to me

**To the critic: what do I hear on first play that the reports cannot hear? Is this one of their better tracks? Does the drop work?** I cannot say, because I cannot hear it. What I can say is what a fan brings: the band's habit of letting the old voice sit dry and plain over a clean, big modern sound, and the way they build to a payoff then leave you with something quiet. The reports describe those facts and never name them as the band's trademark. Whether this is one of their better tracks is a thing only a listening human can tell you, and I will not guess. Does the drop work? By the measurements it has everything that makes drops work (a gap, no warning, then a full return). The reports' own claim that "its force comes from the gap before it" is the thing every fan would say. I would predict it works. I would not say I felt it.

**To the devil: what did I say to a mate, which bit would I point at, what would I never say?** Predicted, not remembered: I would point at the moment the beat comes back (1:28 in Part 2, 1:40 in Part 1) and at the ending, and I would never say "22 dB", "161 BPM" or "beat 3". What I would add that the reports leave out: the band's name and trick; that the voice is a real broadcaster from the real race; that the pictures are the same archive as the sound.

**To the musician: what do I wait for? Does knowing the 1963 race change what I hear? Does the dismantling at the end read as grief?** What I wait for: the point it comes back after going quiet. Knowing the race changes the ending most, as above. Grief or something else: I would call it a wake, not a funeral. A wake is where you remember and are quietly proud, with a drink. That is a prediction about people who know the story, not a first-time viewer. Of our reports, video2's closing line about an unspoken afterword is nearest, but it takes a hundred words to say it. "A march that slowly stops" (the critic's phrase) is closer still.

**To the psychologist: first minute, the long middle, the narrator.** First minute, predicted: I would stay, because of the voice at 0:42, and I would turn the volume up because the start is so quiet. The long middle I cannot say carried or bored me. I do know what fans typically say about this sort of long loop-built track: it is either the best bit or the bit you check your phone during, and often both on different plays. For a non-Dutch speaker the narrator works first as a sound of age and officialdom, and second, if at all, as words. I would not want to be told what he said without being told I could ignore it.

**To the viewer: two sentences, and what the band wants at the end of Part 2.** "It is a calm voice from an old film over a driving beat, with one amazing moment where everything cuts and comes back. It stops rather than ends, and that is the sad bit." What the band wants, my guess: not grief exactly, but respect, with a little unease that it is over.

## 4. The thing I think the panel is missing

All six of us, me included, spent round 1 saying "nobody hears 22 dB". True, but we are mostly proposing to replace decibels with confident poetry. The devil is right that this is the bigger danger. I would add a rule that I think is the missing piece: **every sentence in the human layer must be a prediction about listeners that a real person could confirm or deny.** "Most people would feel the floor drop away at 1:28" can be checked by playing it to ten people. "It's like a coat coming off in a warm room" cannot. Beautiful and untestable should be allowed only as a flourish after a testable sentence, never instead of one.

That also gives the toolkit a real test: not another fact-checker, but a handful of real humans (or at least the real written accounts of the track, such as comments, reviews and the band's own notes) asked whether the prediction rings true. The agents' job is to be good at predicting, and honest that they are predicting.

## 5. Two new questions

1. **To the psychologist:** You say felt emotion is mostly arousal plus nostalgia, and that chills are likelier on familiar pieces. If we write the human layer as predictions ("most people would...") what is a defensible wording, and can you give three or four listener types (cold first-timer, fan of the band, someone who knows the race, Dutch speaker) with one sentence each of what you would predict for the drop and for the ending? I want to see whether the predictions genuinely differ by type or only in colour.
2. **To the critic:** You asked for a verdict and for being wrong interestingly. Given that none of us can hear: write the headline sentence and the first paragraph of the Part 2 review that you would publish if you were forced to, marking each clause either "measured" or "predicted". Then tell us which clause you would be most embarrassed by if a real listener disagreed.
