# Human listening, round 2: the musician

British English, plain speech. I have read all six round 1 files and the two round 2 files already written (fan, psychologist). Still no ears on this: nobody on the panel has heard either track. Where I say "you would hear", it is what a player predicts from the facts the stems give us, not a memory.

## 0. Two corrections to my own round 1, then one thing I checked

**"Four bars of nothing" was wrong.** The psychologist caught it. Drums out at 1:24.3, everything back at 1:28.6 is about 4.3 seconds. At 120 BPM a bar is 2 seconds, so that is two bars, a bit over. I wrote it the way a player writes it from the feel of a break, and I did not count. Worse, the bass only leaves at about 1:25, so the true full-silence gap is nearer a bar and three-quarters. And before it the drums were already thin (the report says they nearly vanish at 1:01 and 1:10), so the ear is not coming from a full groove into a hole. It is coming from a half-built groove into a smaller hole.

**"Fifty dB" is noise and I said so, but I then leaned on it anyway.** The ratio is against a near-silent floor. The honest statement is "the kick comes back at full weight on the one, with no fill". Keep it.

**What I checked.** The report says the bass is "grouped in threes against the kick" and that this "fools" the tracker into 161 BPM. I took onsets from `v1/data/video2/stems/bass.mp3` over 100 to 130 s. The commonest gap between bass notes is 0.372 to 0.375 seconds. At 120 BPM a sixteenth note is 0.125 s, so that is three sixteenths, a dotted quaver. 160 BPM is exactly one pulse per 0.375 s. So the tracker is not fooled by a mystery: it is hearing the bass pattern's own pulse. In player's terms, the bass is playing a **dotted-eighth ostinato over a straight four-on-the-floor kick**. That is a very familiar sound (it is the rhythm of a dotted-eighth delay on a guitar, the Edge's trick; also half of what makes a lot of dance and post-rock records roll). The pattern repeats every three beats against a four-beat bar, so it only lines up with the kick bar every three bars. I have not tested which beat of the bar it lands on, so "only on beat 3, never on beat 1" in the report stays as the report's claim; I would not repeat it. What I would say, and can stand behind: **the bass runs in groups of three sixteenths across a kick in fours.** (Other gaps in my output, 0.116 to 0.25 s, are fills and ghost notes or onset noise. I have not separated those.)

This is useful to answer the fan's question, below.

## 1. The question put to me

### The psychologist: thrill or shock? Does the drop work by surprise on a first listen?

Mostly by fulfilment, not surprise, and only for a certain listener.

If you play or listen to dance, post-rock or any build-and-release music, you know a drop is owed the moment you hear the record drop out. The pad and the voice with no kick under them is a signal that something is about to land on the one. You do not know when; you know that it will. So the thrill is the well-timed arrival of a promise the genre made. That is how most breaks work: the pleasure is in being right, and in how well the player times it. Two bars is a normal length for a break. A producer who wants a shock would hold it longer than expected (four, eight, or a bar and a half that breaks the phrase), or cut early.

If you do not know the genre, the same event is a surprise, and probably a bigger one, because you had no expectation to confirm.

**So the one thing the reports should not have said** is "it comes with no warning". For a fan of the genre there is a warning: the drums thin out (1:01, 1:10), the bass drops, the voice goes on alone. The warning is the removal. The report's own point, "its force comes from the gap", is right, and it is also the thing that tells a listener it is coming. Both can be true: you see the cliff and still feel the step.

**What in the first 28 seconds tells you which record it is?** Three things, in order of how fast you get them. (1) No pulse and no drums: not a rock record, not a pop record. (2) A held pad with a few low, spaced hits: a film score, ambient, or a build track that is taking its time. (3) Very quiet, with almost nothing above 4 kHz: the producer is showing you how loud the loud part will be. Nobody says "this is a build track" aloud, but you start waiting. That is exactly the waiting I called "waiting" in round 1. An experienced listener holds their breath for the first beat; they do not need the 0:28 step to tell them. When the walking begins at 0:28 you recognise a track going somewhere.

### The fan: be ruthless. Which measured details would a trained ear hear?

Ruthless list, sorted. Everything here is a prediction from the stems; I have not heard it.

**You would hear and be moved by:**
- The kick returning on the one at 1:28.6 with no fill. Yes.
- The pad alone, then the walking, then the voice: the order of arrival. Yes.
- The bass rolling in a three-sixteenth figure over a straight kick. A bassist or drummer hears this as "it rolls", a non-player as "it has a lilt". Yes, and it is the groove.
- The ending coming apart layer by layer and stopping. Yes, and in round 1 I quoted the best phrase for it.
- The new-bass-note-every-12-seconds in the clearing: you feel it as the chord changing underneath, slowly, without hearing a chord. A trained ear hears "the bass moves up and the floor shifts". Marginal but yes.
- The top-end opening over the plateau (3:26 to 3:50). A producer hears a filter opening or a synth being pushed. Yes, as a build. Nobody hears 22 dB.

**You would not hear it as a fact, though it works on you:**
- "The missing third" in Part 1. A trained ear cannot name that the third is absent by ear on a wall of distorted guitars. What you hear is that the riff is hard, empty and neither happy nor sad. That is the effect; the missing third is the cause, and the cause belongs in a note.
- "B minor or E-centred." Even a good player hears "a minor-ish drone, probably on B or E". A pedal under a pad. Do not argue the tonic.
- "The seven notes B C# D E F# G A." Zero value to a listener. It is B Aeolian (natural minor). Say "natural minor, stays there" and be done.
- "36% of cuts within 80 ms of the beat." A viewer cannot feel an 80 ms window; the player's version is "the cutting is not on the beat grain", and I now distrust even that (see the viewer, below).
- 42.6 Hz thumps in Part 1 (the viewer's point). Below the range of most laptop and phone speakers. On a phone the listener does not get this at all, on a decent system they get a pressure in the chest. Say "if your speakers go that low".
- "161 BPM", "120 BPM grid" as numbers. Nobody counts. "A steady walking pace, quick" is it. The point of 120 is that it is the tempo of walking and of countless dance records: that is why it feels like a march.

**You would only hear if someone told you:**
- Which exact seconds. No.
- That the stems say the voice is "band-limited". You hear that it sounds old. "Old radio" is the whole report.

So, the ruthless verdict: of the technical things in the reports, **about five survive**: the pad-then-walk-then-voice order, the withheld kick, the rolling bass, the build over the plateau, the stripped ending. The rest is how we got there.

### The devil: which technical observations would I say to another player, which are noise?

Said to another player across a table: "It's a build record. Four on the floor, bass rolling in dotted eighths across it, filter coming up over about a minute, a break and slam at 1:28, the voice is the lead. It ends by taking the layers off one at a time." That is the lot. Five sentences. The report is correct, and it is those sentences with a lot of padding.

Noise to a player: the tracker disagreeing; the p-values; the dB deltas; "within 80 ms"; the seven pitch classes; where the note falls inside the stem. A player does not reject these as wrong. A player just does not reach for them.

I disagree with the devil's fear that dropping decibels invites invention (the choir). My answer is the rule the fan wrote: every sentence must be checkable against the stems. "A kick, bass dotted-eighths over it, filter sweep on a synth, voice on top" are all checkable. A choir is not in the stems. The vocabulary is a way of describing the stems, not a licence to add to them. Instruments I would name (kick, bass, pad, a synth layer) are the claims the stems let me make; "banjo" is not one.

### The viewer's spare question: what would I notice about the groove and the loop that no stem separation tells you?

Four things. All predictions.

1. **Whether the bass and kick make the groove feel like pushing or dragging.** Stem separation hands you onsets. The feel is in the micro-timing: a bass note a few milliseconds behind the kick feels relaxed and heavy; ahead feels urgent. The reports can measure that gap (the onset times are right there) and have not. This is the single most informative number about "groove" that nobody has looked at, and it is checkable.
2. **The sound of the kick.** A soft, round, boomy kick is a different record from a tight click. The reports have no word for the kick's character, only its timing. That is the biggest hole.
3. **What the loop does to your attention.** In a three-against-four pattern, the ear keeps almost finding a repeat and then not finding it for three bars. It stays interested without any event. A listener calls it "hypnotic". That explains why 47 seconds does not drag, and we could say so, because the pattern length is known: it takes three bars of the kick to line up with one cycle of the bass. (I have not checked that the real bass loops this way. The ostinato spacing suggests it.)
4. **The space.** Where a track sits in the stereo field, how dry or wet the voice is. We have stems but we are not saying anything about width, reverb, or distance. "Dry and close" is what the report says of the voice; a producer would also say "mono, up front, no room around it", which is the sound of an archive recording sat on top of a big modern mix.

## 2. Where I disagree

**The psychologist's forward-in-time pass.** I like it a lot and I would build it. But I disagree with one thing: that the surprise at 1:28 is something we can recover by feeding cumulative chunks. A first-time listener's surprise is not only "not knowing the next bar", it is **missing the warning signs that a player sees**. A forward pass by an agent that knows what drops look like (as these models do, from text) will see the warning and write "anticipation". That is more truthful than a pretend-surprise, and I would say so in the report: "if you know the genre, you see it coming".

**The critic: "commit, be wrong in an interesting way."** I back the critic on a verdict and I back the psychologist on "do not pick one emotion word". For a musician those do not collide. You can commit on the playing ("a rolling bass over a straight kick, built to a break and slam") and leave the feeling mixed. What you must not do is hedge the playing. Round 1's "how it was made cannot be recovered" about the late layer reads as a tic. If you cannot say what a layer is, say "a synth-sounding top layer, probably, and a vocal-like fragment I cannot pin down". That is allowed to be a guess.

**The fan: "skip the plateau, it's the bit you check your phone in."** In a build record the plateau is the point; the information is in what the filter does. But that is taste, and the fan is a better judge. The report should have one such line. Not two.

**The viewer's "the picture refuses to cheer for the drop is a story told by the detector."** The psychologist agrees with the viewer; in round 1 I said the opposite and I now think I overreached. A cut on the one at 1:28.6 would be a player's "obvious" choice; the film not doing it may just mean the edit was done to the pictures, not the music. I cannot hear intention in a sync statistic. I withdraw "that is a decision" and keep "the picture does not punch the drop, and that is a restraint someone may feel".

**The devil's "state one reader".** I would pick the musician-adjacent listener: someone who plays or cares about arrangement, who will open a report and wants to know how it is built, in words they use. Different from the fan's pick (a fan of the band). Probably the toolkit needs one report with two layers: a three-line "how it plays" and a longer "what it does to you". Otherwise each of us is pulling the one voice in our own direction.

## 3. What I want to build on

- **The viewer's "a feeling of an absence where the winner should be."** Hearing it as a player: it is a bar of rest at the end, where the band expects the last chord and gets silence. The piece stops on an unresolved bar; no tonic arrives. It works because the harmony never resolves anywhere to begin with (the report says: "never changes key or resolves"). That is a structural reason for the viewer's feeling, which is the best kind. Say it that way.
- **The psychologist's "taught, then denied."** In music-making terms: you set up a groove for sixty seconds (teaching), then take a layer out (denial), then restore it (payoff). Play-the-gap is exactly that. I would write it as the three-beat arc and not the dB.
- **The fan's "a wake, not a funeral."** That is also an arrangement statement. A funeral march is slow, minor, falling. A wake has a steady walk and then people leave one at a time. The ending subtracts layers one by one. The layers are the guests. It is a genuinely good reading and it is consistent with the stems.
- **The devil and fan's rule: sentences must be predictions about listeners that a real person could confirm.** For music: "the bass rolls" is testable by tapping along; "the kick comes in on one" is testable by anyone. I would add a second sort of test the fan did not: **a player test**. Give three working musicians the stems and the draft, and ask them to say which sentences they would not have said. That is cheap and it tells us which parts are really how players talk.

## 4. How would I play it to someone to make them hear the gap before the drop?

(The critic asked this in round 1; nobody has yet answered.) Set up the walk. Say nothing; put on the record about ten seconds before 1:28 so they have the groove in their feet. Then, as the kick drops out, **point at the person's chest, not the speaker**. The bass is the thing that has been under their feet, and for about a bar and three quarters the floor is a pad and an old man's voice. If you are playing live, you do not stop the music: you take the kick off and count the bar out loud, "one, two, three, four" over the top, and bring the kick back on the next "one". Then ask them what they felt. The answer is usually a small laugh: the body has noticed it is being played with.

Written down, for a report, this becomes: "For about a bar and three-quarters the floor goes: no kick, no bass, just the pad and the old voice saying what the day is about. Then the kick comes back on the one, plain and heavy, with no fill. It lands because you were waiting for it." That is not poetry. It is a prediction about attention and a statement about the stems, in that order, with the number removed.
