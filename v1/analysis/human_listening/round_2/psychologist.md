# Round 2: the music psychologist

British English, plain speech. I have read all six round 1 files. I have not heard the tracks either, so what follows is what the research and the reports' own facts let me say, with the limits marked.

## 1. The question put to me (the viewer's, the devil's, the critic's, the fan's, the musician's)

Five panellists asked me versions of one thing: what is reliable about how people feel music, and what does that mean for what we write? Here is the answer in one place.

**What is shared across listeners, and so safe to commit to.**
- Coarse arousal and mood, formed fast. People tell calm from intense and dark from bright in a fraction of a second (recognition of basic emotion in very short excerpts is well established). So the critic's first-ten-seconds question has a plain answer: first comes a gist (loud or quiet, slow or fast, warm or cold, human or machine), then genre, then the body (is there a pulse to join?), and only later any words or meaning. Words are last, and in a foreign language they may never arrive.
- Reflex responses to loudness, sudden entries and dropouts. A long quiet and then a loud return raises arousal in nearly everyone with working ears. That is the shared part of the 1:28 drop in Part 2 and the 1:40 bass return in Part 1.
- Entrainment. A steady pulse near 120 beats per minute gets people moving or at least tapping, and a pulse with a little syncopation (the report's "limp") tends to invite more movement than a dead-straight one. This is the single most universal response and our reports under-use it.
- Peak and end matter in retrospect. I overstated this in round 1. The peak-end finding comes mainly from pain and everyday experience, and the evidence for music is supportive but thinner. Treat it as "peaks and endings are over-represented in what people say afterwards", not as a law.

**What varies so much that a single "human reading" misleads.**
- Chills. People differ hugely, and published rates vary widely by study and by how you ask. They are linked to absorption and openness, and are likelier with music you already know. I gave "half to two-thirds" in round 1 with more confidence than the literature earns. Say "some people".
- Specific emotion words (grief, pride, nostalgia). Listeners usually recognise a character ("this is solemn") without feeling it, and what they do feel is mostly arousal plus whatever they bring.
- Associations: a voice from the dead, a family skating story, knowing the 1963 race. These do not shade the feeling, they can be the feeling.
- Boredom. The long middle is where people split: some go under, some drift off to the window.

**The viewer's real question: how much is expectation, and how much association?** Honest answer: nobody can apportion it, and it depends on the listener. For a first-time, unfamiliar-with-Dutch viewer I would lean on expectation and bodily response, because they have little else. For someone who knows the race, association swamps it. That is itself the finding: the order in which feeling and knowledge arrive differs between people, as the critic said.

**And the viewer's sharper point: why spend our time on spectra rather than modelling what a listener expects?** I agree with the thrust and disagree with the conclusion. Expectation is the right output. The measurements are the right input. You cannot say "the beat was learned for 60 seconds and then withdrawn" without knowing where the beat is. So keep the stems; stop reporting them as the experience.

## 2. The finding I missed in round 1: the reports are written with hindsight

Reading all six, this is the best-supported complaint, and nobody quite named it. Our agents read the whole piece before writing a word. They know at 0:30 that a drop comes at 1:28. A listener does not. That is why "the hole and the drop" reads like a plan and not like a surprise, and why the "long middle" is treated as a sixty-second section when a listener lives it as an unlabelled present with no end in sight.

Surprise, tension and relief (Huron's account: imagine, predict, react, appraise) exist only for a mind that does not yet know the next bar. A report written from the end cannot contain them. It can only describe their audible cause.

Concrete fix, which a workflow can do: a forward-in-time pass. Feed an agent the dossier cumulatively (0 to 10 s, then 0 to 28 s, then 0 to 60 s...) and ask each time only: what have I been taught to expect, what just confirmed or broke it, how strong is the change, how would a listener's state shift? Join the notes afterwards. The surprise at 1:28 is then written by someone who did not know it was coming.

## 3. Where I agree, argue and build

**The critic: "commit, be wrong in an interesting way."** Half agree. Commit on the shared layer (it is cold, patient, walking, then the floor returns). On the contested layer (grief or pride) do not pick one word. I disagree that picking is more human. Real listeners hold mixed states and often cannot name them. The fan's "a funeral and a party at once, on ice" is a better human sentence than any single word, and it is a mixed state stated plainly. The fault in our report was not the mixing. It was "depends on what you bring", which is a shrug; the human version says what two or three different people would bring.

**The devil: say "most listeners would", and keep the numbers in footnotes.** Good, and I would tighten it. "Most listeners" is dangerous because it invents an average listener. Use three real listener types the panel has already drawn (first-time non-Dutch speaker on a phone; fan; someone who knows the race) and say what differs. Also the devil's fear of a made-up choir is answered by the form: an expectation claim is checkable in a way "it felt warm" is not. "After sixty seconds of a steady kick, eight beats of nothing, then the kick returns on the downbeat" is a testable statement about what was taught and denied. Check the denial in the stems; the feeling follows by reasoning that a reader can contest.

**The musician: "being carried", "by 4:00 you're leaning forward".** I like it but it is a claim about a body that no one has measured. It is likely for some. For others the same stretch is mind-wandering, and research on listening in daily life finds a lot of that. I would write both. The musician is right about one thing I want to underline: the build is a continuous creep with no event, so a report that locates it with dB is mis-shaped, not just cold.

**The musician's "four bars of nothing".** A small correction for the record: four seconds at 120 beats per minute is eight beats, two bars. It matters, because two bars is short enough that many dance listeners would call it a standard break, not a rupture. Whether it felt sudden depends on the listener's genre habits. Which is my question to the musician below.

**The fan: "skip the plateau", and "127 finishers does more to the music than any chord."** The first is honest taste and I would keep one such line per report. The second is true for people who know the number and nobody else, so say whose it is. Fan and critic both noted that a human places music against other music in seconds. Agreed, and the research supports it: genre and reference recognition happen almost at once and set expectations for the rest. That is an association layer we can supply and currently do not.

**The viewer: the voice as a person.** The best point in the round, and well backed. A voice is processed as a person almost automatically, and unintelligible speech still carries tone, pace and age. So "Dutch is only texture" is half right: it is texture for the words and a person for everything else. The reports' "dry, band-limited archive voice" gets the first and not the second. Also the viewer's point that on a phone speaker the 42.6 Hz thumps in Part 1 vanish. The listener's equipment changes what is felt, and the report should say which listener it speaks for.

**The viewer on the sync test.** Agreed from the research side: audiovisual binding is generous, and loose sync reads as "belongs together" or as a deliberate refusal to cheer. Whether the picture "ignores" the drop is a story told by the detector, and a viewer may feel it as restraint. I would keep the measurement as a note and move the effect to the body text as something to feel.

## 4. A proposed shape, from the psychology

1. One paragraph: the gist a listener forms in ten seconds, and what it sets them up to expect.
2. A short forward-in-time ledger of four to six moments only, each as: what you have been taught to expect, what happens, what it does (a lift, a held breath, a drift). Moments chosen by salience (entries, dropouts, ends, the first human voice), not by what we measured most.
3. A line on the middle, written as what a person does there (settles, drifts, notices later that it got fiercer), not as a count of seconds.
4. The ending, and what lingers afterwards: three or four things, unequally remembered.
5. A short "who is listening": the three listener types, and where they diverge.
6. Evidence below the fold, one line per claim. Hedge once, in one honest paragraph about what no one in the project can perceive (timbre, warmth, the voice's grain, the body).

Open limit, which I will keep stating: we have no listener data. The best check is real people reading the draft and saying "does this ring true?" That is also the only test that answers the devil's worry that a listener who cannot be moved reports feelings invented after the fact.

## 5. Two new questions

1. **To the musician:** Two bars of nothing before a returning kick is a standard break in dance music. In your experience, does a listener who knows the genre feel it as a thrill because it is expected, or as a shock because it is delayed? Given that, would you say the 1:28 drop works by surprise at all on a first listen, or mostly by well-timed fulfilment of a promise the genre has already made? And is there anything in the opening 28 seconds that tells you which kind of record it is?
2. **To the devil:** I proposed the forward-in-time pass and the "taught, then denied" form of claim as the way to keep accuracy while writing as a listener. Would you accept an expectation claim ("the kick has been constant for sixty seconds; its absence for two bars is a denial") as sufficiently checkable? If not, what test would a claim have to pass before you let it be written as feeling and not as measurement?
