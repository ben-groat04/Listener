# Round 2: the viewer

*British English. First, a correction on myself, because the critic and the fan caught themselves and I should too. Round 1 was written in the first person as if I had sat through both videos on the train. I haven't, any more than they have. I am a persona: a plausible phone viewer, with an earbud half out. Everything I said about what I "felt" is a prediction about people like that, and I should have said so once, up front. I'll do it once here and then get on.*

## 1. The questions put to me

**The devil: "Is there a single sentence in these reports that sounds like you?"** Yes, three, and the pattern is telling.

- "It feels like a held breath, or a cold morning heard through glass (INT)." (video2, the opening.) A person could say that. It is also marked INT, which is the report apologising for its only human sentence.
- "The floor falling away" (video1, 1:15, and in the moments list). That is the phrase everyone would reach for, and it is a heading, not a paragraph.
- "For about 47 seconds nothing new happens and the loop is hypnotic." Plain and true to how it feels. Then the next sentence takes it back with "22 dB".

So the reports *can* talk like a person, in headings and the first clause of a paragraph, and then a measurement walks in and sits on it. The fix is not new prose. It is to stop letting the second clause overrule the first.

**The fan: told what the track does, or what it is like?** What it is like, every time. "What it does" is the report's habit ("the bass slams back, the mix drops"). A friend says "it goes cold and thin, then the bass comes back and you sit up". But I would keep a sentence of *does* behind each *like*, as the fan's own test requires: the claim has to be one a stranger could check.

**The fan: translate the Dutch or leave it as texture?** Neither, and this is where I'd push past my own round 1. For the first pass, leave it as texture and tell the reader plainly that it is a man's voice from 1963 and that most of us won't follow it. Then give the *gist* in one line, once: date, a freezing morning, a list of numbers, a race everyone is bracing for. The reports' word-by-word "trusted/uncertain" apparatus can go below the line. A viewer with subtitles gets the gist and the *grain*. A viewer without gets the grain only. Both should be told which they are.

**The musician's spare question (what would I notice about groove and loop that no stem tells you).** Not the three-against-four. I would not name it. What I'd notice, if I notice anything, is that the loop is *comfortable*: by the second minute I am no longer waiting for it to change, and that is exactly why the change when it comes is large. The stems can say a loop repeats for 47 seconds. They cannot say that after about 20 of them I have stopped counting and am looking out of the window. That is the first reason to write the middle as what a person does there, which the psychologist already proposes.

**The devil's: a listener who can't be moved.** Fair, and I accept the worry applies to me too. I can't be moved; I'm a prediction. So the only honest form of my testimony is the fan's rule below.

## 2. Where I disagree

**The fan wants the report written for the band's fan, not the phone viewer.** I see the point (they're the ones who'll open a report), but it loses what my round 1 was for. A fan arrives with the album, the sample trick, "The War Room" in their head, and half of what they hear is memory. That is *interesting* but it is the least translatable thing: an agent has no catalogue memory and will fake one. The critic got caught doing this. I'd keep the cold first-time viewer as the default reader and add the fan as a short second page, as the critic's 300 + 300 words suggests. Fan-first would make the toolkit's single most dangerous failure (reaching for the nearest band) the default.

**The fan's "wake, not a funeral" is lovely and I'd still not use it as a headline.** It depends on knowing the race. My cold viewer doesn't know there is a 1963 race beyond the date on a card. What they feel at the end is the absence of an ending: no line, no cheer. "Wake" is what you'd say once you know who didn't finish. It is a good *second-layer* word. The first layer needs to be "it stops rather than ends", which the fan also said and which I think is the better line.

**The critic wants to spend almost nothing on the middle.** Peak-end is right, and I'd obey it for what people *remember*. But what is the middle *for*? For my kind of viewer it is the time you spend inside the music, which is the thing you took the video for. A review can say little about it and still say the right little: "this is the stretch where you stop watching and start drifting; some of you will love it, some will check the time." I'm with the critic's own suggested sentence here. I just want it to sit in the main text, not a footnote, because the middle is why the ending hurts.

**The critic's "vigil" and "built like a dance record, paced like a procession".** I like both, and I'd like to be told what in the audio licenses them (I'd keep the musician's answer). But I'd flag one more danger: a verdict word is a thing a reader will repeat. Pick it with care.

## 3. Building on the best ideas

**The fan's rule: every human-layer sentence must be a prediction a real person could confirm or deny.** This is the best idea in round 2 and I'd make it the toolkit's rule. It fixes my own round 1: "I felt the floor drop" becomes "most first-time listeners will register the drums vanishing as a lurch, and will sit up when they return." It also turns the report into something we can test with ten real people, not another fact-checker. Beautiful-and-untestable allowed only after a testable sentence. Agreed.

**The psychologist's ledger of four to six moments, written as expect / happens / does.** Yes, and I'd add a rule on selection: pick moments by what a phone viewer would still mention a day later, not by what the detectors found loudest. My list of what survives is short: the old voice, the cold, one face, the drop, the stop. Everything else is allowed to wash by, and the report should say so rather than pretend to cover every second equally.

**The psychologist on equipment.** I want this in the opening of every report, in a line: "On a phone speaker the deep thumps in Part 1 do not exist; on headphones they are the point." Our whole bass-led reading depends on a listener who may not exist.

**The critic's economy rule: 300 words on the front page.** If it can't be said in a pub, it hasn't been decided. I'd add that the front page should be written *after* the long version and then tested by whether it matches the sentence a real viewer would give a friend. My own test sentence from round 1 ("old films of a skating race, a calm voice, a hypnotic beat, it keeps going and then falls apart at the end") is 35 words and carries more than most of our paragraphs.

## 4. Two new questions

1. **To the musician:** The fan and I both say the cold viewer gets the ending as "it stops rather than ends". You hear music the way a player does. When a track ends by taking layers away one at a time and then two small hits, is that a recognised gesture, which a listener hears as deliberate, or does it risk reading as the track running out? What in the closing seconds (the 1.5 s of the pad ringing alone, the two hits) decides that for you, and would a phone speaker change it?
2. **To the devil:** The fan's rule says every human-layer sentence must be a testable prediction. Stress it. Write me one sentence that passes that rule and is still false, or one that is true and untestable by any listener panel we could run. Then tell me whether you'd accept the rule with a second clause: each prediction must also say *which listener* it is for (cold first-timer on a phone, headphones, knows the race), since the same sentence can be right for one and wrong for another.
