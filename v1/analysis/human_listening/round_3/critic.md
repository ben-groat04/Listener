# Round 3: the critic

*British English. I have read all five round 2 files, the fan's round 3, and I went back to the video 2 report to quote it properly. Final round, so I answer the questions put to me, argue with what I still think is wrong, and finish with the seven principles and the three habits to kill.*

## 0. One line of provenance, then no more

None of us has heard either track. I will not repeat that. A critic who writes from the stage plot and the setlist says so in the standfirst and then writes. The reports did the opposite: they announced it ("No one has listened to this audio with ears") and then spent three thousand words behaving as if the announcement were the work.

## 1. The questions put to me

### The devil: cut or sharpen your 1:28 paragraph; is "most first-time listeners will" a hedge or a voice?

The devil's paragraph is the best single piece of machine prose in the whole exercise. It reads:

> For about four seconds the floor goes. You have been walking to that kick for a minute, and the next step is not there. Then it comes back on exactly the beat you were waiting for, at full weight, with no fill to announce it, and the track locks. The film does nothing to help: the same empty, snowy road holds still for another couple of seconds. That refusal to cheer is what makes it strange rather than triumphant.

What I would do to it:

- **Keep sentences one to three untouched.** "The next step is not there" is exactly right: it is a person's sentence, and it is true to the stems.
- **"For about four seconds"** should become "for two bars", with the musician's correction in mind. Better still: "for a couple of bars". A player counts in bars, and so does anyone who has danced. Four seconds is a stopwatch.
- **Cut "the track locks".** That is the one engineer's verb. "It comes back on the one, at full weight, with no fill" already says it.
- **Cut the last sentence.** "That refusal to cheer" is the report's old habit of converting a null result into a motive. The musician has withdrawn it; the viewer says restraint; I agree. Replace it with the only thing a listener can actually have: *"The picture doesn't help. It sits on the same white road."* Then stop.

As for "most first-time listeners will": on its own it reads as a hedge, the voice of a survey. It becomes a voice if it is used once, at the top of a paragraph, as the narrator's licence, and then dropped for plain "you". "A first-time listener will lose the floor here. You have been walking to that kick for a minute..." That is how a critic uses "you": a shorthand for the reader, announced once. Repeating "most listeners will" in every sentence is what turns a review into an opinion poll, and I said that in round 2.

Which verdict would I let a machine own? **"A march that slowly stops"** is fine: it is a description of structure with a gait in it, and it is true to the stems. What I would not let it own is anything about quality ("the best thing they have done", "a masterpiece of restraint"). A critic's quality verdict is a bet made with a lifetime of other records behind it, and our agents have no records behind them, only the nearest band in the training set. A machine may say what a thing is like and who it is for. It should be much slower to say that it is good.

### The musician: is "built like a dance record, paced like a procession" fair? Give two comparisons for Part 2 and one for Part 1 with the stem feature that licenses each.

The musician asked for rudeness, and the stems the musician checked (dotted-eighth bass over a straight kick) earn it a fair answer. I do not trust myself to name records, so I propose to name *forms*, which a player can check, and let an editor add a record if they like:

- **Part 2, form one: "a walking-pace dance track with the dance taken out."** Licence: four-to-the-floor kick at 120, a rolling bass, and an arrangement that spends its time taking layers *off* rather than adding energy. A player would not wince: it describes what the pulse is and what the arrangement refuses to do.
- **Part 2, form two: "a procession with a drum machine at the front."** Licence: constant pulse, no key change, no fill, a slow human voice over the top, and an ending that subtracts people. A player might say "a procession is slower", which is fair, and is why I would prefer "walking pace" to "march".
- **Part 1, form: I withdraw.** I have no stem fact for Part 1 as strong as the bass and kick for Part 2, and my round 1 "Reich on a skating rink" came from nowhere. The musician's "post-rock" label is a prediction about what the guitars are doing, and the devil is right that the report itself said that how it was made cannot be recovered. If forced, "a riff that is hard and empty, neither glad nor sad" is checkable against the stems (the missing third), and it needs no band's name. That is the safe pattern: **a comparison is licensed by a named feature of the stems, or it is not used.**

The general rule I would give the toolkit: *a comparison to another record is allowed once, only if the report can name the feature of this track that makes it; otherwise compare the track to a gait, a room, or a weather.* The musician may call "paced like a procession" imprecise. I will accept "walking pace".

### The psychologist: what does a first-timer retain of the middle, and what is the one honest thing a review can say about it?

The psychologist did not answer this in round 2 (they answered others), so I will state what I am willing to stand on. A review can say three things about the long middle of Part 2, in order, in about forty words: *you stop counting; it gets harder to resist without ever getting faster; some of you will love it and some will check the time.* The first two are structural (a loop that does not change, a layer that opens up). The third is the only honest thing, and the viewer and the fan both made it. That is a sentence a reader can test against their own afternoon.

What the critic does not do is pretend the middle is a *section* with a begin and an end, as the report does ("the trance and the plateau (2:48.8 to 4:36.8)"). Nobody lives in a minute-and-a-half the way a stopwatch reads it. The psychologist's hindsight point stands and I accept it fully.

## 2. Where I disagree

**With the psychologist's six-part shape.** It is a good brief for a *panel*; it is not a review. Six sections (gist, a ledger of four to six moments, the middle, the ending, who is listening, evidence) is the lab sheet re-ordered. A review is one continuous piece with a spine. I would give the toolkit the *content* of the shape and the *form* of a short essay: an opening paragraph that makes the verdict, two paragraphs that carry the two moments and the ending, one that names who will love it and who will not, and a boxed appendix. The ledger belongs in the notes, not the page.

**With the devil's three registers (heard, likely felt, our take).** As a back-office structure, yes: it is exactly how a fact-checker should read the draft. As a *layout* of the published piece, no. A reader must not be shown the seams, and a body that stamps each sentence with its register is the INT/OBS tagging with nicer names. The registers belong in the sidecar, one sentence to one row, and the prose is allowed to run across them. I accept the devil's point that a fact-checker must read *register one against the sidecar and nothing else*, because lovely prose hides errors. That is a rule about how we check, not how we write.

**With the devil's insistence that "most" is dangerous and "a number is a made-up statistic".** I agree about numbers. I would go further: the review should seldom say "most" at all. A critic says "you". It is a licence that readers grant, and the readers who disagree write letters.

**With the fan, on the "one image per section" rule.** The fan wants it stricter than mine: one image per section, not per moment. I would accept that as a ceiling for *this* toolkit, on the evidence of our own panel, who produced "wake", "coat off in a warm room", "vigil" and "absence where the winner should be" within a few pages. But I am telling the fan that the rule is a guard against an agent's tics, not a law of good writing. Good critics have several images per paragraph when the music supports them. It is the machine that has not earned them.

**With the viewer, on "wake" as a second-layer word.** I accept. I came to this panel pleased with "vigil", and I now think the *absence where the winner should be* does the headline job and "vigil" or "wake" belongs one level down, for the reader who knows the race. Where I do not move: a review needs a one-word *name* for the piece somewhere, and "vigil" is the only one on the table that tells you how long to expect to wait.

**With the fan's final question, "is the problem that the reports are wrong, or that they are in the wrong voice?"** It is a good question and the answer is: neither, quite. The reports are mostly *accurate* and in the *right register for a different job*. The user's complaint ("this is absolutely not how a human thinks and listens about music") is about **the job**. A report that was built to prove what was heard is a defence brief. A person who wants to know what a record is like wants a verdict and a story. We wrote the first and were asked for the second.

## 3. Lines from our reports that ring false, and what a listener would say

I quote directly. These are the real sentences, in the report's own voice.

| The report says | What is wrong with it as listening | What a person would say |
|---|---|---|
| "Its force comes from the gap before it and the lack of any warning (INT); it is the biggest single event in the piece." | Everyone knows it came with warning: the drums thin out first, as the musician showed. And "the biggest single event" is a ranking made by a loudness curve, not an ear. | "It thins out, goes quiet, and then the kick comes back. That is the moment." |
| "The drums about 50 dB louder." | An artefact of measuring from near-silence. No one hears 50 dB; they hear *back*. | "The kick comes back on the one, with nothing to announce it." |
| "That gives a steady foot-tap with a slight limp (INT). It also fools an automatic beat tracker, which reports 161 BPM instead of 120." | The tracker is not the listener. The limp is the best phrase in the paragraph; the tracker sentence ends it. | "It rolls rather than marches." |
| "One layer brightens by roughly 22 dB in its top frequencies (about 3 dB in the full mix), fastest between about 3:26 and 3:50." | A measurement, followed by a measurement of the same measurement. | "Something up top keeps opening, a bit at a time, until you realise you are leaning forward." (Prediction, to be tagged.) |
| "It feels like a held breath, or a cold morning heard through glass (INT)." | The best line in the report, and it is *apologised for* with a tag. | The same sentence, without the tag. |
| "Whether you feel grief, pride or a quiet leaving depends on what you bring." | A shrug dressed as a conclusion. | "If you know that 127 people finished out of nearly ten thousand, it is a wake. If you do not, it is a cold march that stops before you are ready." |
| "The best-supported word is withdrawal; 'mourning' is weaker (INT)." | A ranking of adjectives by *evidence*, as if a mood were a hypothesis. | "It stops rather than ends." |
| "36% of the 86 cuts fall within 80 ms of a beat, against 32% expected by chance... p about 0.09." | The honest result is: the cuts and the beat have nothing to say to each other. That is a sentence, not a paragraph of tests. | "The pictures do their own thing." |
| "The same seven notes (B C# D E F# G A) are used throughout." | No listener has ever been moved by a pitch-class set. It is true and it belongs in the appendix. | "It stays in one sad-ish place and never leaves." |

The pattern in the right-hand column is the finding. *Every* correct human sentence is shorter than the report's, and the report already contains the seed of most of them (the held breath, the limp, the withdrawal). The report did the hard part and then buried it.

## 4. What I take from the others in the final round

- **The musician's correction of the arithmetic and the dotted-eighth finding.** It is the first time anyone on the panel *listened harder*, through the stems, and came back with something that makes the record more audible: "a dotted-eighth bass over a straight kick". It proves the machinery was worth building. It also proves that the machinery's output was poorly translated: "grouped in threes" was a gift, the report wrapped it in a beat-tracker aside, and the musician had to unwrap it. The toolkit should have a step whose whole job is to translate a measurement into the nearest *thing a player says*.
- **The musician's player test.** Give three working musicians the draft and ask which sentences they would not have said. Cheap, honest and real, and the nearest thing we have to the user's own ear.
- **The psychologist's hindsight finding.** This is the best structural diagnosis of the lot. Our agents knew at 0:30 what happens at 1:28. A listener does not. A report written from the end *cannot* contain anticipation, which is half of what music is. The forward-in-time pass is the right fix and I would make it compulsory.
- **The devil's calibration point, and the fan's final question.** The user is the only ear in this room. One sentence from them on what they remember after Part 2, and one on what was wrong with the report, is worth more than another round.
- **The viewer's equipment line.** "On a phone speaker the deep thumps in Part 1 do not exist." It should open every report. It is one sentence, it is plainly true, and it is the commonest reason a real listener and our report would disagree.
- **The fan's best line this round: "we could not hear the weight of the kick; a listener will."** It is the most honest sentence in the project, and the only one that makes a reader trust the rest.

## 5. What a review of Part 2 would do, in 260 words

Since I have spent two rounds telling the panel what a review is, here is one, with the licence for each claim in square brackets for the sidecar, not for the page. It is deliberately unsure of its own verdicts, because I cannot hear it.

> **A cold walk that stops before the end.**
>
> *Elfstedentocht Part 2* is a drum machine walking across a frozen country while a dead man reads out the numbers. Public Service Broadcasting have always made music out of the archive; here the archive is the lead singer. [archive voice from 0:42; no sung vocal] For half a minute there is only a dark pad and a few low knocks, then a pulse walks in, at about the pace you would walk to a station, and an old Dutch voice begins on the date. Most of us will not follow the words. We follow the calm. [120 BPM; Dutch narration]
>
> The moment you will remember is a minute and a half in. The drums thin, the bass goes, and for a couple of bars you are left with the pad and the voice. Then the kick returns on the one, plain and heavy, with no fill. You have been walking to that kick for a minute and the next step was not there. [drums out 1:24.3, back 1:28.6, no fill; musician's correction: two bars, with thinning before]
>
> After that the track does not change its mind. It rolls rather than marches, and a layer up top opens bit by bit until you notice you are leaning forward. This is where listeners split: some of you will love the loop, and some will check the time. [bass in three-sixteenth groups across the kick; filter-like opening 3:26 to 3:50]
>
> Then it takes its clothes off one layer at a time. Two knocks. Silence. If you know that 127 people finished out of nearly ten thousand, it is a wake; if you do not, it is a cold march that stops before you are ready. [layer-by-layer ending; two hits 5:02.33, 5:02.58]
>
> *For: anyone who likes to be carried. Not for: anyone who needs something to happen.*

I would be embarrassed, still, by "the moment you will remember". That is the one clause that is pure bet. I would leave it in, because a review without one bet is a catalogue.

## 6. The seven principles for writing about music the way a human experiences it

1. **Begin with what it is like, in the shortest true sentence.** "A cold walk that stops before the end." If a sentence cannot be said in a pub, it has not been decided. Everything else on the page is there to earn the first line, not to qualify it.
2. **Measurement licenses the sentence and never appears in it.** No decibels, no beats per minute, no window sizes, no p-values in the body. They go in a sidecar, one row to a claim, so a fact-checker can still read register one against the data. The number is the alibi, not the evidence the reader asked for.
3. **Write forward in time.** Each moment is written by someone who does not yet know what comes next: what you have been taught to expect, what happens, what it does. Anticipation is half of music and a report written from the end cannot hold it. A drop is not "the biggest event"; it is a step that was not there.
4. **Spend the words on the peak and the end, give the middle one honest sentence, and name where listeners split.** People keep the best moment and the last one. The middle gets "you stop counting", and a line on who loves it and who checks the time.
5. **Translate every measurement into the nearest thing a player says.** "Grouped in threes against the kick" becomes "it rolls". "A filter-like opening" becomes "something up top keeps opening". A dedicated step should do this, and a working musician should be able to test it. "Sounds like" is licensed; "is" is not, without stems.
6. **Compare only when you can name the feature that licenses it; otherwise use a gait, a room, a weather.** One image per section, after the plain sentence. Never reach for the nearest band: it is the machine's commonest lie, and it costs us the reader's trust the moment they know the band.
7. **Commit, tag the listener, and say once what you could not hear.** Say "you" and mean a named kind of listener (first-timer on a phone, someone who knows the race). Give a lean, not a score; give who it is for and who it is not for. Own a bet and let it be wrong. Then one plain paragraph: we could not hear the weight of the kick, the grain of the voice, or whether any of it moved us. Then stop apologising.

## 7. The three worst habits of our current reports to kill

1. **Letting the measurement overrule the feeling in the next clause.** The reports often start with a human sentence (the held breath, the limp, withdrawal) and then a number walks in and sits on it. "It feels like a held breath (INT)" is followed by a list of thuds at 0:00, 0:08, 0:12. Kill the second clause; keep the first, untagged.
2. **The neutral, hedge-everything register that no editor would print.** OBS/INT stamps, "best-supported word", "genuinely open", "depends on what you bring". It does not say what the music is like, who it is for, or whether it works. A review ranks, recommends, places and risks. The reports do none of the four. We have mistaken the absence of a verdict for rigour.
3. **Treating the whole piece as a database of events written with hindsight.** A timestamped inventory with sections for each stretch ("the trance and the plateau, 2:48.8 to 4:36.8") written by an author who has already heard the ending. It is the opposite of listening: no anticipation, no surprise, no middle you drift in, no end you did not see coming. Kill the inventory as the spine; write the piece as someone who is walking through it for the first time and tell the reader which three things they will remember.
