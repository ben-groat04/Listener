# Round 3: the devil's advocate

British English. I have read all five round 2 files. I also went back to `v1/analysis/music/video2/music_reading.md` to check something the psychologist's question made me suspicious of. It cost me my own round 2 paragraph. That is the first answer.

## 1. The questions put to me

### Psychologist: is "the kick was constant for sixty seconds; its absence for two bars is a denial" checkable enough?

It is checkable in form and, as written, false in fact. I got it wrong in round 2 too, and so did the musician, the fan and the psychologist. Look at what the reading says about 0:28 to 1:24 (section "The early groove"): "sparser and different... no four-on-the-floor yet". The drums are intermittent: silent 1:01 to 1:03, near-silent 1:10 to 1:15, scattered hits at 1:16, 1:19, 1:20. The steady kick that "has been under your feet" begins at 1:28.6, the first time it ever plays. It does not return. It arrives.

So these sentences are all wrong:

- Mine, round 2: "You have been walking to that kick for a minute, and the next step is not there. Then it comes back on exactly the beat you were waiting for."
- The musician: "the kick comes back on the one... It lands because you were waiting for it."
- The psychologist: "after sixty seconds of a steady kick, eight beats of nothing, then the kick returns".
- The fan and viewer, by echo: "the beat comes back".

Five confident, musical, mechanism-bearing sentences, all built on a "denied prediction", and the thing that was supposedly denied was never established. What the listener has actually been taught for a minute is a sparse, limping, half-there pulse and a bass that plays one long note per four seconds. The gap takes away those two things, and then something bigger than anything before it arrives: a new, full, regular groove. That changes the mechanism. It is less "your floor is pulled away" and more "the floor you were promised finally exists". The sentence that survives the stems is something like: *for a minute the beat has only half-turned up, stumbling in and out. Then it stops altogether for four seconds while the old man names the race, and when the music returns it is a different thing: a plain, full, four-square kick, on every beat, that never lets go.* Strictly, the release is of withheld arrival, not of restored expectation. A listener who knows dance music will feel it as the drop they were half-waiting for; that is a better version of the psychologist's genre question than the one they asked.

To answer the question directly: **an expectation claim is checkable only if the expectation is itself checked.** The "taught" half needs its own measurement, not an assumption. My test, before a claim may be written as a feeling:

1. **Name the taught thing and prove it was taught.** How long, how regular, how loud, in the stems. ("Kick on every beat for 60 s" fails; "sparse pulse, gaps up to 5 s" passes.)
2. **Name the denial or payoff, with its time.**
3. **Name the mechanism from the whitelist.** (The psychologist's list has one; I asked for it.)
4. **Say for which listener.** The first-timer sees (1) as a vague sense of walking. The genre-literate listener sees it as "a build with no drop yet".

That is a four-line sidecar entry, and it would have caught five writers. It is also an uncomfortable result for everyone who said the audit voice was the problem: the *audit* is what found the truth here and the *human voices* are what forgot it. I still think the audit voice must go from the page. The audit must not go from the process.

### Viewer: write one sentence that passes the testable-prediction rule and is still false; one true and untestable; accept the "which listener" clause?

False and passes the rule: "Most first-time listeners will feel the floor drop away at 1:24." It is a prediction, a panel could test it, and I think it is probably false. At 1:24.3 the drums go from a sparse pattern to nothing, bass follows at 1:25, and the mix drops only 2 to 3 dB. The narrator is mid-sentence. A cold listener on a phone, following the voice, may not feel a floor go at all; they may simply notice that the music has thinned under the talking. The felt event is the arrival at 1:28.6, not the loss before it. So the rule lets a plausible, testable, wrong sentence through, and what it needs is the sidecar above. Testable is not the same as tested.

True and untestable by any panel we could run: "The voice works on a listener who does not speak Dutch as the sound of officialdom and age, and not as information." Probably true, but to test it you need people who cannot parse Dutch, and who also report what they are *not* attending to, which people are bad at. This is the class of claim where the fan's rule runs out: attention, absorption and mind-wandering are mostly not available to introspection. So I would add a second category for sentences that are plausible but cannot be tested: **write them, once, in the take, owned.**

Yes to the second clause. Every prediction names its listener: cold first-timer on a phone; headphones; knows the band; knows the race; knows dance music. And I would go one further: a sentence true for only one of those is allowed and useful, provided it is labelled. The real error is writing "you" as though there were one.

## 2. What I take from round 2, and where I argue

**Musician: the micro-timing gap between bass and kick is the best new idea on the table.** It is a *measurement that already is a human feeling*: whether the groove pushes or drags. It is checkable, it needs no mechanism, and it speaks the player's language. I would run it before writing another adjective. The musician also said the cross-rhythm "rolls and never sits down". Keep that; it matches the reading ("steady foot-tap with a slight limp") and says it in half the words. The "bar and three-quarters" in the musician's round 2 is defensible, not wrong: bass and drums both absent is 1:25.0 to 1:28.6, about 3.6 s, which is 1.8 bars. I withdraw my catch from round 2 on that sentence (my "four bars" objection was to round 1 and still stands). But I note the musician's instruction to "put on the record ten seconds before 1:28 so they have the groove in their feet" would make a listener hear the *sparse* pattern, not the groove in their feet. The groove they are about to be given is a different one.

**Musician: the player test.** Adopt. Give three players the stems and the draft; ask which sentences they would not have said. It is cheap, and it would also have flagged the kick sentence, because a player listening to 1:00 to 1:24 in isolation hears that there is no steady kick.

**Critic: a ceiling of one image per moment, after the plain sentence; a 300-word front page; "intelligent stranger" as the reader.** All adopted. The ceiling is the most important single rule for the prose, because a beautiful sentence is the best hiding place for an error. The "coat coming off in a warm room" cannot be wrong, which is exactly why it must not carry weight. The critic's four review moves (rank, recommend, place, risk) are what our reports lack. I accept all four with one proviso: *place* is the most dangerous, because it is where the invented choir lives. A placement may use only what the stems or the context file support ("built like a dance record, paced like a procession"). A named band is an association, once, labelled.

**Critic on the p-value, and my own reply to my round 2.** I defended the p-values in the margin. I stand by it, but I would now go further: **there is no reason to ever write "p about 0.09" in front of a reader.** The finding is "the cuts do not follow the beat". The margin keeps the number. The viewer is right that the tracker, the 161 BPM, and "within 80 ms" are not ever a listener's concern.

**Fan: "the voice is a real broadcaster, the pictures are the same archive."** Adopt, because it is a fact that changes how the music is heard, and the reports bury it. And "a wake, not a funeral": the viewer's objection stands, it is a second-layer word that needs the race. Use it in the informed reading, not the headline. I would say the same for my own favourite from round 1, "it stops rather than ends": that is the first-layer line.

**Psychologist: the whitelist.** Still the piece I most need and have not seen in this form. The three listener types plus the "taught, then denied" form are useful. I would ask them to commit to the whitelist in round 3 so the sidecar has something to cite. Also: "most listeners" is allowed only when the writer is willing to name the listener type it is *not* true for.

**Viewer: "the voice is music, not information."** Correct, and it should be in the report once, plainly. It also lets us drop most of the Dutch transcription from the felt layer. It stays in the informed layer, where it changes the ending.

**Viewer: the report's only human sentences are marked INT and then overruled by a number.** The best diagnosis of the *surface* in any of the files, and a gift: it means the fix is smaller than we thought. The human sentences already exist. The audit has to stop speaking in the same paragraph.

## 3. What would be lost, and what to keep

What is lost by going human, now with evidence rather than a worry:

1. **The facts the audit found.** It has already caught the tempo octave (161 against 120), the invented bass exit, the "calm" second half. In round 2, and again above, it was the audit that caught an error all five human-voiced panellists made. If the human voice is written without the sidecar, we reproduce those errors in better prose.
2. **Honesty about what an AI cannot perceive.** One paragraph says it. The body does not repeat it.
3. **A reason to disagree.** The old reports let a reader see why a claim was made. The sidecar keeps that, but it must be *linked* from each sentence or it will rot.

What to keep, with the audit voice gone:

- **Every sentence in the body is anchored in the sidecar** (id, timestamp, the measurement, the taught/denied/payoff trio where there is an expectation claim, the mechanism, the listener type).
- **A fact-checker whose only job is the plain sentences**: "does the sidecar say this, and is the number right?" Not the prose.
- **A second reader: the cold read by someone who has not seen the sidecar**, asked "which sentence would you cross out because it sounds invented?" That catches the mismatch of tone that a data check cannot.
- **Human calibration**, still the only ground truth. One note from the user per video: the moment they would name, and the feeling. Score us against it in the repo.

2. **To the psychologist:** given that the "denied prediction" turned out to be a misreading in five reports, which of the whitelisted mechanisms is least vulnerable to the writer misdescribing the stimulus, and which would you forbid us to cite without a measurement of what was taught?

## 5. My seven principles for writing about music the way a human experiences it

1. **Start from what a listener does, not what a machine measures.** Tap along, lean in, lose the thread, notice the voice. The measurement is the reason a sentence is true; it is almost never the sentence.
2. **Write the shape of the thing over time: where it starts, where it turns, where it leaves you.** Spend the words on the moment that changes and on the ending, and give the long middle a sentence.
3. **An expectation is a claim about the past, so check the past.** Before writing "the beat returns" or "the floor drops", prove from the stems what was actually taught. (We failed this five times at 1:24.)
4. **Say whose ear it is.** Cold first-timer on a phone, headphones, knows the band, knows the race, speaks Dutch. One sentence, one listener, with "most" or "some" and never a made-up percentage.
5. **Plain sentence first, then one image, never two.** Pictures are allowed only after a checkable statement, and a picture that could not be wrong must not carry weight.
6. **Commit to a verdict, a lean and a placement, and own them as one writer's take.** Be wrong in the one place where it is cheap and visible; keep the rest of the page plain.
7. **Keep the evidence behind the page, not in it.** Every sentence has an id in a sidecar with its timestamp, measurement, mechanism and confidence, and a reader can open it; the sentence itself carries no decibels, trackers or p-values.

## 6. The three worst habits to kill

1. **The apology stamp.** "(INT)" after the only sentence a person could say, and "no one has listened to this audio with ears" at the head of every section. Say it once, in one paragraph, and then write. The viewer's catch is exact: the report puts its one human line in brackets and its decibels in the main clause.
2. **The number that overrules the feeling.** "Something like a held breath... 22 dB", "about 50 dB louder", "fools the beat tracker to 161", "p about 0.09". A figure in the body is the audit speaking over the listener. It also overstates (50 dB is the distance from near-silence to full level, not what anyone hears).
3. **Describing events, not experience, without checking what came before them.** The reports list what happens at each timestamp in the order the machine found it, and then, when human voices tried to repair that, they described a payoff for an expectation nobody had measured. Both fail in the same way: they never ask what the listener has been led to expect by that point, and they never check it.
