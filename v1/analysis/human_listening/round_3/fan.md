# Human listening, round 3: the fan

British English. I have read all five other round 2 files. As in round 2: I have not heard either track, and nor has anyone here. I am the fan as a persona, and everything below that sounds like memory is prediction about people like me. I say it once, here, and then get on.

## 1. The question put to me: is a dotted-eighth bass over four-on-the-floor a PSB signature? Is the kick big and round or small and tight?

The musician asked this, and the honest answer is that I can only half give it.

- **The bass figure.** I would not call it a signature. A rolling bass against a straight kick is how half of the electronic-adjacent world gets a groove, so played the bass stem alone I would say "that could be anyone". What *is* theirs, as far as a fan can tell, is not a part but a habit: a clean, big, modern machine playing underneath a dry, plain, old voice, and a long build that ends by leaving you with something small. That habit is in the reports as facts (the layer order, the voice at 0:42, the thin ending) and is never named as the band's habit. Naming it is exactly what a fan adds.
- **The kick.** I cannot tell you if it is big and round or small and tight. Nobody on this panel can. The musician is right that this is the thing the reports cannot say, and it is the clearest example of what we should stop pretending about. One line in the report: "we could not hear the weight of the kick; a listener will." That is worth more than a confident adjective.

## 2. Where I disagree, and where I now disagree with myself

**With the critic, on who the default reader is.** The critic, viewer and musician all say: the intelligent stranger, with a fan paragraph second. I argued for the fan in round 2. I have moved, partly. The viewer's point beat me: the toolkit is video-agnostic, and a fan-first report invites an agent to fake catalogue memory, which is the most dangerous failure we have. So: stranger first, fan as a short second page. But I hold one line. The stranger should be told *within the first three sentences* what a fan would already know (the band's trick: the archive is the instrument). Otherwise the report treats the best thing about the record as a footnote.

**With the critic, on the "one image per moment" ceiling.** Agree on the ceiling, and I want it stricter. One image per *report section*, and it must come after a plain sentence. I counted my own round 2: "wake, not a funeral", "a coat coming off", "a march that slowly stops". Three images for one ending. Pick one.

**With the musician, who says in a build record the plateau is the point.** Yes, for the person who likes build records. My round 1 line "skip the plateau" was a taste claim, and the devil is right that it contradicts the viewer and critic and should not be resolved silently. The honest version is the devil's: *this is where listeners split, and here is who goes which way.* People who love long loops: this is the best bit. People who need something to happen: this is where you check the time. Say both, once, without picking.

**With the psychologist, on "not elegy".** Half my round 2 stands. For a cold listener, arousal and a wistful emptiness; I accept it. But the psychologist's own fix, three real listener types with a sentence each, is better than either of us. I would simply add that the *fan* type's sentence is the one the reports most lack, because it is about expectation of the band, not of the genre: "you expect them to leave the old voice alone and let the machine do the feeling, and here it goes quiet so that he can be heard."

## 3. What I now think the panel got right, and what I steal

- **The devil's mechanism test.** For any "feels like" sentence: can you point to the thing in the signal *and* the named mechanism linking it to the feeling? That is the best honesty rule here, and it lets us drop "(INT)" stamps. It also deals with my own round 2 rule better than my rule did.
- **The musician's "it takes its clothes off one layer at a time and then stops".** Plain, checkable, sounds like talk.
- **The viewer's "an absence where the winner should be".** Still the best line in six files. For the ending, use it; drop my "wake" from the headline and keep it as a second-layer word for people who know the race, as the viewer says.
- **The critic's "measurement licenses the sentence, it does not appear in it."** That is the whole job in one line.
- **The critic's 300 + 300 words.** Anything longer and a mate has left the pub.
- **The musician's player test**: give three working musicians the draft and ask which sentences they would not have said. Cheap and real.

## 4. A correction to my own round 2 rule

My rule was: every human-layer sentence must be a prediction a person could confirm. The critic says it would produce survey results; the viewer and devil say it needs a "for which listener" clause. Both right. Revised: **each moment gets one plain, testable, listener-tagged sentence ("a first-time listener on a phone will notice the drums vanish and sit up when they return"), then at most one image.** If a sentence can be neither tested nor tagged, it is a flourish and there is a budget for flourishes.

I also cannot accept the devil's strict ban on any "reminds me of" from a fan. Allowed once, labelled as the writer's association, and only if it comes from something checkable (the band's own words in `context.md`, tags). Everyone's reflexive comparisons in round 1 ("Reich on a skating rink", "Mogwai", "Boards of Canada") were the machine reaching for the nearest band, and I include mine.

## 5. Lines from our reports that ring false, with what a mate would say

These are the standing offenders (from the round 2 tables and the reports' habits):

- **"Brightens by 22 dB."** A mate says: "it opens up gradually, like a filter being turned." Nobody hears 22 dB.
- **"It comes with no warning."** Wrong on the stems (the drums thin out before). A mate says: "it thins out, goes quiet, then slams back."
- **"Withdrawal, not arrival."** The one phrase the committee got right, and it is still a committee phrase. A mate says: "it just stops, like someone leaving the room."
- **"Depends on what you bring."** A shrug. Say what two or three real people would bring.
- **"Composer is in effect the mixer."** Interesting to us, meaningless to anyone who has listened to a record.
- **"The picture ignores the drop."** Do we know that? Softened in round 2 to "the edit does not punch the drop", which is the true statement.

## 6. Two new questions (final round, so they go to the orchestrator and the user)

1. **To whoever runs the next round:** the one real human in this conversation is the user. Could we ask them for *one* real data point: a single sentence about what they remember after one listen of Part 2? Everything else we have written is prediction, and one real sentence would let us score it.
2. **To the user:** is the problem that the reports are wrong, or that they are written in the wrong *voice* even when they are right? The panel has assumed the second. If it is the first, our fixes are cosmetic.

## 7. Seven principles for writing about music the way a human experiences it

1. **Say what it is like before what it does.** "Goes cold and thin, then the bass comes back and you sit up" comes first; the signal that licenses it goes in a note.
2. **Measurement licenses the sentence; it does not appear in it.** No dB, no BPM, no window sizes in the body. Numbers live in the sidecar.
3. **Spend your words on the peak and the end.** People keep the best moment and the last one. Give the middle a sentence, and one honest line about where listeners split.
4. **Tag the listener.** Every feeling sentence says who it is for (cold first-timer, fan, knows the race, Dutch speaker) and how many ("most", "some"), never a number invented for the occasion.
5. **Never say "I felt".** Say "you'd hear" or "most people will". For each feeling, the panel's mechanism test: a thing in the signal plus a named mechanism, or cut it.
6. **Plain sentence first, then at most one image, then stop.** Place the music against other music once, labelled as an association, never as hearing it.
7. **Be short and commit.** About 300 words up front (verdict, one moment, the ending, what it is like, who it is for), then 300 for the listener types, then the evidence. A lean, not a score. Say plainly what we could not hear (the weight of the kick, the real feel of the voice) rather than dressing it up.

## 8. The three worst habits of our current reports to kill

1. **Citing a measurement as the feeling.** "22 dB", "161 BPM", "36% within 80 ms" presented as if they were what a listener has. Nobody has these. They are how we checked, and they belong in a sidecar.
2. **Flat, hedge-everything neutrality.** Every claim stamped OBS/INT/SPEC and then hung with "depends on what you bring". It is the one register no editor would print and no friend would use. Commit to a reading, give it a plain confidence word ("probably"), and be allowed to be wrong.
3. **Telling the listener what they felt, in a made-up voice that pretends it heard.** Either claiming testimony we do not have or inventing a fan's memory and a comparison from the nearest band. Replace with labelled prediction, and a short plain admission of what the machine cannot hear.
