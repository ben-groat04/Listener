# Round 3: the musician

British English, plain speech. I have read all six round 2 files and the fan's and psychologist's round 3. Same caveat as always: nobody here has heard these tracks. "You would hear" means a player's prediction from the stems and the reports' facts, not memory.

## 0. What I owe from round 2

### The critic: are "built like a dance record, paced like a procession" and "vigil" fair? Give me safe comparisons.

"Built like a dance record" is fair on the stems: four-on-the-floor kick at 120, a bass figure rolling across it, a break and a slam on the one. A player would not wince. "Paced like a procession" is half right. A procession is slow and the track is not; 120 is a brisk walk, the tempo of marching bands and of half of all house records. I would say "a march that is also a dance record", which is the actual tension. "Vigil" is a mood word with no licence in the stems, so I would drop it.

Rule for a safe comparison: it must name a **device**, not a band. The licence is the thing the stems actually show.

- **Part 2, one:** "the build-and-break structure of a club record": layers arrive, one layer slowly opens up over a minute, everything cuts out for two bars, the kick returns on the one. Licence: the break and the return are in the stems; the genre expects them.
- **Part 2, two:** "a pulse that never changes under a voice that is free", like a metronome under a speaker giving a talk. Licence: constant kick for 90 seconds under a narrator who is not in time with it.
- **Part 1, one:** "a guitar riff played like a loop", the riff repeated as machine-like as a sample, with a drum kit on top. Licence: the report's own finding that the riff repeats without variation. I will not go further on Part 1 as I have said little about it, and I would not invent it.

Not safe: "Reich", "Mogwai", "Boards of Canada". The fan is right that these were reflexes. A safe comparison survives the question "which stem says so?" If the answer is "none, it just sounds like them", cut it.

### The viewer: does "layers off one at a time, then two hits" read as deliberate, or as running out? What decides it, and would a phone change it?

Deliberate. Players know this gesture as a **stop**, and a stop is the opposite of a fade. A fade says the record ran out; a stop says someone decided. What decides it, in order:

1. **Layers leave in a sequence, not all at once.** Accident does not take things away in order. The ear hears an arrangement.
2. **The groove stops dead**, then the pad rings alone for about a second and a half. That is a held rest, the "all stop, hold it" you do on the last bar of a gig. A pad ringing in silence is the same as the room's air being heard.
3. **Two drum hits, quarter of a second apart, at the same loudness as the groove.** Two hits is a tag. It is the "dum-dum" a band plays to say: finished. A listener who has never played hears "that was the end", firmly. Short, loud, final, and with no tail: that is what a punctuation mark sounds like.

A phone speaker would lose the bottom of the pad but keep the stab of the two hits, so the ending is, if anything, **safer on a phone** than the drop. The drop needs a chest; the tag needs only an ear. That is worth a sentence in the report, and it is better evidence for "it stops, it does not end" than anything about harmony.

My round 2 line about "an unresolved bar, no tonic" is the structural reason for the unease. I keep it, but I would not claim a listener consciously hears it. The stop is deliberate; the unease is the music never having resolved anywhere, so there was nothing to land on. A player knows that. A listener just feels "it stopped, and I am not satisfied".

### The fan: is the dotted-eighth bass a PSB signature? Is the kick round or tight?

I cannot answer either and I will not pretend. The first I leave to the fan: I only know the generic fact that dotted-eighth figures over a straight kick are common everywhere. The second is the biggest hole in the whole project: nobody has said what the kick sounds like (round and boomy or tight and clicky), and **that is the first thing a player would say about the record**. I would rather the report said "we could not hear the kick's character" in one line than ignore it. It is also not unmeasurable: attack time and the spectral centroid of the kick hit are in the drums stem, and a decent descriptor could come out as "short and dry" or "long and soft". That is a measurement worth taking, because it turns into a word a person uses.

## 1. Where I disagree, now that I have read everyone

**The fan, round 3: "no dB, no BPM, no window sizes in the body".** Agreed for dB and windows. Not for BPM. "120" is not a measurement a listener counts but a **fact about the record** that shapes the feel (walking pace, march, dance record). Put it in once and say what it means. "A steady walking pace" alone loses the information that tells a dancer or a runner exactly what this is. So: no figure for its own sake, one for its meaning.

**The fan: "never say 'I felt'".** Yes. But also never say "you feel" as a command. Say what the playing does and what the likely reaction is.

**The critic and the psychologist, "spend your words on the peak and the end".** Correct, with one amendment from the bench. In a build record the middle is not filler. The 47 seconds of loop and the slow opening of the top layer are the **engine** of the end. The ear is being loaded. The report should give the middle one sentence that says that: "nothing happens on purpose, and the track is getting heavier". Not two. The fan's "check your phone" line is the honest second reading, and it can be the one line of taste.

**The devil, "sounds like is allowed, is a is not".** Right, and I conceded as much in round 2. I would go further: for the late layer, which we cannot identify, **say what it sounds like and say that it is a guess**. "A synth-ish top layer that keeps opening up, with vocal-like scraps on the bar line, we cannot tell if they are chopped archive speech". That is what a player says about a mix they cannot solo. It is not a hedge; it is exactly the sentence a producer would say with the stems in front of them.

**The viewer: "the loop is comfortable; by the second minute I stop waiting".** I accept this as better than my "hypnotic" and my three-against-four. I would name the mechanism a player knows: a groove is working when you **stop hearing it as events and start hearing it as the floor**. That is when the change lands hardest. The three-against-four is a reason it stays alive; the listener will say only "comfortable", and the report should say that.

## 2. What I am keeping from the others

- **The psychologist's "taught, then denied, then paid".** Best structural sentence in the whole panel. It is a groove, a removal, a return: three beats. Write the drop that way.
- **The psychologist's forward-in-time pass.** I want it, with the caveat I gave: a model that knows what drops look like will see the warning. That is the truth for a genre-aware listener, so record that. Two listeners: the one who sees the cliff, and the one who does not.
- **The viewer's two readers.** First-timer without Dutch, and the second reading. For music I would add that the first-timer is also the one for whom the voice is "an old man, and I'm in a documentary", which carries more of the track's feeling than any word he says.
- **The critic's 300 + 300 words.** And mine: the top of the report is five sentences a player could say across a table. The fan's 300 words can hold them.
- **The devil's three registers and a sidecar.** Fine. The player's register is one of them.
- **The fan's "a wake, not a funeral."** I have already said why it is true as arrangement: a steady walk, and then the guests leave one at a time.

## 3. A player's paragraph for Part 2, to test against the above

"It is a build record with a march in it. A held pad, then a walking kick and bass. Then an old Dutch voice, close and dry, who sounds like he is announcing the day. The band drops out for two bars, leaving the pad and the voice, then comes back on the one, flat and heavy, with no fill. After that the groove never changes: the bass rolls over a straight kick, and over the next two minutes one layer slowly opens up. At the end it comes apart one layer at a time, stops dead, rings, and finishes on two hits. I could not hear the kick's character, and I cannot say what the late voice-like scraps are."

Every sentence there is checkable against the stems or says it is not. Nothing in it is a number except "two", which is a count a person can make.

## 4. Seven principles for writing about music the way a human experiences it

1. **Describe the playing, then what it does to you.** "A bass rolling over a straight kick" first, the feeling second. Players and listeners both start from what they hear, not what was measured.
2. **Use the words a player would use, and only for things the stems allow.** Groove, build, break, drop, tag, stop, pad, bass, kick. Say "sounds like" for anything you cannot isolate. Never name an instrument or a trick the stems cannot show.
3. **Numbers earn their place by meaning.** No dB, no windows, no p-values in the body. A tempo is allowed once if it says what the record is. Counts a person can make ("two bars", "two hits") are fine.
4. **Write the arc, not the inventory.** Taught, denied, paid; load, then release; layers on, layers off. A track is a shape in time and a reader remembers the shape.
5. **The peak and the end get the words; the middle gets one honest sentence.** In a build record say what the middle is doing (loading the end), and say where listeners split.
6. **Say who is hearing it.** Genre-aware or not, phone or speakers, knows the race or not, Dutch or not. The same drop is a fulfilled promise to one and a jolt to another, and a record on a phone has no floor.
7. **Admit the hole in one plain line.** Say what we could not hear (the kick's weight and character, the room, what the late scraps are). One honest line beats a hedge on every claim.

## 5. The three worst habits of our current reports to kill

1. **Reporting the instrument reading as the experience.** "22 dB", "161 BPM", "seven pitch classes", "36% of cuts within 80 ms". They are how we checked. A listener has a filter opening, a kick that comes back, a bass that rolls.
2. **Hedging the playing while overclaiming the feeling.** "How it was made cannot be recovered" sits beside confident "feels like a held breath". It is back to front. Commit on what the music does ("a synth-sounding layer opens up"), and be modest about what it does to a person ("for some listeners, ...").
3. **Treating the sound as a table and leaving out the groove.** The reports track what happens and when but never how it moves: the roll of the bass, the weight of the kick, whether it pushes or drags, the comfort of a loop you stop hearing. That is what a player listens to first, and it is where the record actually lives.
