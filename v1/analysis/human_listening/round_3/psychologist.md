# Round 3: the music psychologist (final round)

British English, plain speech. I have read all six round 2 files. Nobody on this panel has heard either track, me included, so everything below is what the research and the reports' stem facts license. Where I know the evidence is thin I say so.

## 1. The questions put to me

### The devil: give us a whitelist, and say what in round 1 was weaker than it sounded

Mechanisms I would let the toolkit cite as the reason behind a "most listeners will" sentence. Ranked by how safely.

**Safe to cite (large, replicated, mostly pre-conscious):**
1. **Expectation, and its violation or delay.** Listeners learn a pattern within a few repetitions and register departures from it. A taught pulse that is withdrawn and then restored is the cleanest case we have. Cite it as "what had been taught, what was denied".
2. **Arousal from loudness, density and sudden change.** A large jump in level or a sudden dropout raises physiological arousal in most people. It says nothing about which feeling follows. That is the point: it licenses "you sit up", not "you grieve".
3. **Entrainment.** A steady pulse in the walking-to-dancing range pulls the body into step, and a little syncopation tends to increase the urge to move. Fine for 120 BPM; say "foot-tap", not "joy".
4. **The voice as a person.** Voices are picked up as someone, with an age and a mood, before any word is understood. This holds in a language you do not speak.
5. **Audiovisual binding is generous.** Loose sync is accepted as "belongs together". So a sync statistic cannot be turned into "the picture refuses to cheer".

**Cite with a hedge:**
6. **Peak and end are over-represented in recall.** Good evidence from everyday experience, thinner for music. Write "people tend to carry away the peak and the ending", not a law.
7. **Absorption and mind-wandering in repetitive music.** Both are real and individual. I will not say what proportion do which.

**Weaker than I made them sound in round 1.** The chills rate ("half to two-thirds"): the literature gives wide ranges depending on how you ask, so the safe wording is "some people", and chills are likelier for familiar music, which makes them nearly irrelevant to a first listen. "Most felt emotion is arousal": half right. People often recognise a character ("solemn") without feeling it, but I overstated how little more there is. Associations can carry real feeling, and for a listener who knows the race the association is most of it. Drop the "most". Also my stated figure on how fast genre is recognised: it is fast, but I should have said "within seconds, for familiar genres", not implied it works for every listener.

**Smallest piece of real human data.** One listener, one video, three questions, answered before reading anything: *where did you sit up, where did your attention go, what do you remember a day later?* Ten people doing that for Part 2 would let us score every "most listeners will" sentence as hit, miss or split. Five would already tell us whether the 1:28 claim is a hit. Free-text comments and reviews from the video's own audience are the cheapest first source, with the caveat that the people who comment are the ones who were moved.

### The critic: what does a first-time listener keep of the long middle, and what honest sentence can a review say about it?

My prediction, labelled as one: **a mood and a gait, not a sequence.** After the event is over, people remember "it kept going, steady, got harder at some point" and not "three sections". Nearly all the detail of a repetitive stretch is lost and it is replaced by a schema ("a build"). Some will recall the voice because it is the only thing that sounds like a person. A few will recall a moment they happened to be attending when something shifted, and that is luck, not design.

The honest sentence, testable by any reader: **"This is the long stretch where the beat stops being something you listen to and becomes something you walk on. Some people will love it, some will check the time, and most will only notice afterwards that it had got fiercer."** It makes three claims, one about habituation, one about individual difference, one about retrospective noticing, and the third is the one most likely to be true and the one reports never make. The middle is not empty. It is where the later feeling is bought.

I side with the viewer against a pure footnote: the middle goes in the main text, in two sentences.

### The fan: a defensible wording, and four listener types for the drop and the ending

Wording. Use "would", never "felt"; name the group; give the mechanism in four words or fewer; allow one split. Template: *"A first-time listener would sit up when the kick returns, because they have just had a minute of it taken away; a few will shrug."* The "a few will shrug" is the honesty. "Most" is allowed only when the mechanism is on the safe list above.

Do the predictions really differ by type, or only in colour? Both, and the table shows where.

| Listener | At the 1:28 return | At the ending |
|---|---|---|
| Cold first-timer, phone speaker | A lurch in the gap, a lift on return. If they were drifting, the silence is what wakes them. | "It stopped rather than finished." Mild puzzlement, then a pause. |
| Cold first-timer, headphones | The same, with the low end felt as well as heard. Stronger. | The same, plus the ring of the pad alone is noticed. |
| Fan of the band | The drop is read as the band's trademark payoff. Pleasure in being right, as the musician says. Little surprise. | Expected as the quiet after the payoff; a reading of it as a choice. |
| Knows the race (any language) | The same lift, but the voice in the gap now carries weight. | Different in kind. The absence of an ending is the point: 127 finished. The feeling is association, not arousal. |
| Dutch speaker | The words are heard during the gap, which the others do not get. The voice is the event as much as the kick. | The narrator's last line matters in a way the rest cannot test. |

The drop differs mostly in strength and in what carries it. The ending differs in kind. That is the single most useful sentence for the toolkit: the first minute is the bit where people agree, the last is the bit where they do not.

## 2. Where I agree, argue and build

**The musician corrected my arithmetic and I accept it. It changes the claim, not just the number.** A bar and three-quarters of nothing, after drums that were already thinning, is a normal break. So for anyone with dance habits the drop is mostly fulfilment, as the musician says, and mostly surprise for people without them. That is a gift to the report: the same four seconds is two events for two kinds of listener, and we can say so. It also kills a line in the video2 report outright, and I will name it below.

**The musician on warning, versus my forward-in-time pass.** The musician says a forward pass by an agent that knows genres will see the cliff coming and write "anticipation". I agree, and I think that is a feature. The pass should run with a persona stated in advance ("a listener who has never heard a dance record"), and again with a second ("a regular listener of loop-based music"), and the *difference* between the two readings is the finding. I would not pretend we can recover a real naïf. I withdraw "the surprise at 1:28 is then written by someone who did not know it was coming". Say instead: written by someone told to forget it.

**The devil on the expectation curve.** Good, with a warning. A curve of surprise over time will, if built from the stems alone, put the 1:28 gap at the top, because that is where the signal changes most. That is a plausible but circular check: we will have measured the thing we already knew was there. What it cannot show, and what matters, is the slow stuff. Surprise is not the only engine. Pleasure in a long build comes from tension held and gradually resolved, and nobody has found a good number for that. Use the curve for the peaks, write the middle by hand.

**The critic's "vigil".** It is a good word and it holds up psychologically: a vigil is a state of waiting, and our one real finding about the first 28 seconds is that people start waiting. I would let it stand as a summary word for the first-time listener, and put "wake" explicitly in the "if you know the race" paragraph. The fan was right that the fan's own word needs the history behind it.

**On "not elegy".** The critic says I was too tidy. Fair. What I should have said is: *the first-listen body response is arousal, strain and release; the interpretive frame, which people reach for in seconds once they have a title, a date and a dead narrator, is remembrance.* Both are true, they arrive in that order, and the second reframes the first in retrospect. Write them in that order. I do not retract that a cold listener does not feel grief; I do concede that criticism may rightly say what the piece is about.

**The viewer's "an absence where the winner should be".** The best line in six files. It is also a good psychological line: an ending that withholds closure is felt as incompleteness, and that is a bodily thing before it is a thought.

**The fan on translating the Dutch.** Agreed with the viewer's middle position: tell the reader plainly that it is a man's voice from 1963, give the gist once, put the word-for-word below. I would add that for someone who does not speak the language the voice is still heard as calm, formal, unhurried, and that is the feeling.

**Where I stand against the panel.** Several of you now say the report should "commit". On the shared layer yes. On the contested layer I still say commit to the *structure* of the disagreement and not to one word. "Remembrance for those who know, a march that stops for those who don't" commits fully and is a better sentence than either alone.

## 3. How this answers the user's complaint

The user said this is not how a human thinks and listens to music. The whole panel has now found the same four causes, and I will state them without the decoration.

1. **Order.** People get a gist first, then body, then maybe words. The reports put measurement first, then, grudgingly, a tagged feeling.
2. **Time.** People live forward in time, without knowing the end. The reports were written from the end of the file.
3. **Unit.** People do not hear events with dB values, they hear a shape: waiting, walking, a lift, a drift, a stop. The reports treat each event as an equal data point.
4. **Who.** The reports have no listener. Humans differ, especially about the ending and about the middle, and a report that does not say who is listening says nothing.

## 4. The seven principles

1. **Say what it is like before what it contains.** A gist in a sentence a listener could say to a friend. Everything else hangs off it.
2. **Write forward in time, from the listener's position.** What has been taught, what is now expected, what just happened. Surprise and relief exist only for someone who does not yet know what is next.
3. **Name the listener.** Cold, on a phone, with headphones, fan, knows the race, Dutch speaker. State which one the sentence is true for. Split where they split, and say so plainly.
4. **Use "would", not "felt".** We are predicting, and the label is honesty enough. Every feeling sentence should be one that ten real people could confirm or deny.
5. **Spend the words where memory spends them: the peak, the ending, the first voice, the thing that lingers.** Give the long middle two honest sentences about what people do there, not a timetable.
6. **Keep the body and the head in order: arousal and pulse first, then the character they recognise, then what they bring (history, voice, memory).** Mixed states are allowed and normal. State both and which arrives first.
7. **Numbers license sentences and do not appear in them.** Stems, dB, BPM and sync scores go in a footnote. The test is that a reader who never sees them still finds the sentence true.

## 5. The three worst habits to kill

**1. The evidence-first sentence that demotes the human one.** Quote from the video2 report: *"It feels like a held breath, or a cold morning heard through glass (INT)."* That is the best sentence in the piece and the report apologises for it with a tag. Elsewhere: *"For about 47 seconds nothing new happens and the loop is hypnotic"* is true to life, and the next sentence takes it away with a measurement. What a listener would say: "it's hypnotic", full stop. The tag, the figure and the caveat go underneath.

**2. Treating the hindsight as the listening.** The reports say "it comes with no warning" at the drop, and "the picture ignores the drop". The first is false for anyone who knows the genre (the drums thinned out for thirty seconds first), and the second is a story told by a detector about an edit whose intent nobody knows. A listener knows only what has come before, and the picture's loose sync is, for most people, just "belongs together". Kill the omniscient narrator.

**3. The shrug that stands in for the answer.** *"Depends on what you bring."* A real listener does not say that, and it is the sentence where the report should be at its most specific. Say what three named people would bring: the cold first-timer a stopped march, the fan a trademark payoff, the person who knows 1963 the empty place where the winners would be. A shrug is not humility. It is leaving the job to the reader.

## 6. Last limit, kept once

We still have no listener data. The best correction to the whole approach is five or ten real people reading a draft and saying "that is not how I heard it". Until then, everything above is a better-made prediction, and the reports should say so in one line at the top and then stop apologising.
