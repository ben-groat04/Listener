# Round 3: the viewer

*British English. Final round, so: what I'd change in my own account, where I still disagree, what I'd steal, and then the two lists I was asked for. I am still a persona, a plausible phone viewer. Everything I say about what I "feel" is a prediction about people like that. I've said it once; the fan's rule says to hang a label on it, so here it is, and I'll stop repeating it.*

## 1. Mistakes in my own round 2 (and round 1)

**"It just keeps going and then falls apart at the end" is wrong.** I made that my test sentence and three people borrowed it. But the ending doesn't fall apart. The musician's reading is the accurate one: layers come off one at a time, two knocks, an unresolved bar. That is taken to pieces on purpose. "Falls apart" is what a tired viewer says when they weren't paying attention; "gets taken away piece by piece" is what a viewer who was says. My own best line was a half-listened one. I'd keep "stops rather than ends" and drop "falls apart".

**"I knew it was after the man said Elfstedentocht."** Round 1, the drop. I wrote that as if I remembered it, and nothing in our dossiers says the drop follows that word. It was a plausible story and I told it with a straight face. That is exactly the invented-but-fluent sentence the devil keeps catching in the rest of us. Cut.

**"A child, a hat, someone looking at the camera."** Plausible for archive film of a crowd. I haven't seen the frames and nobody on this panel has verified a child. Say "faces, mostly small, some looking into the lens" only if the frames show it; otherwise it's the general feel of old footage, not this footage.

**"Time goes strange."** Fine as a prediction, but I said it as if universal. The psychologist is right: some drift, some check the time. I should have written both.

## 2. Answers and disagreements

**The devil: our reports contain knowledge no first-time viewer has.** This is the sharpest thing said in round 2 and I want to push it further. Our agents know the narrator says 575 and 9,500, know 127 finished, know the date, because they read transcripts and research. Then they write the music as if the listener had that. A first-time phone viewer has none of it. What they have is: a man's voice, a date on a card, the sense of a race. The numbers don't land as numbers, they land as *a lot*. So I'd go beyond "two readers". I'd say the report should make the order of arrival part of its structure: first what reaches you with no Dutch and no history, then what changes once you know. The fan says the emotion lives in the second layer, the 127 finishers. I agree that's where grief lives. But if the report opens there, it describes a listener who doesn't exist, and the cold viewer's real experience (a calm, ghostly voice, an absence at the end) is lost. The order is the experience.

**The fan: write for the fan; the critic: write for the intelligent stranger; the musician: write for someone who cares about arrangement.** Three readers, three pulls. I stay with the cold stranger as the default, for the reason I gave before: a toolkit that must work on *any* video can't assume the reader loves the band. But I concede the musician's two-layer proposal ("how it plays", then "what it does to you") and the fan's extra paragraph for those who know. The stranger is the main text. The fan and player get a margin each.

**The critic: the middle gets a sentence; peak-end rules.** I accept that the words should go to the drop and the ending. But I still want that sentence on the middle to be in the main text and to say what a person does there, not what the music does. The psychologist's offer ("some go under, some drift to the window") is that sentence. And I'd say why the middle matters to the ending: the 47 seconds of loop is what makes the stop hurt. If you cut the middle from the account you lose the cause of the ending's effect.

**The musician: "the kick is the biggest hole; the reports have no word for its character."** Yes. And I'd add the same hole for the voice. The reports say "band-limited" and "dry". A person says "like a man in the next room, on a tape that's been in a loft". We don't have to invent what it sounds like, but we should admit the gap in plain words, not leave it empty.

**The musician on the opening 28 seconds ("you start waiting").** This is the best answer to my own round 1 point about being braced. A viewer who has never heard of "builds" still learns, in thirty seconds of a quiet drone, that something is being held back. That's what "turning the volume up on the train" is. I'd write the first half-minute as *being taught to wait*, and the 0:28 step as the wait being acknowledged.

**The psychologist's forward-in-time pass.** I'd make this the main change to the *workflow*, not just the prose. The reports sound like hindsight because they are hindsight: the agent knew at 0:30 what came at 1:28. A cumulative read (0 to 10 s, 0 to 28 s, 0 to 60 s, and so on) fixes the thing I couldn't name in round 1: why every moment reads as a plan. It would also fix what I called "attention that wanders", because the agent that reads only to 0:28 has no idea the middle matters.

**The devil's expectation curve.** Yes. And a caution from the phone: the curve needs a listener profile. The 42.6 Hz thump is zero on my speaker, so my curve has no bump there. State the equipment at the top, as I said before, and let the curve be drawn for that listener.

**The devil's worry that six of us agreeing is one prior counted six times.** True, and I'm the worst offender: "a funeral and a party at once" was mine too. I'd treat it as a hypothesis for the user to confirm, not a finding. The only evidence we have that any of it rings true is a person who has actually heard it. I second the devil's ask: one note from the user per video, "the bit I'd name, and what I felt". Score us against that.

**The critic's ceiling: one image per moment, after the plain sentence.** Agreed, and I'd add a second ceiling: no image that needs knowledge of the race to work, in the first layer. "A wake" is good, but it goes in the second layer.

## 3. What I'm stealing

- **Musician: "the bass runs in groups of three sixteenths across a kick in fours."** It's a player's phrase and it is also, in my words, why the loop is comfortable without being dull. Said in ordinary language: it rolls, it never quite sits down. I'd use it as the one groove sentence.
- **Musician: "the pad and the voice with no kick under them is a signal that something is about to land".** That is the answer to my "hearing the floor fall away": for someone who knows builds the floor falling *is* the announcement. Correct the "no warning" line, as the devil and musician both say.
- **Psychologist: "the voice is a person for everything except the words."** I said this in a worse form. Theirs is better.
- **Critic: "measurement licenses the sentence, it does not appear in it."** The best summary of what is wrong with our reports.
- **Fan: "a mate would say 'pretty sure it drops about a minute and a half in'".** Plain-English confidence words in place of OBS/INT/SPEC.

## 4. Two things nobody has covered

1. **What the picture does to the music.** The psychologist and I agreed the sync test answers a question nobody asking about feeling asks. But we haven't said what the right question is. It's this: what does the footage *make the music mean*? A calm drone over a title card is a held breath; the same drone over a skater's back is effort. A hypnotic loop over crowds on a frozen canal is a procession; over empty road it is waiting. The reports never make the music read the picture or the picture read the music. They treat them as two files that either line up or don't.
2. **Where the viewer is.** Phone, train, one earbud, the screen going to black. The black at the end isn't a shot. For this viewer it's the phone going dark in your hand. The report should know that "silence after the end" is something you do, not something a detector flags.

## 5. The seven principles, in order of importance

1. **Say what a person would feel first, and why second.** The feeling is the sentence, and the measurement is the alibi, kept to a footnote. Never let the second clause take back the first.
2. **Write forward in time.** A listener doesn't know what is coming. Write each moment from what has been taught so far, so surprise reads as surprise and not as a plan. Use a cumulative read to do it.
3. **Name your listener, and the order in which they learn things.** Cold, on a phone, no Dutch: voice as a person and texture, a catch of a date, a feeling of a lot of numbers. Then what changes for someone who knows the race or the band. Say the equipment, too.
4. **Spend the words where memory lives.** The opening wait, the one drop, the ending, the silence, and a sentence on what a person does in the middle. Say plainly that most of the rest washes by.
5. **Taught, then denied, then paid.** Describe music as an expectation being built, broken and met. That's the form a human story takes, and it is checkable against the stems.
6. **Every human sentence is a prediction a real person could confirm or deny, with plain confidence words** ("surely", "probably", "a guess") and one image at most, after it. No "I felt", no "most people" figures, no stolen band names unless they can be checked.
7. **Admit the gaps once, in plain words, and ask a human.** Kick character, voice grain, body, picture-meaning: say what nobody here can perceive in one paragraph, then give the user's own ear the final vote.

## 6. The three worst habits to kill

1. **The measurement that sits on the feeling.** "A held breath... (INT)", then a dB figure in the next clause. The report has human sentences; it gives each one a measurement to answer to. Kill the number-after-the-feeling reflex; keep the numbers in a note.
2. **Hindsight and equal coverage.** Every second explained as a plan by an agent who has seen the end, with the middle given the same weight as the drop. No one lives a piece like that. Kill the full-length even-handed walk-through.
3. **Confidence tags and shrugs as style.** OBS/INT/SPEC/H/M/L and "depends on what you bring" make the report sound like a committee minuting itself, and a committee shrugging at the end. Say what two or three kinds of listener would feel, in a sentence each, and own the guess.
