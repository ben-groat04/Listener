# Elfstedentocht Part 1: a heavy step towards a finish line you never see

*Public Service Broadcasting, 2013, 3:09. Black-and-white archive film of a 1963 ice-skating race, a Dutch voice, a loud instrumental band.*

**The line.** A cold, grinding march in which the band does all the feeling, and it stops before anyone arrives.

## The front page

Worth three minutes, and best loud. If you like records that build by taking things away rather than adding them, or you have a soft spot for old film with a band shoved underneath, this is for you. If you need a tune, a chorus or someone to sing to you, it is not. There is no tune. There is a heavy, stiff, slightly limping beat that never changes speed, a droning guitar-ish wall of sound, and a bass that comes and goes.

The moment you will remember, and this is my one bet, is about a minute and forty in. The bass has been gone for most of half a minute and the music has been floating with no floor under it. Then it slams back. It is not much louder. It is heavier, and after the wait it feels like your feet hitting the ground.

The ending is the other thing you keep. The voice says its last line, everything drops out for a heartbeat, a banner reading FINISH flashes past, and a bright, hard run starts. It never shows you anyone crossing a line. Then it simply stops, mid-stride, with no final chord.

## First listen, forward in time

**The opening.** It starts with a swell and a hit, like a door being kicked open, and the drums are already running. Within a second a thick wall of sound joins, and a couple of seconds later a bass creeps in and climbs. You are being taught the rules: a fixed pulse, layers arriving one at a time. It is tense, and the pictures agree: a night crowd, a newspaper, pale faces under lamps.

**The first voice.** About ten seconds in, a calm Dutch voice gives the date, the hour (half past five in the morning) and a distance for people strong enough to attempt it. It then says that what everyone is getting ready for is the Elfstedentocht. The voice is flat and unhurried, and the band does not make way for it. A calm man reading the facts over an engine that will not ease off is the feeling of the whole piece. When he finishes the sentence the band opens up, brighter and wider, and for a moment it is the band's piece. If you cannot follow Dutch, he is a steady human presence and a date.

**The build.** More short bursts of talk (sixteen degrees below zero, a monster ride of more than 200 kilometres) while the hi-hats open out and the picture moves from landscape to inside the pack, bibs and fast cuts. It all leans forward. Then at about 1:15 the bass cuts dead. What is left is light, thin and slightly uneasy, and into the cleared space the voice reads out the numbers, a few hundred racers and about nine and a half thousand tourers, over a registration table and writing hands. When the voice stops, the band surges back up, still with no bass, and a slow line climbs over the top. It is the nearest thing to a melody in the piece, and it sounds like longing.

**The landing.** Then the bass returns. You have been taught, now, for twenty-five seconds, that the floor is missing, and the payoff is weight, not noise.

**Then the held breath.** Around 1:55 the bass leaves again and the sound empties to something thin, high and narrow, and the film goes indoors: a kettle, young people sitting with cups of tea, someone looking at the camera. After the roar this is the quietest and humanest stretch. Underneath it, six very low thumps arrive, slowly, one every six seconds or so. On headphones they are a second, slower clock. On a phone you will not hear them at all, and what you feel instead is the wall dropping out.

**The town list.** From about 2:15 the sound thickens, and the narrator reads the towns of the route one by one, slowly, while a sign or an inn for most of them appears within a second: Workum, Bolsward, Franeker, Dokkum, a signpost to Leeuwarden. It is almost affectionate, like someone reading out the stops on a line. It is also the only time the film and the voice neatly meet.

**The end.** The weight comes back, and the opening sentence returns, the same words that began the piece. After it comes a hole, a fraction of a second where the drums and bass drop away and the guitar holds, and a banner says FINISH. Then comes the long bright run, steady and hard, with a gritty circling figure low down, and at 3:08 it is cut off. Nothing resolves.

**The long middle** is the stretch from the first build to the second. This is where the beat stops being something you listen to and becomes something you walk on. Some people will love it, some will check the time, and most will only notice afterwards that it had got fiercer.

## Who hears what

**A cold first-timer on a phone, no Dutch.** You get a cold, relentless pulse, a voice that sounds like history, and a grey crowd. The bass drop and its return survive if the speaker is decent, but the six thumps will not. You will like the tea scene. You may feel cheated at the end, and I think that is right: it is built to cheat you.

**A fan of this kind of band.** You will hear that nothing changes except what is switched on. Drums, one drone, no tune. You will either find that hypnotic or a bit thin, and you will probably wish the thin passage at 1:55 and the earlier floaty one at 1:15 were not so alike.

**Someone who knows the race, or speaks Dutch.** This is a different piece. "Half past five", sixteen below, the towns, they all mean something to you. In that January only about 127 people finished, out of nearly ten thousand starters, and you know the film is Frisian regional archive. For you the stop is a held breath, and the missing finish is a question.

## What the picture does to the music

The footage is mostly paper, signs, crowds and anonymous skaters seen from behind. Nobody is named, nobody smiles in any frame we could read, and the cold is in everything: frosted caps, frosted reeds, a ground blizzard. The band gives it stakes and drive. Put a cosy major-key bed under the tea scene and it would be a different film. And the music says "arrive" while the picture, in everything we checked, never shows an arrival. The edit does not seem to follow the beat. It follows the narrator.

## Where it sits

A drum pattern that never changes, a voice that supplies the facts and a band that supplies the feeling is the Public Service Broadcasting method, and the archive hiss is part of it, not a flaw. Beyond that I would not name a comparison.

## What nobody here could hear

None of us heard this. We worked from separated stems, levels and pitch tracks, and from frames. We could not hear the weight of the bass coming back, the grain of the voice, the room, or whether any of it moved us. We cannot say whether the wall is guitars, synthesisers or both, or whether the drums are played or programmed. Where this piece says "you", it is a prediction about a named kind of listener, and we would like your ear to overrule it.

**What stayed with you? Which sentence rang falsest?**
