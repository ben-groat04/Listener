# Waiting for the floor to come back

I can't hear; this is how it would land, built from a close analysis of the sound and picture.

It made me restless, then braced, and then, for a little while, oddly tender. Three minutes of black-and-white film about a skating race in the Dutch cold, under a grinding rock track that hardly ever lets go of its step. For most of it I was being pushed along. What stayed with me was the stretch where the floor went missing, and the moment it came back.

It starts with a snap: a hit, drums already running, and within a few seconds a low bass slips in, quiet at first, and swells until it is the weight of the whole thing. Like an engine catching on a freezing morning. The picture is dark. A crowd at night, bright lamps, a lit sign reading POSTHUMA, a tilted newspaper front page announcing the race, a number on a skater's back. It felt like the hour before anything happens, when everybody is awake who shouldn't be.

Then the voice: a Dutch voice reading out the date, the hour, the distance in a formal, factual way, like a bulletin and not a story. Sixteen degrees below zero. A monster of a ride, over two hundred kilometres. It doesn't lean on anything, and the band doesn't politely step aside for it; the music thrums on beneath it. A voice reading figures over a track that sounds ready for war was the first thing that got to me. It was a clerk at a desk with the weather going on outside.

After that it works less like a song than like a machine being run hard. There is no tune to hum. When the voice stops, the band opens out, brighter and wider, and you feel the climb. For the first minute and a half I was happy enough to be carried, though it was a little stern and a little samey. The riff is blunt and asks for patience.

Then the bottom drops out. The heavy note that has been underneath everything is simply gone, and the music turns lighter and slightly uneasy, like stepping onto a stair that isn't there. The voice returns with numbers of racers and skaters while the picture shows a registration table, hands writing, a bib. When the voice stops again, the sound swells with nothing underneath it, a slow line floating over empty space. I held my breath. It is the nearest thing in the piece to a tune, and it felt like reaching for something with no ground to stand on. It hangs there a good while.

When the bass finally returns, it is the best moment in the piece. It isn't louder, exactly; the ground is just suddenly there again and the chest lets go. It put me in mind of someone finally getting a door open in a film. The waiting did the work. The picture goes pale and open: a snowy landscape with two small skaters, a high view of a snowy village with a canal curving through it. Then comes a further loud, busy stretch, the loudest of the film, with cuts tumbling over each other and a scrum of people, and I was swept along again.

Then the bass leaves, the music thins and goes high, and the picture goes indoors. This is the part I loved. A kettle pours into a cup. People sit shoulder to shoulder on a bench drinking from white cups, some in hats or hoods, and one of them looks straight into the camera. Underneath come very low, slow thumps, six of them about six seconds apart, like a second, slower clock behind the quick one. They run on well past the tea. It is among the quietest and warmest passages of the whole thing, in the middle of a track that is otherwise all effort. I'd call it the heart of the piece.

Then the effort comes back. The voice reads out the towns of the route one by one, and the signs flick past almost as they are named: an inn, a welcome sign, a town name in two languages, a signpost to the last city. This is where voice and picture fit best, and it felt like being led, stop by stop, down a very long road. Under the list the music shifts around, a restless, wavering pad of chords, with no bass at all. When the list is done the bass comes back, and the voice says again the sentence from near the start, the one about what everyone is getting ready for, the Elfstedentocht. It closed a loop.

At about 2:48 there is a brief hole, a fraction of a second where drums and bass vanish and the last syllable of the voice fades into the gap. A held breath, with the sprint just behind it.

And then the sprint: bright, wide, cymbals hammering, a low growl circling underneath. I expected someone to cross a line, and nobody does. There are skaters with numbers, blurred crowds, a newspaper again, a town sign, a banner reading FINISH, and last of all a banner in an indoor hall reading Reportage ELFSTEDENTOCHT, advertising something I couldn't read the rest of. Then black, as the music tapers to nothing in about half a second. The music says arrive. The picture says: here is a crowd, a headline, a cup of tea.

That mismatch is what I carried away. The band gives it urgency; the film gives it faces and cold and small kindnesses. It is collective, grey and a bit gruelling, and it made me feel the cold more than any triumph.

I'd go back for the stair that isn't there, and the tea.
