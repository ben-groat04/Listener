# Waiting for the floor to come back

I can't hear; this is how it would land, built from a close analysis of the sound and picture.

It made me restless, then braced, and then, for a while, oddly tender. Three minutes of black-and-white film about a skating race in the Dutch cold, under a loud, grinding rock track that never lets go of its step. For most of it I was just being pushed along. What stayed with me was the stretch where the floor was taken away, and the moment it came back.

The start is a snap: a hit, drums already running, and a low, heavy note creeping in underneath until it is carrying the whole thing. It is like an engine catching on a freezing morning. On screen it is dark: a crowd in the night, a blur of coats, a newspaper held up to the lens with the race on its front page, a lit shop sign, bright lamps. Someone's back with a number sewn on it. It felt like the hour before anything happens, when everybody is awake who shouldn't be.

Then the voice. An old-sounding Dutch voice, dry and flat, reading out the date, the hour, the distance, as if it were a bulletin and not a story. Sixteen degrees below zero. A monster of a ride, over two hundred kilometres. It doesn't raise itself or lean on anything, and the band doesn't politely step aside for it either. The music thrums on beneath it while it recites its facts. That gap between a voice reading figures and a track that sounds ready to go to war was the first thing that got to me. The voice is the clerk; the band is the weather outside.

The middle of the piece is built less like a song than like a machine being switched on and off. There is no tune to hum. When the voice stops, the band opens up, brighter and wider, and you feel the climb. I nodded along, I'll admit, and in the first minute and a half I was fairly content to be carried, though I'd have said it was a little stern, a little samey. If I have a mild complaint, it is that. The riff is blunt, and it asks for patience.

Then the bottom drops out. The heavy note that has been carrying everything just goes. The music turns lighter and slightly uneasy, like stepping onto a stair that isn't there. The voice comes back and reads out numbers of racers and skaters, a registration table on screen, hands writing, a bib. Then the voice stops and the wall of sound swells and swells with nothing under it, a slow line floating over empty space. I caught myself holding my breath. It is the nearest thing in the piece to a tune, and it felt like reaching for something with nothing to stand on. For a good twenty-odd seconds the music hangs there.

When the bass comes back, after that long wait, it is the best moment in the piece. It isn't louder, exactly; it is that the ground is suddenly there again, and your whole chest relaxes. It put me in mind of the moment in a film when someone finally gets a door open, or of a long note on a church organ arriving under a choir that has been singing without it. That's a comparison, not a claim. But the effect is real enough: the waiting did the work. Over it the picture has gone pale and open: a white, windy landscape, a couple of skaters tiny in the distance, then an aerial view of rooftops.

After that the music goes thin and high again, and the picture goes indoors. This is the part I loved. A kettle pours tea into white cups. Young people sit in a row in knitted hats, drinking, and one of them looks straight into the camera. Underneath, a few very low, slow thumps come in at long intervals, like a second, slower clock behind the quick one. If you're on a phone speaker they simply vanish, and what you feel is the dropping of the ground. It's the quietest, warmest, most exposed minute of the whole thing, and it sits in the middle of a track that has otherwise been all effort. I'd call it the heart of it, though a stranger could easily miss it.

Then the effort returns. The towns of the route are read out one by one, a slow list, and the signs flick past almost as they are named: an inn, a welcome-to sign, a town name in two languages, a signpost to the last city. It is the tidiest meeting of voice and picture in the film, and it felt like being led, stop by stop, down a very long road. The bass comes back, the harmony starts to walk, and the opening sentence returns, the one that began the whole piece, as if the film were closing a loop. Then, just before the end, a brief hole, a fraction of a second, where drums and bass drop out and the last syllable of the voice hangs in the air. A held breath. A whip of the camera, a banner reading FINISH.

And then the sprint: bright, wide, cymbals hammering, the low figure gritty and circling. I expected someone to cross a line. As far as I could see in the frames, nobody does. There are bibs, skaters, blurred crowds, a newspaper again, a town sign, a notice about a film showing, and then black, cut off mid-stride with no resolution. The music says arrive. The picture says: and here is a crowd, a headline, a cup of tea.

That mismatch is what I carried away. The band gives it stakes; the film gives it faces and cold and small kindnesses. It isn't heroic. It's collective, grey, and a bit gruelling, and it made me feel the cold more than I felt triumph. The numbers the voice reads out are the part that sits oddly in the body. Outside the film, I know that nearly ten thousand people started that race and a hundred and twenty-seven finished. None of that is said here, and the voice never reacts to it. Knowing it, I heard the flatness of that voice as someone keeping very still on purpose.

I'd listen again for the stair that isn't there, and the tea.
