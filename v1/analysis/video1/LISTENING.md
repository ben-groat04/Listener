# Cold, and a banner that says FINISH

I came out of it braced, the way you are after standing in a wind for a while. Not sad, not lifted. Tired for the people on screen. The drums run from the first second to the last with hardly a stumble, and over them sits a thick, grinding, heavy-booted wall of sound with no tune to hum and no chorus to wait for. It is stern. It put me in mind of a long march, or of a factory heard from the pavement outside, something enormous going on whether or not you are paying attention.

The picture is old black-and-white film of a skating race across frozen Friesland, and for the first half minute I did not know what I was looking at. A smear, a dark band over a pale cloud. Then a crowd at night in front of a lit building, nearly black, all heads and blur. A newspaper front page tilts into view announcing the Elfstedentocht for Friday; another calls it "superhuman hard". Somewhere in there a dry Dutch voice comes in and reads out a date, 18 January 1963, and later an hour that sounded like half past five in the morning. It does not tell you a story. It reads facts out, like a bulletin on a station you half-tuned in. The band does not make way for it. The voice drops into the spaces the music happens to leave and then gets swallowed again.

What I felt first was the cold. Frosted faces on a dark path. Someone stares straight into the camera; a few seconds later someone runs at the lens, rime on their clothes. A skater lurches. Later the voice gives the temperature, sixteen degrees below zero, and the frost on those faces had already said it.

The first moment that stayed with me is a small one. The voice finishes its sentence, *what everyone is preparing for, that is the Elfstedentocht*, and stops, and the music opens out, wider, as if a door had been pushed back. The picture is a hazy windmill, a wooden platform, a telegraph pole, then a line of skaters, tiny and black on a white plain. It felt like being let out into the open and finding it enormous and empty.

Then the first real surge: loud, bright, the pictures coming fast. Numbers on backs filling the frame one after another, a blur of ice and legs. I was swept along with it.

And then the floor went. The deep rumble that had been holding everything up simply vanished, and what was left was lighter and edgier and not at all comfortable, like a stair that is not there when your foot comes down. A registration table, hands writing on forms, the voice giving figures that sounded like 575 racers and about 9,500 tour skaters. Paperwork. I drifted. The sound was thin, the picture was forms and gloved hands, and my attention loosened.

What woke me was the stretch after the voice goes quiet at 1:27. There is still no bass, for about twenty-five seconds in all, but the rest of the band surges wide and loud, and the picture opens out to match: a bright snowy street with a lift bridge and a church spire, then smaller and smaller skaters in pale fields, then a single skater small beside reeds and a fence. One slow, stepping line floats up top, not quite a tune, more like someone walking carefully across thin ice. Then the bass came slamming back. Not louder so much as heavier, like a hand laid flat on your chest after you had got used to nothing there. It was the best moment in the film for me, relief and weight together, over a pale ridge of windblown dunes with two specks of skaters coming into it. The picture had changed a breath before, which made it feel almost inevitable.

After that it is loud and busy and sprawling: rooftops seen from high above, a skater climbing a wooden ramp, a rough handheld scramble of collars, legs, crowds lining a street, people heaving one another over rough ground.

Then the thing I did not expect. The rumble leaves again, the music shrinks to something thin and high, and the picture is a kettle pouring, cups and saucers on a patterned cloth. Four or five people on a bench in hats and hoods, drinking from white cups, one of them looking straight at me. It was the quietest, most exposed place in the whole piece, and into it drop half a dozen very slow, very deep thuds, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. A face in a snow-crusted knitted cap, mouth open. A hunched figure. Someone swigging from a bottle. A skater kneeling in the open snow, then getting up again. This is where the film turned human for me. Everything before was a crowd and a plain; this was people drinking from cups in the cold. It was the one place I felt tenderness, and it caught me off guard in something this hard-faced.

The voice comes back and reads the towns of the route one by one, a little higher and slower, with signs flashing up near the names: IJLST, WORKUM, DOKKUM. A small aeroplane over a white plain, a helicopter. It has the cadence of a litany, and the music walks along underneath it. The bass returns, the opening sentence comes round again, the same words if not necessarily the same take, and I felt everything closing in on a point.

Then a hairline flicker where the drums and bass drop away, and a white banner cuts in with black capitals: FINISH, just as they come back. A crowd in hoods behind it. And the sprint begins, wide and steady and relentless. It does not build. It holds one level of effort and will not let go, while the pictures race past: numbers, a skater bent double on the ice, a blank white plain, the "zwaar, zwaar, zwaar" headline again, cracked ice and a skate.

What I wanted was a skater crossing a line. In the pictures I looked at, nobody is seen crossing one, and no winner is named or shown. The banner says FINISH and the music says arrival, but the picture gives people in peaked caps (role unclear), a crowd, blur and glare. Less than a second after the banner there is one skater in a bib with his head down and a man's hand on his back, and then the picture moves on. I found I liked that more than I expected. The music promises an ending and the film steps round it.

The ending itself is a shrug. The sound tapers away in about half a second and stops, with no cadence. Four of the road signs from the town list come round again in about a second and a half (IJLST, DOKKUM, BOLSWARD, FRANEKER), an indoor hall with a banner announcing a report on the race, then black.

I would call it brave more than beautiful. It is relentless and a bit stubborn, and in the paperwork stretch it tried my patience. But when the bass came back after that thin wait, and in the hush of the bench with its cups and slow thuds, it got to me.
