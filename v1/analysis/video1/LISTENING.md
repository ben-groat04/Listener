# Three minutes of cold, and a banner that says FINISH

I came out of it braced, the way you are after standing in a wind for a while. Not sad, not lifted. Tired on someone else's behalf. The track never lets up. The drums are running from the first second to the last, and over them sits a thick, grinding, heavy-booted rock sound with no tune to hum and no chorus to wait for. It is stern. It put me in mind of a long march, or of the way a factory sounds from the pavement outside: something enormous going on whether or not you are paying attention.

The picture is old black-and-white film of a skating race across frozen Friesland, and for the first half minute I could not tell what I was looking at. A smear, a dark band over a pale cloud. Then a crowd at night in front of a lit building, nearly black, all heads and breath. Newspaper front pages tilt into view, headlines about the race being on, about it being "superhuman hard". Somewhere in there a dry, flat Dutch voice comes in and reads out a date, 18 January 1963, and an hour, half past five in the morning. It does not tell you a story. It reads facts out, like a bulletin on a station you half-tuned in. And the band does not make room for it. The music simply carries on, and the voice sits down inside the noise like someone speaking from the next room.

What I felt first was the cold. Frosted faces on a dark path. Someone staring straight at the camera, rime on their clothes, as if the lens had interrupted them. A skater lurching. Later a voice says sixteen degrees below zero and I believed it before the number arrived.

The first moment that stayed with me is small. The voice finishes its sentence, *what everyone is preparing for, that is the Elfstedentocht*, and stops, and the music opens out, wider and brighter, as if a door had been pushed back. The picture is a hazy windmill, a wooden platform, a telegraph pole, then a line of skaters, tiny and black on a white plain. It felt like being let out into the open and finding the open was enormous and empty.

Then the first real surge: loud, bright, the pictures coming fast. Numbers on backs filling the frame, one after another, a blur of ice and legs. I was nodding along by then.

And then the floor went. A little after a minute in, the deep low rumble that had been holding everything up simply vanished, and what was left was lighter and edgier and not at all comfortable, like a stair that is not there when your foot comes down. A registration table, hands filling in forms, the voice reading out how many racers, how many tourists. Paperwork. I will admit I drifted a little here. The sound was thin on purpose and the picture was bureaucracy, and I felt my attention loosen.

What woke me was the long stretch after the voice goes quiet: about twelve seconds with nothing underneath, and the picture empty to match. Tiny skaters in a white void. A single skater small beside reeds and a fence. One slow, stepping line of sound floating up top, not quite a tune, more like someone walking carefully across thin ice. I held my breath, honestly. Then the low sound slammed back. Not louder so much as heavier, like a hand laid flat on your chest after you had got used to nothing there. It was the best moment in the film for me: relief and weight at once, over a pale ridge of windblown dunes with two specks of skaters coming into it. The picture had changed just before, which only made it feel more inevitable.

After that it is loud and busy and sprawling. An aerial view of rooftops and a canal, a skater climbing a wooden ramp, then a rough handheld flurry, close collars, legs, crowds lining a street, people heaving each other over rough ground.

Then the thing I did not expect. The rumble leaves again, the music shrinks to something thin and high, and the picture is a kettle pouring into a cup. Teacups on a patterned cloth. Four or five people on a bench in hats and hoods, drinking from white cups, one of them looking straight at me. It was the quietest, most exposed place in the whole track, and into it come six very slow, very deep thuds, one every few seconds, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. A weathered face in a snow-crusted cap with its mouth open. A hunched figure. Someone swigging from a bottle. A skater kneeling in the open snow, then getting up again. This is where the film became human for me. Everything before was a crowd and a plain; this was people warming their hands. If I am honest it is also where I felt closest to something like tenderness, which surprised me in a piece this hard-faced.

The voice returns and reads the towns of the route one by one, a little higher and slower, with signs flashing up near the names: IJLST, WORKUM, BOLSWARD, FRANEKER, DOKKUM. A small aeroplane over a white plain, a helicopter. It has the cadence of a litany, a roll-call, and the music walks along underneath it. The low sound comes back, the opening sentence comes round again, and I felt the thing closing in on a point.

Then comes a hairline gap, a fraction of a second where the drums and low sound drop out, and a white banner cuts in with black capitals: FINISH. A crowd in hoods behind it. And the sprint begins, bright and wide and steady, cymbals splashing, a gritty circling low figure underneath. It does not build. It holds one level of effort and refuses to give it up, and the pictures race past: numbers, a skater bent double on the ice, a blank white plain, the "zwaar, zwaar, zwaar" headline again, cracked ice and a skate.

What I wanted, and did not get, was a skater crossing a line. There is no winner here, no arms in the air. The banner says FINISH and the music says arrival, but the picture shows officials, a crowd, blur and glare. I found that I liked this. The music promises an ending the film declines to show.

And the ending itself is a shrug. The sound tapers over about half a second and stops, with no final chord. The road sign for Dokkum again, an indoor hall with a banner announcing a report on the race, then black.

I would call it brave rather than beautiful. It is relentless and a bit stubborn, and in the paperwork stretch it tests your patience. But the moment the low sound came back after that long thin wait, and the quiet of the tea bench with its six slow thuds, I was properly moved. It is the sound of effort, with the faces left in.
