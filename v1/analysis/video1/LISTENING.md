# Cold, and a banner that says FINISH

I came out of it braced, the way you are after standing in a wind for a while. Not sad, not lifted. Tired on someone else's behalf. The drums run almost without a break from the first second to the last, with one tiny gap near the sprint, and over them sits a thick, grinding, heavy-booted rock sound with no tune to hum and no chorus to wait for. It is stern. It put me in mind of a long march, or of a factory heard from the pavement outside: something enormous going on whether or not you are paying attention. The weight comes and goes, but it never stops driving.

The picture is old black-and-white film of a skating race across frozen Friesland, and for the first half minute I could not tell what I was looking at. A smear, a dark band over a pale cloud. Then a crowd at night in front of a lit building, nearly black, all heads and blur. Newspaper front pages tilt into view, one saying the race is on, one calling it "superhuman hard". Somewhere in there a dry, flat Dutch voice comes in and reads out a date, 18 January 1963, and an hour that sounded like half past five in the morning. It does not tell you a story. It reads facts out, like a bulletin on a station you half-tuned in. The band does not duck for it; the voice slips in where the music leaves a gap and sits inside the noise like someone speaking from the next room, through an old speaker.

What I felt first was the cold. Frosted faces on a dark path. Someone staring straight into the camera, rime on their clothes, as if the lens had interrupted them. A skater lurching. Later the voice gives the temperature, sixteen degrees below zero, and the frost on those faces was already doing the work.

The first moment that stayed with me is small. The voice finishes its sentence, *what everyone is preparing for, that is the Elfstedentocht*, and stops, and the music opens out, wider, as if a door had been pushed back. The picture is a hazy windmill, a wooden platform, a telegraph pole, then a line of skaters, tiny and black on a white plain. It felt like being let out into the open and finding the open was enormous and empty.

Then the first real surge: loud, bright, the pictures coming fast. Numbers on backs filling the frame, one after another, a blur of ice and legs. I was nodding along by then.

And then the floor went. About a minute and a quarter in, the deep low rumble that had been holding everything up simply vanished, and what was left was lighter and edgier and not at all comfortable, like a stair that is not there when your foot comes down. A registration table, hands writing on forms, and the voice reading out what sound like entry numbers, racers and tour skaters. Paperwork. I drifted a little here. The sound was thin and the picture was bureaucracy, and my attention loosened.

What woke me was the stretch after the voice goes quiet: ten or twelve seconds with no low sound at all, just drums and a thin band going on, and the picture emptied to match. Tiny skaters in a white void. A single skater small beside reeds and a fence. One slow, stepping line floating up top, not quite a tune, more like someone walking carefully across thin ice. Then the low sound slammed back. Not louder so much as heavier, like a hand laid flat on your chest after you had got used to nothing there. It was the best moment in the film for me: relief and weight at once, over a pale ridge of windblown dunes with two specks of skaters coming into it. The picture had changed just before, which made it feel almost inevitable.

After that it is loud and busy and sprawling: rooftops seen from high above, a skater climbing a wooden ramp, a rough handheld scramble of collars, legs, crowds lining a street, people heaving one another over rough ground.

Then the thing I did not expect. The rumble leaves again, the music shrinks to something thin and high, and the picture is a kettle pouring, cups and saucers on a patterned cloth. Four or five young people on a bench in hats and hoods, drinking from white cups, one of them looking straight at me. It was the quietest, most exposed place in the whole piece, and into it drop half a dozen very slow, very deep thuds, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. A weathered face in a snow-crusted cap, mouth open. A hunched figure. Someone swigging from a bottle. A skater kneeling in the open snow, then getting up again. This is where the film became human for me. Everything before was a crowd and a plain; this was people with a hot drink in the cold. It is also where I felt closest to tenderness, which surprised me in something this hard-faced.

The voice comes back and reads the towns of the route one by one, a little higher and slower, with signs flashing up near the names: IJLST, WORKUM, DOKKUM. A small aeroplane over a white plain, a helicopter. It has the cadence of a litany, and the music walks along underneath it. The low sound returns, the opening sentence comes round again, and I felt everything closing in on a point.

Then a hairline gap, a flicker where the drums and low sound drop away, and a white banner cuts in with black capitals: FINISH. A crowd in hoods behind it. And the sprint begins, wide and steady and relentless. It does not build. It holds one level of effort and refuses to give it up, while the pictures race past: numbers, a skater bent double on the ice, a blank white plain, the "zwaar, zwaar, zwaar" headline again, cracked ice and a skate.

What I wanted, and did not get, was a skater crossing a line. There is no winner here, no arms in the air. The banner says FINISH and the music says arrival, but the picture gives men in peaked caps, a crowd, blur and glare. I found I liked that. The music promises an ending the film declines to show.

The ending itself is a shrug. The sound tapers away in a heartbeat and stops, with no final chord. The road sign for Dokkum again, an indoor hall with a banner announcing a report on the race, then black.

I would call it brave rather than beautiful. It is relentless and a bit stubborn, and in the paperwork stretch it tries your patience. But when the low sound came back after that thin wait, and in the hush of the bench with its cups and slow thuds, I was properly moved. It is the sound of effort, with the faces left in.
