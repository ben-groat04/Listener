# Waiting for the floor to come back

I can't hear; this is how it would land, built from a close analysis of the sound and picture.

It made me restless, then braced, and then, for a little while, oddly tender. Three minutes of black-and-white film about a skating race in the Dutch cold, under a grinding rock track that hardly ever lets go of its step. For most of it I was being pushed along. What stayed with me was the stretch where the floor went missing, and the moment it came back.

It starts with a snap: a hit, drums already running, and a low heavy note creeping in underneath until it carries everything. Like an engine catching on a freezing morning. The picture is dark. A crowd at night, bright lamps, a lit sign reading POSTHUMA, a tilted newspaper front page announcing the race, a number on a skater's back. It felt like the hour before anything happens, when everybody is awake who shouldn't be.

Then the voice, a low, dry Dutch voice reading out the date, the hour, the distance, as if it were a bulletin and not a story. I took it as flat, though that is my guess from the wording, not something I can swear to. Sixteen degrees below zero. A monster of a ride, over two hundred kilometres. It doesn't lean on anything, and the band doesn't politely step aside for it. The music thrums on beneath it while it recites. A voice reading figures over a track that sounds ready for war was the first thing that got to me. The voice is a clerk at a desk with the weather going on outside.

After that the piece works more like a machine being switched on and off than like a song. There is no tune to hum. When the voice stops, the band opens out, brighter and wider, and you feel the climb. For the first minute and a half I was happy enough to be carried, though it was a little stern, a little samey. The riff is blunt and asks for patience.

Then the bottom drops out. The heavy note that has been carrying everything is simply gone, and the music turns lighter and slightly uneasy, like stepping onto a stair that isn't there. The voice returns with numbers of racers and skaters, while the picture shows a registration table, hands writing, a bib. When the voice stops again, the sound swells with nothing underneath it, a slow line floating over empty space. I caught myself holding my breath. It is the nearest thing in the piece to a tune, and it felt like reaching for something with no ground to stand on. It hangs there a good while.

When the bass finally returns, it is the best moment in the piece. It isn't louder, exactly; the ground is just suddenly there again and the chest lets go. It put me in mind of someone finally getting a door open in a film, or a low organ note arriving under a choir that has been singing without it. The waiting did the work. Over that stretch the picture goes pale and open: a snowy landscape with two small skaters, then a high view of a snowy village with a canal curving through it.

Straight after, the music goes thin and high again, and the picture goes indoors. This is the part I loved. A kettle pours into a cup, presumably tea. Young people sit on a bench drinking from white cups, some in hats or hoods, and one of them looks straight into the camera. Underneath, a few very low, slow thumps arrive at long intervals, like a second, slower clock behind the quick one. On a phone speaker they would probably mostly vanish, and what you would feel is only the dropping of the ground. It lasts about twenty seconds, among the quietest and warmest of the whole thing, in the middle of a track that is otherwise all effort. I'd call it the heart of the piece, though a stranger could easily miss it.

Then the effort comes back. The voice reads out the towns of the route one by one, and the signs flick past almost as they are named: an inn, a welcome sign, a town name in two languages, a signpost to the last city. Of everything in the film this is where voice and picture fit best, and it felt like being led, stop by stop, down a very long road. Somewhere in that list the music had already started to move, a three-step walk of low notes, and then the bass came back, and a little after it the sentence from near the start returned: the one about what everyone is getting ready for, the Elfstedentocht. It closed a loop. Just before the end there is a brief hole, a fraction of a second where drums and bass vanish and the last word of the voice hangs in the air. The camera whips round just before it, and a banner reading FINISH shows up within about a second. A held breath.

And then the sprint: bright, wide, cymbals hammering, a low growl circling underneath. I expected someone to cross a line. In what I saw, nobody does. There are skaters with numbers, blurred crowds, a newspaper again, a town sign, and finally a banner in an indoor hall reading Reportage ELFSTEDENTOCHT, probably advertising a showing, though I couldn't make out all of it. Then black. Only the music is cut off; the picture just ends on that hall. The music says arrive. The picture says: here is a crowd, a headline, a cup of tea.

That mismatch is what I carried away. The band gives it urgency; the film gives it faces and cold and small kindnesses. It isn't heroic. It is collective, grey and a bit gruelling, and it made me feel the cold more than any triumph.

I'd go back for the stair that isn't there, and the tea.
