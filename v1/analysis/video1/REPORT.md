# Elfstedentocht Part 1: a cold dawn, a heavy step, and a bass that comes back

*Public Service Broadcasting, "Elfstedentocht Part 1" (3:09). Black-and-white archive film of the Frisian ice race of 18 January 1963, scored by a loud, relentless instrumental rock track.*

## The short version

A calm Dutch voice tells you it is half past five in the morning, a crowd is gathering, and sixteen degrees below zero is waiting outside. Underneath, a heavy, grinding step that never relaxes. For about a minute and a half the band builds; then at 1:15 the bass vanishes, and for 24 seconds the music floats with no floor under it. When the bass slams back at 1:40, it is the track's cathartic hit. It lands harder than its loudness suggests precisely because it was withheld. Everything after is a long run towards a bright, loud, cymbal-driven sprint that ends, at 3:08, with the effort still going. Nobody in the film ever crosses a finish line. The music tells you to arrive; the picture shows you cold, crowds, paperwork and tea.

## How to read this report

I cannot hear audio or watch film as you do. The music was "heard" through measurements: separated instrument stems, spectrograms, chord and pitch tracking, loudness curves. The picture was read from sampled frames, about one a second. Feelings below are reasoned inferences from those measurements, not listener data. Where something is uncertain I say so in plain words, once.

## The music

**It is built on one note.** The track sits on a D drone at about 78 beats per minute, with a fast flutter of drums over it that never changes pattern or speed. There is no verse, chorus or tune. The third of the chord is almost never played, so it is neither major nor minor: it sounds stern and driven, not sad and not triumphant.

**The drama is subtraction.** Three things are switched on and off: the bass, a dense, distorted "wall" layer (probably guitars, though the stems cannot tell guitar from synthesiser), and the narrator. The drums keep going for the whole track; their only break is a 0.4-second hole at 2:47.8. The composer here is, in effect, the mixer.

**The journey, in order:**

- **0:00 to 0:10, snap and gather.** A swell and a hit at 0:00.7, drums already running, the wall joining at about 0:01, the bass creeping in from about 0:03. Layers arrive one by one on a fixed pulse: anticipation.
- **0:10 to 0:32, told a story.** The narrator gives the date ("18 januari 1963"), the hour and the crowd, ending on the sentence "Waar iedereen zich voor opmaakt, dat is de Elfstedentocht". The band does not duck for him. A calm voice over an engine that never relaxes.
- **0:32 to 1:15, momentum.** The voice stops on what looks like a bar line and the band opens up, brighter and wider. Short bursts of narration ("16 degrees below zero", "a monster ride of more than 200 km"), the wall swelling about 11 dB, the hi-hats opening from about 1:05, loudness climbing to a first peak around 1:07.
- **1:15 to 1:27, the floor falls away.** The bass cuts out at about 1:15, the mix drops 4 to 6 dB and the wall thins to bright upper lines. Light, suspended, slightly uneasy. The narrator comes in at about 1:19 to read out the numbers: 575 racers, about 9,500 tour skaters.
- **1:27 to 1:40, lift without ground.** The voice stops; the wall surges, the stereo picture doubles in width, loudness comes back, and a slow stepwise line floats over no bass at all. This is the track's one melody-like moment, and the nearest thing it has to yearning.
- **1:40, the landing.** The bass returns. This is the moment most listeners would point to. Loudness barely changes (about a tenth of a unit by one measure), but the bass itself leaps about 35 to 40 dB and the stereo image pulls into the centre. You feel weight and relief, not volume. It works because of the 24 seconds you waited.
- **1:55 to 2:38, held breath, then setting off.** The bass leaves again and the music empties to a thin, high, narrow block, the quietest passage of the piece. Into it fall six very low, identical thumps at 42.6 Hz, one every 6.15 seconds from 2:01 to 2:32, like a slow second clock over the unchanged flutter. On a phone speaker these largely disappear, and what you feel instead is the wall dropping. From about 2:13 the wall thickens, a walk through A, D and G begins, and the narrator reads the towns of the route one at a time.
- **2:38 to 2:48, weight resumes.** The bass comes back, the chords turn, and the opening sentence returns at about 2:44, so the line that began the piece comes back to frame it, and the band then answers it. Whether it is the same recording or a re-cut, the measurements cannot say. Then comes the hole: for about 0.4 seconds the drums and bass drop out while the narrator's last syllable decays and the guitar holds. A held breath that ends in release rather than shock.
- **2:48 to 3:08, the sprint.** The loudest, steadiest, widest and brightest stretch. One chord, D, with a gritty, circling low figure and a cymbal-led kit. By feel it is the riff and the payoff: hard, grinding, relentless. The exact notes cannot be recovered from the recording, but the character is plain. It ends in a quick taper of about half a second, with no cadence, as if the effort carries on offscreen.

**The voice.** It is one flat, unperformed voice at a steady four or five syllables a second, band-limited and centred, with no editing tricks that can be measured. It supplies facts and nothing else; the band supplies the feeling. It enters into space the band has cleared by subtraction (1:19, 2:15) and leaves at changes of state. The longest silent stretch for the voice, 1:27 to 2:15, covers the loudest and the emptiest passages: the story is told in the sparse parts and held back when the band is at full weight.

## How the picture works with it

The footage is made largely of print, paperwork, signs and crowds. Skaters are mostly anonymous bent figures seen from behind or at distance, in bibs. Nobody in the frames sampled smiles. The cold is everywhere: rime on caps and sleeves, frosted reeds, a ground blizzard at about 0:51 to 0:56.

Some of the joins are plain to see:

- **The towns.** As the narrator reads the route from about 2:15, a sign arrives within about a second of each name: IJlst, the Vrouwe van Stavoren inn, "WELKOM IN WORKUM", BOLSWARD / BOALSERT, FRANEKER / FRJENTSJER, DOKKUM, then a signpost to Leeuwarden. This is the tightest word-to-picture match in the film. Language says where, the band says how hard, and the faces are left unlabelled.
- **The numbers.** The registration count at 1:19 to 1:27 plays over a registration table, writing hands and bib 157.
- **The tea tables.** At 1:55 to 2:14, under the thinnest music and the first thump, the picture goes indoors: a kettle, a row of young people drinking from white cups, two of the five looking toward the lens, a frost-caked face, a van with a cross. This is the film's warmest and most exposed passage.
- **The ending.** At 2:47 a whip-pan and a hand-lettered "FINISH" banner arrive within about a second of the hole. Then the sprint, with bibs, a white-out, the same newspaper page again (2:57), the Dokkum sign again (3:06), a banner reading "Reportage ELFSTEDENTOCHT", and black.

Whether the cuts follow the music by design is not settled. Cuts cluster at a consistent point in the beat (a statistical result), but the cut list misses fast changes in snow, and other tests are no better than chance. The fair description is two well-made layers joined at the narrator, with the picture clearly following the narration, rather than a proven counterpoint. Broadly, though, picture and sound do both get busier together: the fastest cutting (about 1:06 to 1:16) sits at the first loudness peak.

## What it means

**The band gives stakes; the picture gives content.** Drive, waiting, return and release come from the music. Faces, cups, cold and crowds come from the film. Put a cosy major-key bed under the tea scene and it would feel entirely different. Here it feels cold, effortful, collective and unglamorous.

**Collective effort, not heroics.** The film's units are places and documents: headlines, a registration table, tea, a control hut, town signs, the finish banner. No skater is named; the only name in the frames is a headline fragment, apparently "R. Paping". The narration reads out "575 racers, about 9,500 tour skaters", not a winner.

**The subject is the effort, not the result.** The sound has an arrival, the 2:48 sprint. The picture never shows one. The panel agrees that viewers experience this; whether the makers intended it is undecidable. It is also only Part 1 of a two-part release, and archive cameras usually stood at control points, so the absence should not be over-read.

**Context.** The piece was commissioned by the Explore the North festival in Leeuwarden for the 50th anniversary of the 1963 race, "De Hel van '63". That year 9,862 started and 127 finished; Reinier Paping won in 10:59. The footage comes from the Fries Film Archief; which films were used, and who the narrator is, are not confirmed. The band's method is to let archive voices carry the story while the music frames them, and the archive hiss and grain are material, not flaws. Reception is thin but positive, centred on drive, physical effort and the fusion of image and sound. Readings of memory or ghostliness are the listener's, not demonstrated properties of the film.

## The moments that stay with you

1. **1:40, the bass slams back.** The strongest contrast in the track, made by absence.
2. **2:47 to 2:49.** The closing sentence, the half-second hole, the FINISH banner, then the sprint with nobody reaching anything.
3. **1:55 to 2:14, the tea tables.** The thinnest music and the film's humanest scene.
4. **2:15 to 2:36, the town list**, with a sign for nearly every name.
5. **1:15, the floor falling away**, and the six slow thumps, if your speakers can reproduce them.

If you only watch one minute, watch 1:55 to 2:48, and watch it loud, on good speakers or headphones.

## Caveats worth knowing

- The instrument identities (guitar versus synthesiser, played versus programmed) are inferred, not proven.
- The tempo could equally be felt at 156 BPM; the weight of the beat is the same either way.
- The Dutch transcription is reliable for the date, the key sentence and the numbers, and loose for place names over the loud band.
- Why the track ends in a half-second taper, studio choice or video edit, is unknown.
