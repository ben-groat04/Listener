# Music critic and producer: Round 1 reading of video1 (PSB, "Elfstedentocht Part 1", 3:08.7)

Basis: `music_reading.md` (the checked music analysis) and `dossier.md`. I cannot hear audio; every sensation below is an interpretation of measurements and carries the music reading's confidence (H/M/L). I did not re-open frames for this round; picture claims are taken from the dossier and flagged as such.

## 1. What this piece is about

As music, it is a study in **subtraction on a fixed ground**. One note (D), one tempo (about 78 BPM), drums that never change gear (the only drum event in the whole track is a 0.38 s hole at 2:47.83), and no tune. Everything that "happens" is three switches thrown on a bar grid: the bass, the dense distorted wall layer, and the narrator. The form is a drama of presence and absence, not of melody or harmony.

The subject, then, is endurance under a regime. The piece does not depict skating, speed or weather. It depicts a body kept moving by a pulse it cannot stop, while load is added and removed. That is why "disciplined urgency, heavy step with a fast heartbeat" (the music reading's phrase, M) seems right to me. The narrator supplies facts (date, hour, cold, distance, counts, towns); the band supplies the stakes. The voice never rises or quickens (a steady 4 to 5 syllable peaks per second), so the emotion cannot be in the voice. It is in what the band does around it.

Genre lineage, with caution: this is post-rock-with-a-documentary-sample, in the line of PSB's own method (found-speech over guitar-led, rhythm-section-driven instrumentals) and, in structure, closer to a long-form "sample-led post-rock" than to song form. The music reading withdrew the motorik label as a pattern claim and I agree: the groove is stiff and syncopated (hat on the 0.385 s slots, kick on odd sixteenth slots), nearer funk or programmed hip-hop drumming than krautrock. What survives of motorik is only the function: a ground that does not argue.

## 2. How it makes me feel, and exactly where

Ranked by how strongly I think the music itself (not the picture) does the work.

1. **1:40, the bass returns after 24 s (M-H).** The best single device in the piece. The bass goes from about -54 dB to -19 dB in one second, width pulls in from 0.78 to 0.40, and yet loudness rises only about 0.1 LU. So the arrival is felt as weight, not volume. It works because of what precedes it: 1:15 to 1:40 is a long bass-less passage, and from 1:27 the wall surges and the width doubles, so the ear is lifted without a floor. That is classic delayed-resolution writing, achieved with a mixer rather than a chord change. Feeling: relief, ground under the feet, a held note finally set down.
2. **1:15, the floor drops (H on cause).** Bass out dead at 1:15.3, mix down 4 to 6 dB, wall thins to bright upper lines, tonic D nearly absent for about 8 s. Light, suspended, faintly uneasy. This is the setup for item 1 and I think the producer meant them as a pair.
3. **2:47.8, the half-second hole (H on timing, M on feeling).** The narrator's last syllable decays inside a 0.4 s drop-out of drums and bass while the guitar holds, then the band returns brighter and wider. It is the track's only drum event, so it carries the weight of an entire arrangement vocabulary. It lands as a held breath ending in release rather than a shock: it is a catch inside the bar, not a bar-line stop.
4. **2:48 to 3:08, the sprint (M-H).** Loudest and steadiest stretch (-7.0 to -6.4 LUFS), brightest (drum energy above 5 kHz up from about 1% to 9 to 12%), widest (0.7 to 0.86), on a single D chord with a circling chromatic figure. Hard, grinding, driven. It is not triumphant: no third, no cadence. It feels like effort continuing rather than effort rewarded.
5. **2:01 to 2:31, the six sub thumps (M, and playback-dependent).** A designed 42.6 Hz thump every 6.15 s (eight beats, slot 21 mod 32, a sixteenth after the beat, off the hat accent) over an unchanged fast flutter in the thinnest, narrowest part (width 0.2 to 0.3, loudness about -13.5 LUFS). "Heavy but leaning", coiled, mildly ominous. The caveat is real: on small speakers the tone largely vanishes and the felt event is the wall collapsing.
6. **0:31.7, the voice exits on a bar line (M).** The band opens up: drum centroid about 1.5 to 3 kHz, width 0.35 to 0.55. A small, clean "now it is ours" moment, and the narration's refrain sentence is what it follows.
7. **0:00.7, the swell and hit (M).** Not a cold slam: the rise starts in the drum stem at 0:00.2. A snap to attention.
8. **3:08.4, the taper (M on feel, L on cause).** About 0.4 s to silence, no cadence. Reads as "carries on elsewhere". I cannot tell whether this is the studio ending or the video's.

## 3. What is clever

- **Using the mixer as the composer.** Since pitch barely moves (the third never rises above about 0.16 in power chroma; no V to I cadence; 125 chord labels are mostly detector noise on a static D), the author puts all of the form into stem on/off events. Boundaries at 1:15, 1:27, 1:40 fall on a bar grid in blocks of 24, 28 and 32 bars (M, bar one is tracker-derived). That is a deliberate, almost architectural decision, and it makes the piece legible on first listening.
- **Ambiguous mode as a feature.** D major versus D minor is a tie (0.76 against 0.756), because the third is missing. A bare D5 world sounds stern and neutral, which is exactly right for a documentary voice that must not be told how to feel. A major or minor third would have editorialised.
- **Voice placement by subtraction.** The narrator enters into space the band has cleared (1:18.9 after the bass leaves; 2:16 on the A, D, G walk) and exits at state changes (0:31.7, 2:47.8). The long voice-free block 1:27 to 2:15 coincides with the densest and thinnest sections. The story is told when the band is sparse, held back when it is heavy. That is good gain-staging as storytelling. (The voice is dry, centred, band-limited, and the band does not duck for it, which keeps it from sounding like a pop vocal and keeps the archive texture.)
- **The one melody is hidden.** The only pitch movement worth the name is the slow stepwise line over 1:15 to 1:36 (A, C, B, G, then D; pyin on a polyphonic stem, so M at best), placed precisely where the bass is gone and nothing anchors it. It is the "yearning" the track rations, and it exists only because the floor is missing.
- **The refrain sentence as a frame.** "Waar iedereen zich voor opmaakt, dat is de Elfstedentocht" at 0:27 to 0:31.7 and again 2:44 to 2:48.2 is the nearest thing to a chorus, and the second one ends in the hole. Whether it is the same recording is unproven (waveform correlation 0.09, DTW 0.278, L-M).
- **Drums that never move.** Because the kit is constant, every other change reads clearly. The one drum event therefore reads as seismic.

## 4. What is weak, or at least questionable

- **No harmonic argument.** If you listen for development rather than for layering, 3 minutes on one pedal is thin. The 2:00 to 2:40 A, D, G walk and the F, C, D, C run-up at 2:38 are the only harmonic motion, and they are short. A critic could say the form relies on arrangement tricks that are repeated (bass on/off three times, plus the thin block) rather than escalated.
- **The final sprint is a plateau.** It is the loudest, but it is steady for 20 s, and the "no densification after the stop" finding (about 5.3 drum onsets per second before and after) means the 2:48 release depends on timbre and width, not on a new rhythmic idea. The ending (taper, no cadence) leaves an unresolved feel that is probably intentional, given Part 2 follows, but as a standalone piece it does not close.
- **Tiny dynamic range.** About 8 LU of K-weighted short-term range, mostly at section ends; loudness is heavily limited. Contrast is made by width, bass and bandwidth, not by dynamics. That is why 1:40 only adds about 0.1 LU. It is a legitimate modern-rock mastering choice, but it means listeners on small speakers lose the sub thumps and some of the 1:40 weight.
- **Dutch speech over distorted rock is not always intelligible.** Whisper needs correction against the loud band. That is a mix choice (the band does not duck), good for texture, poor for information.
- **Repetition of device.** The 1:15 drop and the 2:01 pull-down are structurally similar (bass out, wall thinned, voice later). Whether the second is a variation or a repeat is arguable.

## 5. How music and voice or sample interact (summary)

The voice is free in time against the grid, except at its two exits. It never changes rate. It carries facts the band never comments on. The band's emotion runs on its own track, and the two meet at four places: the 0:31.7 exit, the 1:18.9 entry into cleared space, the 2:16 list on a chord walk, and the 2:47.8 hole. That is a clean, restrained arrangement for a spoken-word piece.

Cross-checking against the dossier's picture claims (taken as given, not re-opened): the bass return at 1:40 meets the emptiest, slowest picture (0.46 cuts per 10 s from 1:28 to 1:49), and the hole at 2:47.8 meets a whip-pan into a "FINISH" banner. Cut phase against the beat is not random (mean about 0.32 of a beat, Rayleigh p = 0.0055) but cuts are not snapped (7.8% within 80 ms against 19.7% by chance). From a music-first view, the picture is not following the arrangement; the arrangement is doing independent work, and the film occasionally rhymes with it.

## 6. Verdict

A confident, economical, arrangement-led piece whose emotional weight comes almost entirely from a handful of well-placed bass and voice switches (1:15, 1:40, 2:01 to 2:31, 2:47.8). Its strength is control; its limit is that the control is the whole argument. As an accompaniment to archive film it is nearly ideal: it holds the narrator at arm's length and lets the viewer supply the feeling.

## 7. Three questions for the other panellists

1. **To the film or picture reader:** at 1:40 the bass slams back after 24 s while the picture is at its emptiest and slowest (a white-out, the slowest cutting of the film). Did that mismatch read as deliberate counterpoint (weight against emptiness) or as the editor simply not following the music? Does the white-out make the arrival feel bigger or does it dilute it?
2. **To the narration or context reader:** the refrain sentence returns at 2:44 and ends into the 0.4 s hole before a "FINISH" banner. The music implies an ending the film never shows (we never see a winner or the line crossed). Is the "held breath then release" at 2:47.8 felt, by a viewer who knows the 1963 race, as arrival or as a false finish? Does the Dutch-language audience hear the refrain differently from someone who does not speak Dutch?
3. **To the emotion or human-response reader:** the band never lifts its third (no major or minor), the sprint ends on a taper with no cadence, and the narrator is never moved. Where, in your reading, does the sadness, pride or awe actually come from for a viewer: the stripped 1:55 to 2:14 block over the tea and the blank gaze at 1:58, the 1:40 landing, or something the picture adds that the music withholds? In other words, what in the film is not in the music?
