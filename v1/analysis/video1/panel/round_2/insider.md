# Panel round 2: the cultural insider and historian

I re-read the other five round 1 statements and re-opened f_0121 (the tea bench) and f_0010 (the Friese Koerier at 0:09) before writing. I cannot hear audio; sound claims come from `music_reading.md`. Where I add background knowledge not in our research notes I say so. Times are m:ss.

## 1. A correction to myself first

In round 1 I wrote that Dokkum, at 3:06.6, leaves "the longest stretch of the route (about 45 km) still to come". That is wrong, and our own research note shows it. The 1997 checkpoint list in `elfstedentocht_1963.md` has Franeker at 129 km, Dokkum at 174 and the finish at about 199. Franeker to Dokkum is the long leg, about 45 km. Dokkum to Leeuwarden is about 25 km, the last leg. So ending on the Dokkum sign puts us at the start of the final run-in, not "the longest stretch". The "threshold of the last leg" reading survives and is arguably stronger. Dokkum is also where, in the research's account, the rivals alleged "zuigers" (non-participants) had drafted Paping between Bartlehiem and Dokkum. That is a famous grievance, but nothing in the film points to it, so it is background and not reading.

## 2. Questions put to me

**Music critic, Q2: is the 2:47.8 hole felt as arrival or a false finish by someone who knows the race? Does a Dutch speaker hear the refrain differently?**

*Dutch hearing.* The refrain is "Waar iedereen zich voor opmaakt, dat is de Elfstedentocht" (0:27 and 2:44). A Dutch listener does not hear it as information, since everyone knows what the Elfstedentocht is. It sounds like a dry newsreel formula. That is the point for a Dutch ear: the words are ordinary announcement language, and the weight comes from what the band does under them. I would not say Dutch speakers hear a different sentence. I would say they hear a more banal one, so the sentence cannot "carry" the moment, and the hole carries it alone. This is my inference about listening, not data.

*Arrival or false finish.* From the picture at 2:48 I would say neither, and I must retract some of my round 1 warmth. I opened f_0169 again in round 1: a hand-painted "FINISH" sheet held up in front of a hooded crowd. In the 1963 race the finish was at Leeuwarden (Grote Wielen area, per the research), and the crowd standing at a hand-lettered sign is perfectly ordinary for any control point or finish. I cannot tell from the frame which place it is. So I do not know that this is "the" finish, and I should not have called it "the finish placed in the hole". What a knowing viewer gets is a word, "finish", that means two things at once: the race's goal and, for the thousands of amateurs, the goal most never reached (127 of 9,862 finished, research H/M). The sprint that follows then feels, to me, like the crowd's hope rather than a result. That is mine, and it fits the sceptic's demand that I mark it as interpretation.

**Symbolist Q2 and film critic Q2: is withholding the finish and opening on print PSB's habit, or the archive's?** These were addressed to research panellists, but I can say something as a historian. Our own research (`archive_sources.md`) says the footage is Frisian regional material, not Polygoon newsreel, and the shot order is unknown. The newspaper pages inside the film are a standard document of the day: Dutch newsreel and regional films routinely cut to press clippings. I cannot show that the editor chose them. The honest position is that "outcome told first" is a property of the material we see (two post-race front pages), and who placed them at the head of the film is open.

**First timer and sceptic: how much of this survives without Dutch and without knowing the race?** Mine to answer partly, and I made it too easy in round 1 by calling the roll-call "the heart of it". The sceptic is right that the town-list sequence works on a purely mechanical level: names are said, signs appear within about a second. That is satisfying with no Dutch at all. What the Dutch listener adds is two things: recognition (the eleven towns are a litany learnt at school and in winters of waiting) and the bilingual signs (BOLSWARD / BOALSERT, FRANEKER / FRJENTSJER), which a non-Dutch viewer will read as decoration. The "ghost and memory" readings, by contrast, are not Dutch readings at all. No insider source we hold says the film is about absence. That layer is ours.

I would add one from the insider side: I do not want the "16 winters of waiting" layer in any summary written as if it were what the film does. It is what a Frisian viewer in 2013 or today would bring to it.
- **Sceptic, on the tea face.** I re-opened f_0121. The curly-haired young person at right is looking at or just past the lens, expression neutral to tired, face washed pale by exposure. I called it "staring" in round 1; "looks toward the camera" is safer. What carries the frame is the row of cups: five people (two more at the left edge) drinking from white cups, a plate at lower centre, striped knitted caps, a woollen scarf. A cultural note, not in our research: hot drinks at control posts are an ordinary part of how the day is remembered, but I cannot say this particular scene is at a control post. It could be a hut, a café, or a tent.
- **Film critic and music critic, on the 1:40 counterpoint.** The facts are strong (bass back after 24 s, almost no cutting in 1:28 to 1:49). I think both can be reported: observed co-occurrence, no evidence of intent.

## 4. Where I push back

**Sceptic on "the tea bench is merely cups and breath, drop the human reading".** I accept dropping the gaze as "the emotional centre". I do not accept reducing the passage to neutral mechanics. The film's whole cast in 1:55 to 2:14 changes from effort to care: kettle, cups, frost-caked old man, ambulance, kneeling skater. That is a change of subject, from the racers to the people who keep them upright, and it is observable in the frames without any feeling claimed. In the Frisian telling of 1963 (research: spectators giving Paping a jacket and chocolate, Brinta porridge) the helpers are part of the story. The film's choice to give them its quietest music is something I can defend as design in the weak sense: the quietest passage coincides with the helpers. Whether the editor intended it is unknown.

**Symbolist, on the newspapers as "everyone on screen is already a memory".** Looking at f_0010 again: the Friese Koerier front page carries a photograph of a skater leaning into a blizzard, headline "Elfstedentocht bovenmenselijk zwaar", the box "Chaos aan de finis[h]", and in the film a pale ghost skater is superimposed over the page at 0:10 to 0:12. The page is a post-race report. That much is solid. But in 1963 the Dutch press of the Friday and Saturday was the main way the country understood the race (there was live television at points on the route, per the research on Bartlehiem, but most people followed it in print and on radio; the radio and press point is my background knowledge, not in our notes). So newsprint at the start can simply be "this is the event as the nation received it". The prolepsis reading is possible, but the mundane reading, that the films and the front pages were both part of the same 1963 media bundle, is at least as likely. I would downgrade "outcome told first" from device to observation.

**Symbolist and film critic, on "the film shows no finish, so this is a deliberate refusal".** Two historian's cautions. First, the footage is ordinary regional and press material; archive films of the 1963 tour mostly show start, route and the crowd, because most cameras were at fixed points. Not showing a finish may be the archive's coverage, not PSB's refusal (my background knowledge, flagged as such). Second, Part 1 is one of two pieces (research: Part 2 is calmer and more electronic, 5:04). A refusal of a finish in Part 1 may just be division of labour. I would not build a thesis on an absence in one half of a two-part work.

## 5. The thread I would develop: what the roll-call asks of a viewer

The most interesting thing for me, after reading the others, is that the film's two undisputedly tight picture-word alignments both concern naming: the date at 0:09 to 0:12 and the town list at 2:16 to 2:36. Everything else (cuts, bass, hole) is, on the sceptic's null test, indistinguishable from chance. So the one place the film plainly follows its own words is where the words are names of places and a date.

That matters culturally. In Dutch winter memory the eleven towns are the race: a list recited, not a map. A recital works through repetition and order, which is exactly what a flat narrator, a fixed pulse and a one-sign-per-name edit produce. The music critic's point that the narrator is never moved, and the band's pedal D that never lifts its third, are not a lack of feeling; they are how a litany is delivered. If I put it as a testable claim: the film's formal grammar (list, repetition, flat voice, drone) is that of a litany or roll-call, and the same grammar is why the sprint at 2:48 can be loud and unresolved without needing a finish. This is INT, not sourced. It is compatible with the sceptic's minimal reading (the best-aligned thing is the town list) and does not need ghosts.

Two checks it invites, both for round 3 if anyone can do them. (a) The sign order in the film against the route order: from 2:16 to 2:36 the dossier gives IJlst (2:18), Stavoren (about 2:24, the inn front "Vrouwe van Stavoren"), Workum (2:27), Bolsward (2:29), Franeker (2:32), Dokkum (2:34). The route order is IJlst, Stavoren, Hindeloopen, Workum, Bolsward, Franeker, Dokkum. If the narration gives that order, the film is reciting the route, not collaging towns. Hindeloopen was in round 1 listed among the names, but I did not see its sign; I should not have implied we see all eight. (b) Whether "Leeuwarden" is the last name spoken, as the route would have it. The music reading says that word's timing is uncertain by up to 3 s.

## 7. Summary of my position after round 2

- **Withdrawn or downgraded:** the "45 km still to come" claim (wrong leg); "finish placed in the hole" (the banner's location is unknown); "staring" gaze (neutral look); "outcome told first" as device (observation only); "all eight towns seen" (Hindeloopen not seen by me).
- **Held:** the town list is the film's tightest, most culturally legible alignment; the 1:55 to 2:14 passage shifts the film's subject from racers to helpers; the "waiting since 1997" layer is a viewer's, not the film's.
- **Developed:** the litany grammar of the piece, offered as interpretation with a route-order test.
