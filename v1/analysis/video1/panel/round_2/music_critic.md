# Music critic and producer: Round 2 (video1, PSB "Elfstedentocht Part 1")

Basis: my round 1, the other five round 1 statements, `music_reading.md`, and frames I opened this round (f_0101, f_0102, f_0168, f_0169). I still cannot hear audio; "feel" means a reasoned inference from measurements. OBS = seen or measured, INT = interpretation.

## 1. Answers to the questions put to me

**Film critic, First-timer, Insider (all on 1:40): does the arrival need the empty picture, would it feel smaller over a busy shot, and does it depend on the long absence?**
- It depends on the absence, almost entirely. The measured step is about 35 dB in the bass stem (-53.8 to -19.1 dB, 1 s bins), but only about +0.1 LU overall. A bass line is not "big" in itself. It is big because the ear has had 24 s of nothing under it, with the wall surging from 1:27 (width 0.78) to raise the expectation. Play 1:40 cold and you hear a competent rock groove; play 1:15 to 1:40 first and you hear a floor return. So yes: the arrival is made in the audio alone, and the picture cannot be its cause.
- Would a busy picture make it smaller? I think probably slightly, by competing for attention at the moment the low end should be felt. But I cannot test this, and I would not claim the white frame "makes it bigger". Best statement: a quiet picture does not get in the way. That is weaker than "counterpoint".
- Insider's stem question: if the bass was muted programmatically, does "arrival" change? To a listener, no. What the listener hears is the final mix. The flat -65 dB floor with no ring-out fits a muted or gated stem, but demucs gates quiet stems itself (music reading, M-L), so I cannot say whether this was a studio mute, a gated synth, or a band stopping and starting. The 24/28/32-bar grid says it was planned on a grid; that is all I can claim. If the bass were a played instrument that simply stopped, I would expect some note decay; the absence of that favours an edit or a sequenced part (INT, M-L).

**Sceptic: name one picture-music alignment that survives a null test; and which feelings survive phone speakers and no Dutch?**
- I concede the alignment point. I cannot name one that survives. The Sceptic's count (3 of 9 section boundaries with a cut within 0.5 s, against about 25% by chance) is roughly chance, and 7.8% of cuts within 80 ms of a beat against 19.7% expected is, if anything, avoidance. I said in round 1 that "the film occasionally rhymes with the arrangement". I should have said it rhymes no more than chance would give. I withdraw "rhyme" as evidence of design and keep only: the picture is not following the arrangement.
- The one alignment I would still defend is not a cut: it is the town list (2:16 to 2:36), where words and signs agree within about 1 s. That is picture following voice, not music, and the Insider and Sceptic both rate it. It tells us the editor cut to the narration.
- Feelings by dependency (my own ranking, all INT):
  - Survive phone speakers and no Dutch: 1:15 (bass-out thinning; the mix drops 4 to 6 dB and the wall goes bright, audible on any speaker), 1:27 to 1:40 lift (width doubles, wall surges), 2:48 sprint (loudest, brightest), the voice exit at 0:31.7 (bar-line stop), the 2:47.8 hole (a drum gap is audible anywhere, though only 0.4 s).
  - Partly survive: 1:40 (a laptop will carry the 100 to 200 Hz body of the bass but not any sub; the surge and the width change should still register as "fuller").
  - Do not survive: the 2:01 to 2:31 thumps (42.6 Hz, effectively absent on a phone), and the refrain's meaning, the town-list humour and any sense of "Frisian". Those need Dutch or a subwoofer.
  - Picture-dependent, not mine to claim: the tea moment.

**First-timer: does the 1:15 to 1:40 line register as a melody?**
Probably not as a tune. It is a pyin trace on a polyphonic stem (A, C, B, G, then D; music reading M), and the intervals against D (fifth, flat seven, sixth, fourth, tonic) are a slow descent-and-resolve of a kind. It will register as "a line over no floor", something the ear follows because nothing else moves. That is a texture function more than a melody function. I would stop calling it "the one melody" and say "the one moving line". I over-sold it in round 1.

## 2. Where I agree, push back, and concede

**Agree with the Sceptic** that "guitar" is an inference; I have used "the wall" where I can and will keep to it. Also agree that a feeling claim built on one mechanism (what plays, what does not) is a hypothesis, not a finding. Nobody on the panel has heard a listener's response.

**Push back on the Sceptic's "the same thing would look the same" test for 1:40.** The Sceptic asks what the film would look like had the editor not intended contrast. Fair: the answer is "the same", so intent is unproven. But the Sceptic's alternative ("a long quiet shot happened to sit there") is also unproven, and 1:28 to 1:49 shows the picture itself changing rate (0.46 cuts per 10 s, against 7.58 at 1:06 to 1:16). Both are explanations of the cutting, not of the pairing. I would put it as: coincidence cannot be excluded; deliberate contrast cannot be shown; the audio effect does not depend on either.

**Correct the panel on the frame.** Several round 1 notes (mine included, second-hand; Film critic, Symbolist, First-timer, Insider) call f_0101 a "white-out" or "near white". Opening it: it is a pale but legible windswept dune landscape, with a horizon of scrubby ridge, a scratch line down the frame (x about 210 px of 600) and a snow-blown foreground. It is bright, not blank. The Sceptic saw "no figures"; f_0102 (second 1:41) shows two small dark skaters at the left, one nearer. So the dossier is right that two tiny figures arrive one second after the bass. The accurate description is: wide, windy, small people, sparse, not empty. That matters musically because "weight on emptiness" is a stronger claim than "weight on a wide, thin landscape with two figures entering". I would drop "white-out" from our shared vocabulary.

**Concede (own error).** In round 1 I wrote that the hole "carries the weight of an entire arrangement vocabulary" and called it "seismic". That treats rarity as size. It is 0.38 s long, the guitar holds, and the loop phase is unchanged (music reading). On a phone it is a very short dropout. I still rate it as a good device because it sits at a sentence end, but "seismic" was rhetoric. "A neat, brief catch" is what I can defend. I also concede that my "the producer meant 1:15 and 1:40 as a pair" is inference from the grid; the three bass events fall at 24/28/32-bar spacing, and that is the only evidence.

**Concede to the Film critic's thread on the 2:47 to 2:49 area, and add something I had missed.** f_0168 (2:47) is a smeared whip-pan over a packed crowd with, at lower right, a goggled figure in a pale helmet-like hood raising a gloved hand (OBS; the meaning of the gesture is not clear and I would not read it as a victory salute). f_0169 (2:48) is the hand-held FINISH banner with a tightly packed crowd behind it. So the music's hole, if it lands where it seems to, sits between a blur and a banner, which gives the whip-pan itself a kind of rest. At 1 fps I cannot say whether the banner appears in or after the hole, and I will join the Film critic and Sceptic in not saying "perfectly timed".

## 3. The thread I find most interesting: why the arrangement is grid-locked and the picture is not

The sharpest fact the panel has produced, taken together, is a pair of opposite measurements:
- The music is exactly gridded: sections at 1:15, 1:27, 1:40 are 24, 28 and 32 bars apart (bar one tracker-derived, so M), harmony changes only at stem boundaries (music reading), the hole is within 0.1 s of the grid.
- The picture is not gridded: cuts sit on average about 0.32 of a beat late, only 7.8% within 80 ms of a beat, 3 of 9 boundaries within 0.5 s (chance level), and no cut at 1:15.3, 1:40.0 or 2:47.8.

That pattern is what you would expect if the two were made separately: a band record built on a grid, and a cut that follows words and footage. The one place the edit demonstrably tracks anything is the narration (the town signs, the refrain on 0:27 and 2:44 as returns). So I would now say, more cautiously than in round 1, that this is a case of a finished band track and a narrated film that were fitted rather than co-composed (INT, supported by the above but unproven; the Insider's and Film critic's research questions are the way to test it, see below). The loudness-motion correlation (r 0.27 to 0.32, but with autocorrelation and picture leading by about 4 s, per Sceptic) cannot carry any more than "broadly, the busy bits are loud bits".

If true, it also answers the Symbolist and Film critic's question (deliberate withholding or following?): "withholding" implies a prior intention to cut on events that were then declined. Nothing here suggests that. What would distinguish the two hypotheses: if PSB made the music first and then cut to it, the edit would likely carry some beat or bar structure; it does not. If the film was cut first and the music written to it, the bass events might line up with picture events; they do not either, except by chance. The simplest reading is that the audio and the picture were each built for their own logic and then joined at the narration. The only testable prediction from the sound side is: the 0:31.7 voice exit and the 2:47.8 hole, the two points where the narrator's line ends against a band event, are the places where voice and band were most deliberately fitted. That would be shown if an unedited version of the voice sample (the archive source) ended at the same place; the research panel may be able to check.

## 4. Smaller points

- **Insider on the Frisian versus world-music frame.** From the sound alone, no regional marker survives: D pedal, 78 BPM, distorted wall, a flat narrator. The Dutch identity lives entirely in the speech and the signs. As music, it is generic post-rock; as a film, it is regional. I would not call it either as a whole.
- **Sceptic on "the effort continues offscreen" at 3:08.** Agreed it is poetic. What the audio supports is "there is no cadence and a 0.4 s taper". The stop is probably the end of an edit for Part 2 (INT).
- **First-timer's "the middle sags".** Fair, and consistent with my round 1 weakness list (no harmonic argument, repeated devices). I add that the drums never changing is both the identity of the piece and its main risk with a listener who does not lock into the flutter.
