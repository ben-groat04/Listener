## 1. Answering the questions put to me

**Sceptic and Symbolist: is the town list in route order, and are Sneek and Sloten absent?** I can now answer from the transcript notes, with a caveat. `words.md` gives the voice as Sneek, IJlst, Sloten, Stavoren, Hindeloopen, Workum, Bolsward, Harlingen, Franeker, Dokkum, Leeuwarden (2:16 to 2:35). The research route (1997 checkpoint list, HIGH) is Leeuwarden, Sneek, IJlst, Sloten, Stavoren, Hindeloopen, Workum, Bolsward, Harlingen, Franeker, Dokkum, Leeuwarden. So the spoken list is the route in order, with the start town moved to the end to close the loop. Caveats: the transcript is medium-low confidence in places (Whisper, "Loftwacht" for Bolsward) and the prompted pass could echo the names I supplied, though the un-prompted pass produced garbled versions in the same order. Also, I was wrong in round 2 to put the narration order in doubt: it is the sceptic who guessed rightly that it follows the route.

What does that tell us? Less than I hoped. A narrator reciting the eleven towns would give route order whatever the editor did; it is how every Frisian schoolchild learns them. Order is therefore evidence that the narration is an ordinary documentary list, not that PSB composed a litany. The editorial evidence is the other half: signs seen are IJlst (2:18), the Vrouwe van Stavoren inn (2:24), Workum (2:27), Bolsward/Boalsert (2:29), Franeker/Frjentsjer (2:32), Dokkum (2:34), with a town view at 2:30 for Harlingen. Those fall in route order and within about a second of each word, but I have not seen signs for Sneek, Sloten or Hindeloopen, so the picture covers six or seven of the eleven names. This answers the sceptic's second part: Sneek and Sloten are not shown on signs in what I have seen (I have not scanned every frame between 2:16 and 2:20). Someone found signs to fit the words, and that someone cut to the voice. Whether it was PSB or the archive's own film is unknown. I keep "tightest alignment" but drop "design signal for the band".

**Sceptic: do the two refrains sound like a newsreel's opening and closing line?** I cannot hear them and I have no archive recording. A Dutch ear (background) would recognise "Waar iedereen zich voor opmaakt" as a stock announcer's sentence, one that suits a film's opening or sign-off. That makes the sceptic's guess plausible, and it fits the 0.09 waveform correlation. I would rank it above "deliberate refrain" but I cannot test it. I accept the downgrade.

**Symbolist: would Frisian viewers hear the roll-call as elegy?** My honest answer is: partly, but not mainly. The recital is a litany, and litanies are solemn in form, but its content is names of towns with their signs, and the picture on f_0150 is a plain sign with a car blurred beneath it. A Frisian viewer likely hears fond recognition first and loss second, if at all, and only if they bring the 1997 date with them. I said "elegy" is a reading of mine, and I hold it as second-order. Neither comedy (First-timer, Film critic) nor elegy is in the data; "affectionate recital" is what I can defend.

**Symbolist: is BOLSWARD / BOALSERT marked for a Frisian eye?** Looking at f_0150: a black board, white capitals, the Dutch name over the Frisian, equal size, no decoration. For 1963 I believe (background, unsourced here) that bilingual place-name signs were not yet the official norm; Dutch-only signs prevailed and Frisian names were added later, with formal bilingual policy coming after. If that is right, a Frisian-name sign in 1963 would be a local or event-made sign, perhaps made for the tour, and a knowing eye might read it as a quiet statement. I cannot verify it from our files and I do not know who made this sign or when. So: possibly marked, unproven, and not to be used in the conclusion. The honest minimum is that the frame shows both names at equal weight, and a viewer who reads Frisian gets a second name.

**Film critic (aerial oblique at 1:44 to 1:46) and Music critic (source of narration).** I have no source that says what was shot from the air or from a vehicle, and no archive timing for the sentence. `archive_sources.md` says only that the material is Frisian Film Archive footage and voice-over, and that the Polygoon source is downgraded to LOW. ## 2. Where I agree, concede and push back

- **Concede to the Sceptic:** "outcome told first" is an observation, not a device; the FINISH banner (f_0169) is a hand-lettered sheet held before a hooded crowd, location unknown, and its word is English "FINISH" (which Dutch also uses, so it is no marker of place). The absence of a finish is an absence in a 3:09 excerpt of a two-part work, and I withdraw "the unseen finish is a kind of truth" as a finding. It was a sentence, not evidence.
- **Concede to the Music critic:** no regional marker survives in the sound alone; the Frisian identity lives in the speech, signs and place names. As music, generic; as film, regional.
- **Agree with Symbolist and First-timer:** what survives without Dutch is weather, effort, the roll of names, the helpers' passage. The "waiting since 1997" layer is a viewer's, not the film's.
- **Push back, gently, on the Sceptic's "null result".** The null tests concern *music and picture* (cuts, loudness, motion). The film does demonstrably follow its voice. A film can be badly synchronised to its band and well synchronised to its narrator, and that is what a fitted archive film with a finished band track looks like. That is not nothing: the narration is the film's spine, the band is the engine, and the two were fitted, not co-made. I take the Music critic's formulation.
- **Hold:** 1:55 to 2:14 turns from racers to helpers (kettle, cups, ambulance, kneeling skater), observed and measurably the quietest passage. Whether intended is unknown.

## 3. The thread I would develop: a recital that closes a loop

The new fact from this round is the list's shape. The narrator begins at Sneek (the first stop after the start) and ends on Leeuwarden, the start and finish town, so the recital is a lap: it leaves Leeuwarden in spirit and returns to it. Dokkum then appears again as the final picture (3:06, the sign), and, as I corrected in round 2, Dokkum is the threshold of the last leg to Leeuwarden, about 25 km. So the film names the whole circuit by 2:35 and then, 30 seconds before the end, shows the last town before the end. That much is observation plus route geography. It is a modest claim: the film's last town is the penultimate checkpoint, so the film stops one leg short of closing the loop its own list has already described (INT). It is the one place I find the absence and the list working together, and it needs no ghosts, no 1997, no Dutch beyond reading a sign. A viewer who knows the route feels the lap is unfinished; a viewer who does not sees a skater and a sign.

## 4. Settled reading (five sentences)

1. This is a Frisian regional archive film of the 1963 Elfstedentocht, with its own narrator, fitted to a finished Public Service Broadcasting track; the film follows its voice closely and the band only loosely.
2. The strongest cultural content is in names and things: the date and the sentence "what everyone prepares for", the newspapers, the bibs and paperwork, the eleven towns recited in true route order and shown on signs within a second.
3. The film's quietest, humanest passage (1:55 to 2:14) is about the helpers, not the racers: cups, kettle, the ambulance, the kneeling skater, with no face we can read for feeling.
4. The "ghost", "memory" and "16 winters of waiting" layers are what a Frisian or well-informed viewer brings, in 2013 or now; they are legitimate as reception, not as findings about the film.
5. The film ends on the Dokkum sign, one leg short of Leeuwarden, and I read that as a piece that recites the whole circuit but stops at the edge of the last stretch, leaving the viewer to finish it (interpretation).

## 5. The single moment

**2:29 to 2:34**, the sign run from BOLSWARD / BOALSERT to FRANEKER / FRJENTSJER to DOKKUM, each appearing within about a second of its spoken name. It is the one place where voice, picture and place agree, it is what a Dutch ear recognises as a litany and a non-Dutch ear hears as a pleasing rhythm, and it needs nothing from us beyond looking and listening.
