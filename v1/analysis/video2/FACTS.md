## 0. What the thing is (safe frame)

- Public Service Broadcasting, "Elfstedentocht Part 2" (about five minutes; released autumn 2013; reportedly commissioned for the 50th anniversary of the 18 January 1963 race, single source). The film is colour archive footage of the Frisian winter and a mass skating race, credited in the notes to the Fries Film Archief. Which reels, and who the narrator is, are NOT known.
- There is no sung vocal, no tune and no chorus. The music is one steady, loop-built electronic groove with a dark held hum under it. The one human voice is a Dutch archive narrator (Dutch by the words heard, see section 2), who speaks until 2:36 and does not speak in sentences again.
- The picture has no credits, no named person, no finish line, no winner. Mostly ordinary or cheerful pictures; any sadness comes from the pairing with the music.

## 2. The voice: what can and cannot be said

Can be said (sure unless marked):
- Language is Dutch. **[FIXED]** Not 'identified with full confidence': the 1.00 came from a run where Dutch was forced. Automatic detection gave English 0.60 on the mix (small model) and Welsh 0.57 on the voice stem, so detection is unreliable on this archive sound. Dutch rests on the words heard and the forced-Dutch transcript (words such as "januari", "kilometer", "graden", "Elfstedentocht" are recognisable).
- It is spoken, not sung, and in a free rhythm of its own. It does not keep time with the beat; it meets the beat only where a sentence ends (at 1:28 and 2:36).
- It is a dry, flat, matter-of-fact reading, at a fairly even pace, in a low, narrow pitch range that wanders a little inside each phrase. The tone is thin and narrow, in the manner of old film sound (probable).

## 3. The shape of the music, in plain words

A very quiet opening of about 28 seconds: one dark, hushed hum with a few low, long-tailed thuds, no beat and no voice. Then at 0:28 a bass and a thin drum pattern walk in under the same hum. A dry archive voice enters at 0:42 and leads; the band holds back behind it, the drums dropping out for a few seconds here and there. At about 1:24 the drums and bass go out entirely and the voice names the event over the hum alone. At 1:28.6 everything returns together, much louder in the drums, with a kick on every beat. That is the one big event in the whole piece.

From there it never changes speed. It is a steady walking-pace pulse (two beats a second), with a bass pattern that sits slightly against the kick so it feels like a firm step with a slight limp. The narrator speaks over it in his or her own time until the head-count ends at 2:36, then never speaks in sentences again. At 2:36.6 a new loop begins and a lighter layer thins out, as if the room has opened.

For about the next two minutes the groove hardly changes: nothing is announced, but a bright, airy layer slowly opens up, short voice-like fragments start to come round the bar from about 3:29, and the whole thing gets a little louder, peaking around 3:40 to 4:36 without ever speeding up. It strains forward without going faster.

The ending is taking apart, not a climax: at 4:36.8 the bright layer falls away; at about 4:48.5 the bass is chopped into short stabs and the hum goes hollow; at 5:00.8 the groove stops dead and the hum rings on alone; two knocks land at about 5:02.3 and then silence. It reads as withdrawal and stopping, not arrival.

Tonality (only if needed): the hum sits on B, with a minor-sounding set of notes; an E-centred hearing is equally possible. It never changes key or resolves. Do not name chords.
