# Nothing happens, and I couldn't look away

I came out of this one braced and a little hollow. Five minutes of an unhurried beat under pictures of people skating through a Frisian winter, and by the end I felt the way you do when someone has been in a room with you for a long time without speaking, and then leaves.

The moment that stays is the quiet one just before the drums come back. The music had been walking along with a narrator reading over it. Then the drums dropped out, then the low notes, and there was only a dark hum and that one flat voice saying that this, what everyone is getting ready for, is the Elfstedentocht. On screen, a long still shot of a snowy road with footprints in the foreground. Only the little knot of people moved, slowly, towards me. It went on a beat longer than was comfortable, and then everything slammed back at once, the drums far louder than anything before them, and the picture jumped to a bridge so crowded the people were a smear. It was like a door kicked open on a quiet house. I actually sat back. After that the drums never dropped out again. The piece does get louder and brighter over the next couple of minutes, but nothing else hits like that.

It opens so quietly I thought something was wrong. A dark board with white capitals, De Winter van 1963 in Friesland, and one low thud that took ages to fade. Then frost on branches against a grey-blue sky, held far longer than a modern edit would dare, with a few slow thuds drifting over it. I liked that. It asks you to settle. When the beat finally arrives it lands exactly on a cut, from a bright blue park to bare black trees against a lilac sky, and the whole thing starts to walk.

The voice is the other thing I keep thinking about. It does not sing and it does not keep time with anything. It is dry and even and a bit thin, a dry, thin, archive-sounding reading, the sort of voice you might half-remember from an old film, though that is only a likeness. It goes through dates and distances in its own rhythm while the music goes about its business underneath. "Eighteen January 1963." Sixteen degrees below. More than two hundred kilometres. Then, about half-way through, it reads out the numbers: 575 racers, about 9,500 tour skaters, and a list I could not make out. It says them in the same tone it used for the temperature. The voice gives no sign of excitement or worry that I could hear, so the feeling has to come from you. When it finished, the music shifted underneath and the picture cut to the first shot from a moving car, along a road lined with frosted white trees. After that the voice never spoke in sentences again, and I missed it more than I expected.

The music itself is a steady, patient pace with a slight limp in it. It put me in mind of a slow march, or of the long middle stretches of those minimal electronic records where the point is that nothing changes and you stop waiting for it to. I will admit that for a stretch in the middle I was bored. Skaters on dark glossy ice, farmhouses, a telegraph post, low light; the same groove, nothing announced. But somewhere in there it had been getting brighter and louder without ever speeding up, and by the time the camera was in among the skaters, close behind dark figures bent forward with their arms held behind their backs and white numbers on their backs, the cuts coming quick, I was leaning towards the screen. It pulled forward without hurrying, like being towed.

There are faces in there, and I looked for them. A young man seemed to glance towards the camera in a flurry of other faces and was gone. Coated onlookers stood at a barrier by a road sign. A figure in a long coat walked away across blue ice. Nobody is followed, nobody is named, and nobody wins. The pictures are mostly ordinary, and the faces that can be seen mostly look cheerful or relaxed. If it is sad, and I think it is, the music is doing that, not the pictures.

The last stretch, from about 4:37, is what I will remember. The bright shimmer on top falls away and the beat goes on a bit bare. A little later the low notes are cut into short jabs, a single wordless cry keeps coming round, and the hum underneath turns hollow. The camera drops to a skater's legs over scratched ice, close and unsteady. Then, for the first time, it turns away from people altogether: ducks and gulls on grey-blue water, then standing on broken ice, then cracks, then slabs of ice on rose-brown water. The ice was not a floor any more.

And then the groove stops dead. The hum rings on alone for a second or so, which felt much longer. Two knocks, close together, with a low note under them. The picture dims as they sound and goes black a moment after the sound has gone. It felt less like an ending than like something being taken away.

Outside sources give roughly 10,000 starters (one says 9,862) and 127 finishers. I did not know that while watching, and it did not change what I felt. This is a very patient piece of music laid over a lot of people out on the ice, and it never tells you how to feel about them. I did not like it all the way through; the middle asked a lot of me. But I would sit through the middle again for the room going quiet at the road, and for the knocks at the end.
