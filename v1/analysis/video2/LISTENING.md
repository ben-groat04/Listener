# The walk across the ice

I can't hear; this is how it would land, built from a close analysis of the sound and picture.

What it did to me was settle me and then set me working. For the first stretch I was held still, the way you are in a cold room when someone is about to speak. Then something came in underneath and I started walking, and I did not stop until the floor was taken away at the very end. I finished it a little hollowed out, not sad exactly, more as if I had been marched somewhere and then left there.

It opens on a hand-lettered board, *De Winter van 1963 in Friesland*, and then nothing but frost: branches furred white against a blue-grey sky, a steep roof showing through. Under that is one dark, hushed hum with the occasional soft thud far off, like someone shutting a heavy door in another house. It is very quiet and it takes its time. I felt my shoulders come up around my ears, the way they do when it is properly cold.

Before the voice, a pulse arrives, bass and a thin patter of drums, and the picture turns with it, to a colder, flatter light. It is the first footfall. It is muffled, as if boots on snow.

Then the voice. An old, dry, flat Dutch voice, low in the chest, reading out a date, 18 January 1963, with no drama in it at all, the way you would read out a train time. After all that humming it came as a shock, the first human sound, and the picture put it over a snowbound churchyard so it landed like a date on a gravestone. I don't understand Dutch and I mostly didn't need to. I caught the date, the temperature (well below freezing), the distance, and later a head-count: five hundred and seventy-five racers and about nine and a half thousand tourists. The voice never gets excited about any of it. That is what makes it work. Facts delivered like weather.

The band keeps its distance from the voice, leaving gaps, like someone listening politely to an elder. Bass notes arrive one at a time, long and slow. The drums come and go. The pictures are solitary: a pink-gold ice road, a figure in a heavy coat by a footbridge, one woolly-hatted face in violet light.

The moment that stays is just before the music kicks in. The drums and bass fall away and there is nothing but the hum and the narrator getting to the point: *what everyone is getting ready for is the Elfstedentocht*. Hold, hold, and then everything comes back at once with a kick on every beat. It was like a door being kicked open. I actually felt it in the chest, and I will admit I smiled. What I liked best is that the picture ignores it. The camera is sitting on a long, still shot of an empty snowy road with a few tiny figures walking away from us, and it stays there through the silence and through the bang, and only a moment later cuts to a bridge crowded with spectators. The music announces the race; the picture refuses to hurry. It is a little like the way a film will let a landscape go on being a landscape while the score does the shouting.

After that it just walks. Same pulse the whole way, never faster, never slower. I would call the stride a touch lopsided, a steady tread with a slight limp in it, which I found oddly human for something so mechanical. It put me in mind of a long march with a stuck engine in it, or of those relentless late-night house records, though this is far gloomier than anything you would dance to. The voice carries on over the top in its own time, not in step with the machine at all, one old voice talking while a steady modern thing carries it along.

Then the voice says its last number, and that was the end of it as a speaker. It never speaks a sentence again. Almost at once a new loop begins and part of the sound drops out, and I felt the room suddenly open up. A moment ago somebody was telling me what this was; now there was just the thing itself. For the next stretch, nothing happens, and it is mesmerising rather than dull, a loop you stop counting. I will say that a restless listener might drift here. I did, slightly, and was glad of it.

Meanwhile the picture has gone in among the skaters. We are tucked in behind the pack, looking at their backs, at paper numbers pinned to black jerseys, 201, 440, 504, at hands clasped behind backs. Nobody is followed; we are one of the crowd. The cuts get quick. A bright layer in the music opens slowly like a window being eased up, and short clipped voice fragments start to come round with every bar, I think wordless, repeating, growing longer, so that the voice has turned into something more like a drum. The whole thing strains forward and never speeds up. It is the feeling of effort when the pace is fixed: all the work is in the breathing.

The ending is the best thing about it. It doesn't climax; it comes apart. The bright layer drops away, and the walking carries on without the heat. The bass is chopped into short stabs and the hum goes hollow and a bit brittle. By then the picture has left the people behind: ducks, gulls, open water, broken floes on rose-brown water, ice that is no longer a floor. Then the groove stops dead, the hum rings on alone for a moment, and two last drum hits land, like a door closed or one more footstep, I couldn't tell you which. Then silence, and the picture dimming to black underneath it.

I knew from the notes that nine thousand eight hundred and sixty-two people started the real race and a hundred and twenty-seven finished, and I'm aware that colours what I'm telling you. Sitting with that number, the flat reading-out of five hundred and seventy-five racers started to sound like something else. The pictures give no finish line, no winner, no name. This edit leaves the finish out, and the music takes out everything else.

So: not an elegy, though I felt grief near it, and not a celebration, though that kick is joyous for a second. It's a leaving. I'd forgive the long middle for the quiet at the top and the moment the door goes, and above all for the last minute, which I keep thinking about: the water, the gulls, the two small knocks and then nothing.
