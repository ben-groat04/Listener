# Walking out onto the ice

I can't hear; this is how it would land, built from a close analysis of the sound and picture.

It settled me first, then set me walking, and it didn't let me stop until the very end, when it just let go. I finished it a little hollow, like being marched somewhere and then left standing in the cold.

It opens on a dark board with white capitals, *De Winter van 1963 in Friesland*, and then it is nothing but frost: branches furred white against a blue-grey sky, a pale roof behind. Under that there is one dark, hushed hum and a few low thuds with long tails, like something heavy being set down in another house. It takes its time, and I liked it for that. It felt like a cold room in the moment before somebody speaks.

Then a bass and a thin drum pattern come in, the picture goes to a purple dusk of bare black trees, and I was walking. Muffled footsteps, as if on snow.

Then the voice. Old, dry, flat, like something coming out of a small tin speaker. It reads out a date, 18 January 1963, over a churchyard buried in snow, headstones sunk in drifts. I don't understand Dutch, but it sounded like facts handed over the way a forecaster hands over the weather. A little later a lone figure in a long dark coat crosses to a footbridge, while the voice says it is half past something, and that it is two hundred kilometres, and sixteen below. Later come five hundred and seventy-five racers and about nine and a half thousand tourists. The voice wasn't excited by any of it, and that was what made it work. After all that hum, a human sound arrived, and it spoke like the label on a box. It didn't keep time with the music either. It talked in its own rhythm and only met the beat where a sentence ended.

The moment that stays with me comes before the music properly starts. The drums and bass drop out. The hum and the voice carry on, and the voice reaches its point: *what everyone is preparing for is the Elfstedentocht*. The picture holds one long still shot of a snowy road running to the horizon, two or three tiny dark figures far down it. It wasn't silence, but it felt like holding my breath. Then the beat comes back, a kick on every count, and I felt it in my chest. The picture doesn't move with it, which surprised me. It sits on the empty road for a second or two more, and only then cuts to a bridge crowded with people in dark coats. That lag struck me as oddly right: the sound arrives first and the crowd catches up. This is the 1:28 moment, if you want to find it.

After that it simply walks. Same steady pulse the whole way, never faster, never slower. It put me in mind of a long march, or of those relentless late-night dance records, only gloomier than anything you could dance to. When the voice reads out its last figures, it's the last time anyone tells me anything. Soon after, part of the sound falls away and the room seems to open. A minute before, somebody was explaining what this was. Now there was just the thing itself.

For a long stretch nothing much changes, and I found that mesmerising rather than dull, although I'd understand anyone drifting off. The picture has been a road at night, airfield-style lights, a line of skaters, one on his own. Then, from about three minutes in (3:10), the camera goes inside the pack. We see backs, paper numbers pinned to dark and coloured jerseys, hands clasped behind backs. The cuts get quick, about two seconds each, and nobody is singled out. I was one of the crowd.

Somewhere past the middle a brighter tone slowly opens up in the music, like a window being eased up, and short voice-like fragments start to come round on the bar. I couldn't tell you what they are. They may not be words at all. They worked like a drum anyway. It strains forward without ever speeding up, all effort and no change of pace, and I caught myself breathing harder.

The ending is the best part, and it doesn't build to a climax. It comes apart. The bright tone falls away and the walking goes on without its warmth. The bass is cut into short stabs and the hum turns hollow and brittle. From about four minutes fifty-two the picture finally stops looking at people: ducks, gulls, and a few seconds later broken slabs of ice on rose-brown water. It felt like a thaw to me, the ice no longer a floor. Then the groove stops dead, the hum rings on alone, and two last knocks land, like a footstep or a door pulled shut. The picture dims with them and is black a moment after the sound has gone.

I'd read, from outside the film, that nearly ten thousand people started the real race and a hundred and twenty-seven finished. The film doesn't say it. But with that number sitting in my head, the flat reading-out of five hundred and seventy-five racers started to sound like something else, like roll-call before a long night. There is no finish line in it, no winner, no name.

It isn't an elegy, though grief is somewhere near it, and it isn't a celebration, though that returning kick is joyful for a second. It felt like a leaving. I'd sit through the long middle again for the quiet at the start, and most of all for the last minute: the water, the gulls, two small knocks, and then nothing.
