# Nothing happens, and I couldn't look away

I came out of this one braced and a little hollow. Five minutes of an unhurried beat under pictures of people skating through a Frisian winter, and by the end I felt the way you do when someone has been in a room with you for a long time without speaking, and then leaves.

The moment that stays is the quiet one just before the drums come back. For a good while the music had been walking along quietly, with a narrator reading facts over it. Then the drums dropped out, then the bass, and there was only a dark hum and that one flat voice saying that this, what everyone is getting ready for, is the Elfstedentocht. On screen, a long still shot of a snowy road with footprints in the foreground, and a little knot of people walking slowly towards me. Nothing else moved. It went on a beat longer than was comfortable, and then everything slammed back at once, the drums far louder than anything before them, and the picture jumped to a bridge so crowded the people were a smear. It was like a door kicked open on a quiet house. I actually sat back. The drums never again return as abruptly as that, though the whole piece does get louder later, over the last couple of minutes.

It opens so quietly I thought something was wrong. A dark board with white capitals, De Winter van 1963 in Friesland, with something pale and wooden-looking behind it, and under it one low thud that took ages to fade. Then frost on branches against a grey-blue sky, held far longer than a modern edit would dare, a few slow thuds drifting over it. I liked that. It asks you to settle. When the beat finally arrives it lands on a cut, from a bright blue park to bare black trees against a lilac sky, and the whole thing starts to walk.

The voice is the other thing I keep thinking about. It does not sing and it does not keep time with anything. It is dry and flat and a bit thin, the sound of a public-information film, reading out dates and distances in its own rhythm while the music goes about its business underneath. "Eighteen January 1963." Sixteen degrees below. More than two hundred kilometres. Then, in a long run from about half-way through the film, it reads out the numbers: five hundred and seventy-five racers, about nine and a half thousand tour skaters. It says them with exactly the face it used for the temperature. Nobody in the soundtrack is excited and nobody is worried, so the feeling has to come from you. When the last number was out, the music shifted underneath and the picture cut to the first shot from a moving car, along a road lined with frosted white trees. After that, the voice never spoke in sentences again, and I missed it more than I expected.

The music itself is a steady, patient pace with a slight limp in it. It put me in mind of a slow march, or of the long middle stretches of those minimal electronic records where the point is that nothing changes and you stop waiting for it to. I will admit that for a stretch in the middle I was bored. Skaters on dark glossy ice, farmhouses, a telegraph post, low light; the same groove, nothing announced. But somewhere in there it had been getting brighter and louder without ever speeding up, and by the time the camera was in among the skaters, close behind dark figures bent forward with their hands clasped behind their backs and white numbers on their backs, the cuts coming quick, I was leaning towards the screen. It pulls forward without hurrying, like being towed.

There are faces in there, and I looked for them. What looked like a young man glanced towards the lens in a flurry of other faces and was gone. Coated onlookers stood at a barrier by a road sign. A figure in a long coat walked away from us across blue ice. Nobody is followed, nobody is named, there is no finish line and no winner. The pictures are mostly ordinary, people who mostly look cheerful or at ease. If it is sad, and I think it is, the music is doing that. Nothing on screen is.

The last minute is what I will remember. The bright shimmer on top falls away and the beat goes on a bit bare. Then the bass is chopped into short jabs, a single wordless cry lands once on each bar, and the hum underneath turns hollow. On screen the camera drops to a skater's legs over scored, scratched ice, handheld and close. Then, near the end, it turns from the skaters to ducks and gulls on grey-blue water, then standing on broken ice, then close on the cracks, and slabs of ice on rose-brown water. The ice was not a floor any more.

And then the groove stops dead. The hum rings on alone for a second or so, which felt much longer. Two knocks, close together, with one low note under them. The picture dims as they sound and is black a moment after the sound has gone. I did not feel an ending so much as something being taken away.

Outside the film: I looked up the race afterwards, and the usual figures are 9,862 starters and 127 finishers. I did not know that while watching, and I have not tried to fit it to the numbers the narrator reads. It did not change what I felt. It would be easy to call this an elegy, but I would not. It is a very patient piece of music laid over a lot of people out on the ice, and it never tells you how to feel about them. I did not like it all the way through; the middle asked too much of me. But I would sit through the middle again for the room going quiet at the road, and for the knocks at the end.
