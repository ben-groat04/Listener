# A long walk on a floor that is about to go

I came out of this one braced, and a little hollow. Nothing happens in it, really. For five minutes a steady, unhurried beat walks along under pictures of people skating through a Frisian winter, and by the end I had the feeling you get when someone leaves a room and you realise you were holding your breath while they were in it.

The moment that stays is the one right in the middle of the first half. For a good while the music has been walking quietly, and the voice of a narrator has been reading out facts over it. Then the drums fall away, then the bass, and there is only a dark hum and that one dry voice saying that this, what everyone is getting ready for, is the Elfstedentocht. On screen a long, still shot of a snowy road, footprints in the foreground, and a small group of people walking slowly towards you. Nothing moves except them. It goes on a beat longer than is comfortable, and then everything comes back at once: a kick on every count, much louder than anything before it, the bass underneath, and the picture slams to a bridge so crowded the people are a blur. It was like a door being kicked open on a quiet house. I actually sat back. After that the film never gets louder in the same way again, and it never needs to.

It opens so quietly that I thought something was wrong. A dark board with white capitals, De Winter van 1963 in Friesland, pale weathered wood behind it, and under it one low hush with a few slow thuds that die away long after they land. Frost on branches against a grey-blue sky, held far longer than a modern edit would hold it. I liked that. It asks you to settle. When the beat finally arrives, it arrives exactly with a cut, from a bright blue park to bare black trees against a lilac sky, and the whole thing starts to walk.

The voice is the other thing I keep thinking about. It does not sing and it does not keep time with anything. It is flat and dry and a bit thin, the sound of a public-information film, reading out dates and distances in its own rhythm while the music goes about its business underneath. "Eighteen January 1963." Sixteen degrees below. More than two hundred kilometres. Then, in a long run just before the half-way mark, it reads the numbers: five hundred and seventy-five racers, about nine and a half thousand tour skaters. It says them with exactly the same face it used for the temperature. And somehow that is what gets to me: nobody in the soundtrack is excited, and nobody is worried, and so you have to supply the feeling yourself. When the last word is out, a new pattern starts in the bass, a lighter layer thins away, and the picture jumps to the first shot from a moving car, along a road lined with frosted white trees. The voice never speaks in sentences again. I felt it go, the way you notice a clock has stopped.

The music is a steady, patient walking pace, with a bass that sits a little against the kick, so it has a slight limp. It put me in mind of a slow march, or of the long middle stretches of those German electronic records where the point is that nothing changes and you stop waiting for it to. I will admit that for a stretch in the middle I was bored. Skaters on dark glossy ice, farmhouses, a telegraph post, low light; the same groove, nothing announced. But the boredom was doing something. When I wasn't looking, a bright, airy layer had begun opening up above the beat, and the whole thing was creeping louder without ever speeding up. By the time the camera was in among the skaters, close behind dark figures bent forward with their arms behind their backs, white numbers on their backs, the cuts getting quick, I was leaning towards the screen. It strains forward without going faster. That is a very particular kind of tension: the feeling of being pulled along by something that does not itself seem to be in a hurry.

There are faces in there, and I looked for them. A young man glances towards the lens in a flurry of other faces and is gone. Coated onlookers stand at a barrier by a road sign. A figure in a long coat walks away from us across blue ice. Nobody is followed, nobody is named, there is no finish line and no winner. It is mostly ordinary, even cheerful pictures. If it is sad, and I think it is, the music is doing that. Nothing on screen is.

The last minute is what I will remember. The bright layer drops away; the beat goes on without it, a bit bare. Then the bass is chopped into short stabs, and a single call, a voice-like cry with no word in it, lands once on each bar, and the hum underneath turns hollow. On screen the camera drops to a skater's legs over scored, scratched ice, handheld, close, and then, for the first time, it looks away from people altogether: ducks and gulls on grey-blue water, then standing on broken ice, then close on the cracks, and slabs of ice on rose-brown water. The ice is no longer a floor. It never says so; it just shows you.

And then the groove stops dead. The hum rings on by itself for a second or so, which feels much longer. Two knocks, close together, with one low note under them. The picture dims as they sound and is black a moment after the sound has gone. I did not feel an ending. I felt something being withdrawn.

Outside the film, I read afterwards that about ten thousand people started this race and only a hundred and twenty-seven finished. I did not know that while watching, and it did not change what I felt, but it explains the narrator's flat recital of numbers better than anything on the screen does: all those people and all that cold, read out like a shopping list. It would be easy to call this an elegy. I think it is quieter than that. It is a very patient piece of music laid over a lot of people who were glad to be out on the ice, and it never tells you how to feel about them. I did not like that all the way through; the middle asked too much of me. But I would sit through the middle again for the room going quiet at the road, and for the knocks at the end.
