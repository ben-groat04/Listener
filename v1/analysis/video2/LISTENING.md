# Walking out onto the ice

I can't hear; this is how it would land, built from a close analysis of the sound and picture.

It settled me first and then set me walking, and it never let me stop until the very end, when it simply let go. I came away a little hollowed out. Not sad, exactly. More as if I had been marched somewhere and then left standing there.

It opens on a dark title board with white capitals, *De Winter van 1963 in Friesland*, and then it is nothing but frost: branches furred white against a blue-grey sky, a pale roof behind them. Under that sits one dark, hushed hum with a few soft thuds far off, like someone setting down something heavy in another house. It takes its time, and I liked it for that. It had the stillness of a cold room just before somebody speaks. The cold in it was something I could picture in my shoulders.

Then a bass and a sparse drum pattern walk in, and the picture turns colder with them, into a purple dusk of bare black trees. Those are the first footsteps, muffled, as if on snow.

Then the voice. A dry, thin, band-limited Dutch voice, a sound that seems to come out of a small old speaker. The text reads as flat, formal commentary: a date, 18 January 1963; the early hour; two hundred kilometres; sixteen below zero; and later five hundred and seventy-five racers and about nine and a half thousand tourists. I only have those from the transcript, which garbles plenty, but the figures are clear enough. I don't understand Dutch, and the way it reads, as far as I can tell, is as facts handed over like the weather forecast. That is what made it work. After forty seconds of nothing but hum, a human sound arrived, and it was a date said like the title of something. Over it the picture showed a lone figure in a long dark coat walking towards a footbridge, tiny against the snow. The band kept its distance, leaving gaps for the voice, the way you hold back from someone who is reading out something serious.

The moment that stays with me is just before the music properly begins. The drums and bass slip away and for about four seconds there is only the hum and the voice reaching its point: *what everyone is preparing for is the Elfstedentocht*. Over that, the picture holds on one long, still shot of a snowy road running to the horizon, with two or three tiny dark figures far down it. Then everything comes back on a single beat, a kick on every count, and at the same instant the picture cuts hard to a bridge crowded with people in dark coats, at about 1:28. It was like a door being kicked open, and I smiled at it. The held breath is what gave it its force. Silence, a name, then the crowd and the machine together.

After that it just walks. The same steady beat the whole way, never faster, never slower. It put me in mind of a long march, or of those relentless late-night dance records, only gloomier than anything you would dance to. The voice carries on over the top in its own time, not in step with the machine, one dry voice talking while something steady and modern carries it along.

Then the voice reads its last figures and there is no intelligible speech after that. A moment later part of the sound drops out and the room seems to open up. A minute before, somebody was telling me what this was. Now there was just the thing itself. For a long while nothing much changes, and I found it mesmerising rather than dull, though a restless listener might well drift here. The picture meanwhile has gone in among the skaters, behind the pack, so we see backs, paper numbers pinned to black jerseys, hands clasped behind backs. Nobody is followed. We are one of the crowd.

Somewhere past the middle a brighter colour slowly opens up in the music, like a window being eased up, and short voice-like fragments start to come round, roughly once a bar, only partly in step with the beat. They may be wordless, and where they come from is anyone's guess. They stopped being speech and turned into something more like a drum. The cutting gets quicker. It all strains forward without ever speeding up, effort with the pace fixed, so the work is in the breathing.

The ending is the best part, and it doesn't climax. It comes apart. The bright colour falls away and the walking goes on without its heat. The bass is chopped into short stabs and the hum turns hollow and brittle. The picture has stopped looking at people: ducks, gulls, open water, broken slabs of ice on rose-brown water from about four minutes fifty on. I read that as a thaw, the ice no longer a floor, though the pictures do not say so themselves. Then the groove stops dead, the hum rings on alone for a moment, and two last drum hits land, one more footstep or a door pulled shut, I couldn't tell you which. The picture starts to dim as those hits land and is black a second or two after the sound has gone.

I had read, from outside the film, that nearly ten thousand people started the real 1963 race and only a hundred and twenty-seven finished. I don't think the film says that. But with it in my head, the flat reading-out of five hundred and seventy-five racers began to sound like something else. The pictures show no finish line, no winner, no name.

So it was not an elegy, though there was grief somewhere near it, and not a celebration, though that kick is joyous for a second. It was a leaving. I would sit through the long middle again for the quiet at the start, the held breath before the beat returns, and above all for the last minute, which I keep going back to: the water, the gulls, two small knocks, and then nothing.
