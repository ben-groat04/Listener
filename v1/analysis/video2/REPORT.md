# "Elfstedentocht Part 2" (Public Service Broadcasting): a report

*About five minutes. Timestamps are m:ss on our file (305.9 s). The official track is 5:04, so times may sit about 1.9 s from the release. Tags: OBS = measured or seen; INT = interpretation.*

**Checked against:** `v1/data/video2/audio/song.wav` and `stems/drums.mp3`, `stems/bass.mp3` (10 ms envelope over 301.5 to 304.5 s, re-measured 2026-11-17); `sync/sync.json`, `sync/sync_kickgrid.json`, `sync/grid_120bpm.json`; `scenes/shots.json` (cut times).

## How this music was heard

No one has listened to this audio with ears. Everything about the sound comes from measurement: separated stems (drums, bass, voice, everything else), spectrograms, chord and pitch estimates, and loudness. So "dark", "hushed" or "brittle" below are readings of those measurements (INT). The measurements are solid (OBS); the feelings are a reconstruction.

## The music

This is a loop-built electronic piece at a steady 120 beats per minute. It has no sung vocal, no tune and no cadence. The same seven notes (B C# D E F# G A) are used throughout. B minor is the simplest name, because the pad sits on B; the first-half bass spends most of its time on E, G and D, so an E-centred hearing is equally possible (INT). It never changes key or resolves. The drama is made by layers, loudness and the human voice, not by harmony or tempo.

**The opening (0:00 to 0:28).** A single dark, hushed pad, with isolated low thuds and long tails at 0:00, 0:08, 0:12, 0:20 and 0:24. There is no pulse, bass or voice, and almost nothing above 4 kHz. It is very quiet. It feels like a held breath, or a cold morning heard through glass (INT).

**The stride begins (0:28).** Bass and a sparse drum pattern walk in under the unchanged pad, and the overall level steps up about 7 dB. These are the first footfalls, still muted and dark.

**A voice, then a band that holds back (0:42 to 1:24).** At about 0:42 a dry, band-limited Dutch archive voice begins, "18 januari 1963". It is the first human sound after 42 seconds of pad. The sudden brightening at that instant is the narrator's own consonants, not a new instrument. The voice leads and the band frames it. The bass plays one long note per four seconds (E, G, D, E, G, D, then a B). The drums are intermittent and nearly vanish around 1:01 to 1:03 and 1:10 to 1:15. The effect is spacious, documentary and a little solemn.

**The hole and the drop (1:24 to 1:28.6).** The drums vanish at about 1:24.3 and the bass at about 1:25, for roughly four seconds. Only the pad and the narrator remain (the mix drops only 2 to 3 dB), and the narrator names the subject: "Waar iedereen zich voor opmaakt, dat is de Elfstedentocht" ("what everyone is preparing for, that is the Elfstedentocht"). Then everything returns on one downbeat at 1:28.6, with the drums about 50 dB louder, a four-on-the-floor kick and no fill. Its force comes from the gap before it and the lack of any warning (INT); it is the biggest single event in the piece.

**The locked stride (1:28.6 to 2:36.8).** From here to 5:00 the pulse never changes. The drums sit at a flat level with the kick on every beat. The bass plays a figure grouped in threes against the kick, so it lands with the kick only on beat 3 and never on beat 1. That gives a steady foot-tap with a slight limp (INT). It also fools an automatic beat tracker, which reports 161 BPM instead of 120. The narrator still speaks over the top in free time, a human voice carried by a machine.

**The last sentence and the clearing (2:36.8).** The narrator's last word, at 2:36.1, closes a head-count of 575 racers and about 9,500 tour skaters, and the voice never speaks in sentences again. A new loop begins, with the bass changing note every 12 seconds (B, A, D), and one layer partly drops out. It sounds like a sudden clearing in which the music must carry the scale of the crowd alone (INT).

**The trance and the plateau (2:48.8 to 4:36.8).** For about 47 seconds nothing new happens and the loop is hypnotic. Then pressure builds with no single moment announcing it. One layer brightens by roughly 22 dB in its top frequencies (about 3 dB in the full mix), fastest between about 3:26 and 3:50. Its notes and rhythm stay the same, so it sounds like a filter opening or the layer being pushed harder, not like new instruments; how it was made cannot be recovered. Overall level creeps up to a peak at about 4:24 to 4:36, roughly 15 dB above the opening.

In the same stretch the voice becomes something else. From about 3:28.6 it returns as short fragments, possibly wordless, that seem to recur once per two-second bar and grow longer (the bar timing comes from the dossier and was not re-tested here). Whether they are sliced archive speech, a vocal-like synthesiser or leakage from another layer cannot be decided. Brightness, loudness and fragment length rise together, so the intensity climbs without anyone being able to say when. It feels settled but straining: a long, determined effort over a pulse that never speeds up (INT).

**The unmaking (4:36.8 to 5:04).** The track ends by taking itself apart, in this order.

- At 4:36.8 the bright layer falls away (about 13 dB at the top). The pulse goes on, so the energy seems to drain while the machine continues.
- At about 4:48.6 the bass is cut into short stabs, and a single unpitched voice-like call lands on each bar. The pad loses its third and goes hollow, so the texture is brittle and lurching.
- At about 5:00.8 the groove stops dead, and the pad rings out alone for about one and a half seconds.
- **The closing tag, 5:02.3 to 5:02.9.** Two drum hits (5:02.33 and 5:02.58), measured. Under them the bass sounds as one sustained tone from 5:02.33 to about 5:02.86, with a brief dip at 5:02.6; the music analysis hears this as three notes a quarter-second apart (5:02.33, 5:02.58, 5:02.83), but the signal alone resolves only the dip and the end (INT). The drum hits are as loud as the groove. Then silence, with digital zero from about 5:04.25.

It reads as withdrawal and a stop, not arrival (INT). "Calm" fits the surface and the gesture, not the energy.

## How the picture works with it

The film is colour archive footage of the Frisian winter and the race, credited to the Fries Film Archief; the exact reels and the narrator are not identified. It opens on a title board, "De winter van 1963 in Friesland", then frost and snow over the pad. A snowbound churchyard appears as the date is spoken at 0:42, which turns a mood into a dated day. Colour arrives at about 0:48 with a pink-gold ice road, followed by small solitary figures: a lone coated figure at a footbridge (about 0:57) and a woolly-hatted face in purple light (about 1:00).

The picture does not follow the beat as far as measured. Against the 120 BPM beat grid (fitted to the kick drum), 36% of the 86 cuts fall within 80 ms of a beat, against 32% expected by chance, and cut timing within the beat (p about 0.09) and within the four-beat bar (p about 0.2) is not distinguishable from chance. The bar test assumes a four-beat bar counted from the drop and is untested beyond that. (The tracker's 161 BPM grid gave 37% against 43%, but that grid is wrong and is not used.) In the first 42 seconds only 10 cuts exist and they lean towards one point in the bar (p about 0.01); with so few, and several tests run, I treat that as a hint only. Cut rate and image motion rise and fall loosely with loudness (correlations about 0.2 to 0.3, which are weak, and the cut-rate result did not survive a shuffle test). That cuts follow the narrator's phrase boundaries before 2:36 is an impression (INT), not tested. A few joints are tight:

- At 0:28.08 a cut to cold, low-contrast light lands within about 0.05 s of bass and drums entering. This is the tightest match in the piece.
- At the drop (1:28.6) the picture does not cut. One static shot of a snowy road (trampled footprints, two or three tiny walkers) runs from 1:22.1 to 1:30.3 straight through the hole and the drop; the cut to the first crowd, a bridge with spectators, comes 1.7 s after the drop. So the picture ignores the biggest moment in the music.
- At 2:36.6 a cut to the first shot from a moving vehicle, on cleaner, whiter stock, lands about 0.5 s after the narrator's last word and about 0.2 s before the new loop at 2:36.8.

From about 3:13 to 4:33 the camera sits inside or directly behind the pack: backs, bibs, hands behind backs, a few faces. Nobody is followed. Cuts are quick (median about two seconds). Relief comes in a few long holds: the wide ice (about 3:00 to 3:09), the bright canal and sidecar (3:46), and a backlit golden skater bent double (about 4:10.5 to 4:12). Shot by shot the pictures are mostly ordinary or cheerful, so any sadness comes from the pairing with the music (INT).

At the end the picture turns away from people. From about 4:52 there are ducks and gulls on open water, then broken floes on rose-brown water. The closing tag sounds at 5:02.3 to 5:02.9; the picture starts to dim at about 5:02.5, is about a third of full brightness when the audio reaches digital zero (5:04.25), is near black from 5:05 and fully black about 5:06. This edit shows no finish line, no winner, no named person and no credits.

## Meaning and context

The race was held on 18 January 1963, the twelfth Elfstedentocht, about 200 km through Friesland. Published sources give about 570 racers and about 9,300 tour skaters starting, and 127 finishing. The narrator's figures (575 and about 9,500) are close, but whether the voice speaks from before the race or looking back is unknown. The piece was commissioned for the 50th-anniversary Explore the North festival in Leeuwarden and released in autumn 2013; no band statement about it is known. The 2012 tour was cancelled, and 1963 remained the benchmark for the hardest race. A viewer who knows this will hear the piece differently from one who does not.

The shape is announcement, endurance, withdrawal. The voice speaks in sentences until 2:36. A long loudness-led stretch with no explanation follows. Then layer, low end, people, sound and picture are removed in turn. A free-timed human voice sits over a rigid modern grid and lines up with it only where its sentences end, at 1:28 and 2:36 (OBS). The recurring subject is a place: a road receding into white, with small figures on it and sometimes none.

Elegy or celebration is genuinely open. The minor key, cold palette and subtraction ending point towards mourning. The festive four-on-the-floor kick and the forward-looking words point towards communal effort. The best-supported word is withdrawal; "mourning" is weaker (INT). "Thaw" or "the end of winters" is a cultural reading carried by the 2012 cancellation, not by the pictures, which show only ice that is no longer a floor (INT). A fair short description is an unspoken afterword: a confident voice recites its figures and leaves, and a steady modern pulse carries anonymous bodies across the ice, brightens, and is taken apart until only water and the closing tag remain, two drum hits at 5:02.33 and 5:02.58 over a bass tone that lasts to about 5:02.86. Whether you feel grief, pride or a quiet leaving depends on what you bring.

## The moments that stay with you

1. **1:24 to 1:30, the hole, the drop and the empty road.** A gap, a named subject, then force with no preparation. The picture stays on the empty road until 1:30.3, then cuts to the first crowd.
2. **2:36, the last sentence.** The head-count ends, the loop changes, the layer thins, and the voice never returns as speech.
3. **0:42, the date over the churchyard.** The first human sound after 42 seconds of pad.
4. **The last 25 seconds.** The bright layer gone, bass stabs, ducks and floes, the stop at about 5:00.8, the closing tag (two drum hits, 5:02.33 and 5:02.58, over a bass tone to about 5:02.86), the fade.
5. **4:10 to 4:12.** A young man glancing at the lens, then the golden skater: a glance within a flurry, not a held close-up.
6. **0:28.** The stride begins, and sound and picture turn together within a few hundredths of a second.

## What we could not settle

- The instruments cannot be recovered from the measurements; no banjo or guitar is claimed.
- How the late build is made, and what the late voice fragments are, are open.
- The narrator and source films are unconfirmed. The trusted words are the date, the title sentence, 16 degrees below, over 200 km, and 575 and about 9,500. The time of day near 1:00 ("half zes" against 6.30 or afternoon in other passes) is contradictory.
- Whether the closing tag (5:02.3 to 5:02.9) was meant as a deliberate full stop is not decided by the signal.
- The tonal centre (B or E) is a hypothesis.
