# "Elfstedentocht Part 2" (Public Service Broadcasting): a report

*About five minutes. Timestamps are m:ss on our file (305.9 s). The official track is 5:04, so times may sit about 1.9 s from the release.*

## How this music was heard

Nobody on this project has heard the audio. The music was "heard" through measurements: separated stems (drums, bass, voice, everything else), spectrograms, chord and pitch estimates, and second-by-second loudness. Where this report says something sounds dark, hushed or brittle, that is a reading of those measurements and not a report of ears. The measurements are solid. The feelings are an informed reconstruction.

## The music

This is a loop-built electronic piece at a steady 120 beats per minute. It has no sung vocal, no tune and no cadence. The pitch set stays the same throughout (B C# D E F# G A). B minor is the simplest name for it, because the pad sits on B throughout. The first-half bass spends most of its time on E, G and D, so an E-centred hearing is equally possible. It never modulates or resolves. The drama is made by layers, loudness and the human voice, not by harmony or tempo.

**The opening (0:00 to 0:28).** A single dark, hushed pad, with isolated low thuds and long tails at 0:00, 0:08, 0:12, 0:20 and 0:24. There is no pulse, bass or voice, and almost nothing above 4 kHz. It is very quiet. The likeliest image is a held breath, or a cold morning heard through glass.

**The stride begins (0:28).** Bass and a sparse drum pattern walk in under the unchanged pad, and the overall level steps up about 7 dB. These are the first footfalls, still muted and dark.

**A voice, then a band that holds back (0:42 to 1:24).** At about 0:42 a dry, band-limited Dutch archive voice begins, "18 januari 1963". It is the first human sound after 42 seconds of pad. The measured jump in brightness at that instant is the narrator's own consonants arriving, not a new instrument. The voice leads and the band frames it. The bass plays one root per four seconds (E, G, D, E, G, D, then a B). The drums are intermittent and nearly vanish around 1:01 to 1:03 and 1:10 to 1:15. The effect is spacious, documentary and a little solemn.

**The hole and the drop (1:24 to 1:28.6).** This is the largest single event in the piece. The drums vanish at about 1:24.3 and the bass at about 1:25, for roughly four seconds. Only the pad and the narrator remain, and the narrator names the subject: "Waar iedereen zich voor opmaakt, dat is de Elfstedentocht" ("what everyone is preparing for, that is the Elfstedentocht"). Then everything returns on one downbeat at 1:28.6, with the drums about 50 dB louder, a four-on-the-floor kick and no fill. Its force comes from the silence before it and the absence of any warning.

**The locked stride (1:28.6 to 2:36.8).** From here to 5:00 the pulse never changes. The drums sit at a flat level with the kick on every beat. The bass plays a figure grouped in threes against the kick, so it lands with the kick only on beat 3 and never on beat 1. That gives a steady foot-tap with a slight limp, and it is why an automatic tracker reports a false 161 BPM. The narrator still speaks over the top in free time, a human voice carried by a machine.

**The last sentence and the clearing (2:36.8).** The narrator's last word, at 2:36.1, closes a head-count of 575 racers and about 9,500 tour skaters. The voice never speaks in sentences again. A new loop begins, with the bass returning to B every 12 seconds (B, A, D), and one layer partly drops out. It sounds like a sudden clearing, and the music must carry the scale of the crowd alone.

**The trance and the plateau (2:48.8 to 4:36.8).** For about 47 seconds nothing new happens, and the repeating loop is hypnotic. Then pressure builds without any single moment announcing it. One layer brightens by roughly 24 to 28 dB in its top frequencies, fastest between about 3:26 and 3:50. Its pitches and rhythm stay the same, so it sounds like a filter opening or the layer being driven harder, not like new instruments (the mechanism cannot be recovered). Overall level creeps up to a peak at about 4:24 to 4:36, roughly 15 dB above the opening.

In the same stretch the voice becomes something else. From about 3:28.6 it returns as fragments, probably wordless, that recur on the two-second bar and grow longer. Whether they are sliced archive speech, a vocal-like synthesiser or leakage from another layer cannot be decided. Because brightness, loudness and fragment length rise together, the intensity climbs without the listener being able to say when. I would call it settled but straining: a long, determined effort over a pulse that never speeds up.

**The unmaking (4:36.8 to 5:04).** The track ends by taking itself apart, in this order.

- At 4:36.8 the bright layer falls away, about 13 dB at the top. The pulse goes on, so the energy seems to drain while the machine continues.
- At about 4:48.6 the bass is gated into stabs, and a single unpitched voice stab lands on each bar. The pad loses its third and goes hollow, so the texture is brittle and lurching.
- At about 5:00.8 the groove is cut, and the pad rings out for about two seconds.
- From 5:02.34 a short tag follows, two drum hits and three bass notes a quaver apart, as loud as the groove. Then there is silence, with digital zero from about 5:04.2.

It reads as withdrawal and a stop, not arrival. "Calm" is true of the surface and the gesture, not of the energy.

## How the picture works with it

The film is colour archive footage of the Frisian winter and the race, credited to the Fries Film Archief; the exact reels and the narrator are not identified. It opens on a title board, "De winter van 1963 in Friesland", then frost and snow over the pad. A snowbound churchyard appears as the date is spoken at 0:42, which turns a mood into a dated day. Colour arrives at about 0:48 with a pink-gold ice road, followed by small solitary figures: a lone coated figure at a footbridge (about 0:57) and a woolly-hatted face in purple light (about 1:00).

The picture does not follow the beat. Cuts sit at chance against the grid. Cut rate and motion rise and fall loosely with loudness. Before 2:36 cuts tend to sit near the voice's phrase boundaries, and afterwards they follow loudness. A few joints are tight:

- At 0:28.08 a cut to cold, low-contrast light lands within about 0.05 s of bass and drums entering. This is the tightest match in the piece.
- At 1:28.5 a long static shot of a road (about 1:20 to 1:28.5, trampled footprints, three or four tiny walkers) ends in a hard cut to the first crowd, a blurred bridge with spectators. It lands within about 0.1 s of the drop and of the narrator's last word. The picture holds up to the drop and cuts on it. Whether the editor placed the drop at the end of that shot is not known.
- At 2:36.6, within about 0.2 s of the voice ending, a cut to the first shot from a moving vehicle, on cleaner, whiter stock.

From about 3:13 to 4:33 the camera sits inside or directly behind the pack: backs, bibs, hands behind backs, a few faces. Nobody is followed, and no face is held beyond about 8 seconds. Cuts are quick, with a median of about two seconds. Relief comes in the long holds, roughly one every 30 seconds: the wide ice (about 3:00 to 3:09), the bright canal and sidecar (3:46), and a backlit golden skater bent double (about 4:10.5 to 4:12). Shot by shot the pictures are mostly ordinary or cheerful. Any sadness comes from the pairing with the minor-key music.

At the end the picture turns away from people. From about 4:52 there are ducks and gulls on open water, then broken floes on rose-brown water. The sound ends first, and the picture fades to black from about 5:02.5. There is no finish, no winner, no named person and no credits.

## Meaning and context

The race was held on 18 January 1963, the twelfth Elfstedentocht, about 200 km through Friesland. Published sources give about 570 racers and about 9,300 tour skaters starting, and 127 finishing. The narrator's figures (575 and about 9,500) are close, but whether the voice is from before the race or looking back is not established. The piece was commissioned for the 50th-anniversary Explore the North festival in Leeuwarden and released in autumn 2013. No statement from the band about these tracks was found. The 2012 tour was cancelled, and 1963 remained the benchmark for the hardest race. A viewer who carries this knowledge will hear the piece differently from one who does not.

The shape is announcement, endurance, withdrawal. The voice speaks in sentences until 2:36. A long loudness-led stretch with no explanation follows. Then layer, low end, people, sound and picture are removed in turn. A free-timed human voice meets a rigid modern grid and touches it only at the 1:28 and 2:36 joints. The recurring subject is a place: a road receding into white, with small figures on it and sometimes none.

Elegy or celebration is genuinely open. The minor key, cold palette and subtraction ending point towards mourning. The festive four-on-the-floor kick and the forward-looking words point towards communal effort. The best-supported word is withdrawal; "mourning" is weaker. "Thaw" or "the end of winters" is a cultural reading carried by the 2012 cancellation, not by the pictures, which show only ice that is no longer a floor. A fair short description is an unspoken afterword: a confident voice from before the day recites its figures and leaves, and a steady modern pulse carries anonymous bodies across the ice, brightens, and is taken apart until only water and one last hit remain. Whether you feel grief, pride or a quiet leaving depends on what you bring.

## The moments that stay with you

1. **1:24 to 1:30, the hole, the drop and the empty road.** Silence, a named subject, then force with no preparation, and a cut to the first crowd as it lands.
2. **2:36, the last sentence.** The head-count ends, the loop changes, the layer thins, and the voice never returns as speech.
3. **0:42, the date over the churchyard.** The first human sound after 42 seconds of pad.
4. **The last 25 seconds.** The bright layer gone, bass stabs, ducks and floes, the cut at about 5:00.8, one last hit at 5:02.3, the fade.
5. **4:10 to 4:12.** A young man glancing at the lens, then the golden skater: a glance within a flurry, not a held close-up.
6. **0:28.** The stride begins, and sound and picture turn together within a few hundredths of a second.

## What we could not settle

- The instruments are unrecoverable from the measurements. I make no claim of banjo or guitar.
- How the late build is made, and what the late voice fragments are, are open.
- The narrator and source films are unconfirmed. The trusted words are the date, the title sentence, 16 degrees below, over 200 km, and 575 and about 9,500. The time of day near 1:00 ("half zes" against 6.30 or afternoon in other passes) is contradictory.
- Whether the last hit at 5:02.3 was a deliberate full stop is not decided by the signal.
- The tonal centre (B or E) is a hypothesis. The method is hauntological, the timbre is not.
