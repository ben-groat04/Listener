# The Sceptic: Round 3 (final) on video2 ("Elfstedentocht Part 2", PSB)

Basis. I re-read my round 2 and the five other round 2 statements, plus the research files on 1963 numbers. New frames opened this round: f_0211 (3:30) and f_0289 (4:48). As before, nobody has heard the audio; "sound" means measurement. Times are m:ss on our file.

## 1. Questions put to me, answered

**Insider: if the plateau bibs are racers (all legible numbers below 575), does the anonymity become about the elite field, and which reading does my plain view prefer?**
First, I must weaken my own bib argument. The research file says 9,862 started: 568 racers and 9,294 tour riders (`elfstedentocht_1963.md`, official site, H on the split). The narration says 575 and about 9,500; the two sources agree to within a few per cent, so the "racers" were a small elite and the tour riders were the crowd. Nothing in `research/` says how numbers were allocated. I searched for startnummer, bib and nummer and found no scheme. So "all under 575 therefore racers" remains a hypothesis. It has a second weakness I should have flagged: the digits are read from blurred, hand-held frames. I opened f_0211 (3:30): the bib on the left-hand skater is a pale rectangle with two or three dark characters, smeared by motion; I could not read it as a number with any confidence. The dossier's list (201, 211, 504, 481, 145, 170, 529, 157, 236, 238) came from notes at 1 fps and several are "44x" or "24x" partials. Evidence grade: L. If the numbers are real and racers, the plateau is the field the narrator counted first (575) rather than the 9,500, and the "ordinary people" reading loses its footing. But I note the cost is small for my plain reading: I never claimed who these skaters were, only that the footage is an endurance procession set to a steady groove. Whether they are racers or tourists, "bodies from behind in a line" is what the picture is. My preference: communal-effort-and-spectacle first, race second, and neither dependent on the bibs.

**Film critic: if this were a celebration, would the director end on people? What image in the last 20 seconds would have made me accept a celebratory reading?**
A fair question and I will answer it straight. Yes, I would expect a finish line, a crowd, a winner or at least faces at rest (the Paping Brinta story is in the research, a ready-made finish image). I note the absence. The last 20 seconds (4:44 to 5:04) are: a flagpole against blue sky (f_0289: I see a red flag-cloth with the pole across it; I cannot confirm tricolour, and the dossier's "red-and-white" is not what I see: red only in this frame), legs and clip-on skates, ducks on open water, floes and fade. So the film's final minute avoids people. I concede that this removes the strongest support for "celebration". But it does not support "elegy" either; it supports "the film does not end on the event". Three plain explanations compete: (a) PSB want a closing image not tied to a result, because they cannot show the finish (they have no finish footage that we can identify); (b) the reel being used simply ends on landscape; (c) the elegiac choice. Only (c) needs intent, and nothing in the data picks it. The cost of having no celebratory image is real, and I accept it as the piece's strongest elegiac lean. I rate "withdrawal" H, "mourning" L-M.

**Symbolist: the minor key and cold palette are the maker's two largest choices, and they are in the text.**
Agreed, and I withdraw "something the viewer brings" for those two features. They are choices. What I still dispute is the inference from a choice to a meaning. Minor key plus cold palette fits a winter piece on any theory: it is what a winter looks like in this stock and what a drone-like pad sounds like. They give "grave", not "mourning". The step from grave to mourning is made by the date and the churchyard, and I have conceded that those are received, not demonstrated.

## 2. What I concede

1. **Joint arithmetic.** The film critic and music critic have done the sums I asked for: two cut joints (0:28 at 0.05 s, 2:36.6 at about 0.2 s) and probably 4:48.96 at 0.4 s. The music critic's 6 % for two bar-aligned points is fair as an upper-bound of weak evidence, and the film critic's honest "one in ten to one in five if I had searched" is the right number. I now say: the editor probably knew where the music's big section changes were and placed two or three cuts near them; the other 80-odd cuts are not beat-locked. That is the ordinary state of a film cut to a finished track. My earlier "selected list" objection stands for 2:57, 3:36.7 and 4:36.0 only.
2. **The long hold at 1:22 to 1:30.3.** The film critic found a second 8.16 s hold (251.84 s onwards), after the 4:10 flurry. That makes the editor's pattern of "long still shot beside the biggest sound event or burst" a repeated one (n = 2) rather than a single case. I move from "plausible, unproven" to "plausible, with weak repeat evidence". I still hold that the first half of the film also has long holds (12 s at 0:12), so the editor simply tends to hold static wides.
3. **The 4:10 stare.** The film critic's shot-length fact settles it: it sits in a 1.32 s shot inside a flurry. It was not held. Nobody now claims that the editor built the moment; the dispute is over.
4. **The 4:28 "held up skater".** Not defended by anyone any more except the first-timer, who has not reopened the frame. I stand by f_0269 and f_0270: skates on, upright, officials standing apart. Strike it.
5. **Voice fragments.** The music critic concedes the metaphor; the symbolist demotes it to decoration. Nobody now argues it is speech. Settled as "bar-locked upper-layer events with speech-like timing, identity unknown". The question I put to the music critic last round (do the fragments repeat identically?) is the single test that would resolve it, and it is still not answered; I leave it as a flagged open item rather than reopen it.

## 3. Where I still push back

**"The picture is happier than the piece" (first-timer's thread).** This is the best new idea of round 2 and I have only a partial objection. It is right that most frames, taken alone, are cheerful: f_0262 (4:21, sunglasses, red scarf, blue ice) is "a nice day out". But I want to qualify "the sadness is made by the pairing". The pairing includes things the first-timer lists (minor key, loud locked pulse) and one it counts too lightly: the pulse. A 120 BPM four-on-the-floor kick is the most celebratory device in electronic music. If the same pictures ran over this groove in a major key, the film would read as a joyful ice festival. So the piece is held in suspension between a funereal key and a festive pulse, and I would resist any account that resolves that to one side. This is my consistent position since round 1, and it has gained supporters: the music critic's "neutral-to-grave in colour, unmistakably determined in gesture" is the same sentence in better words.

**The insider's before-the-race voice.** I called it the best idea on the panel and I keep that, with one limit the symbolist made explicit: it is a property of the artefact only if the narration really dates from before the race. The research file (`archive_sources.md`) lists the source reel as unresolved. Words such as "half zes", "alle steden zijn klaar" and the entry numbers could equally be a retrospective documentary reading a schedule. The numbers 575 and about 9,500 are close to the official 568 and 9,294, which slightly favours pre-race entry figures (a retrospective would more likely quote the 127 finishers). That is a useful piece of evidence the insider did not use, and it favours the insider by a modest amount (M). I note it as the strongest support the "before the race" thesis has, and still short of proof. Also my last-round idea that the two-act form (pride, then reckoning) fits the signal without outside knowledge survives: the drop at the name of the race (1:28.6) is the proud moment; the sag and stop (4:36.8 to 5:00.8) is the only place where the sound alone leans away from drive.

**The flag at 4:45.** The dossier and several panellists call it a probable Dutch tricolour. f_0289 (second 288, 4:48) shows a red flag-cloth below a pole with insulators and an electric lamp; no white or blue band visible. The panel's "civic, proud breath" rests on a flag we cannot identify. It could be a red pennant or the red band of a tricolour with the rest hidden. I would remove "the Dutch tricolour" and leave "a red flag". Minor, but an example of a reading built on a guess.

## 4. The thread I find most interesting: what the piece does not show

Round 3 lets me put the whole discussion on one line. In 305.9 s there is no finish, no winner, no collapse, no named person, no title card after the first, no credits and no sentence about any cost. The narration stops 2 minutes 25 seconds from the end. Everything the panel feels about the piece's tone (elegy, hindsight, withdrawal) therefore comes from absence plus a small set of facts: the date, the churchyard, the key, the loudness arc, the subtraction at the end, the thaw. That is a sound editorial strategy for a commission about an anniversary: show the build-up and the anonymous effort, never say how it ended, and let the audience's own memory (the "Hel van '63") fill the gap. The Dutch viewer supplies it; the English viewer gets a handsome, ambiguous endurance film. That is, I think, the honest reading of what the artefact does, and it needs no overclaiming of intent. The cost, which the first-timer saw, is that the piece is private to those who know.

## 5. Settled reading (5 sentences)

1. This is a loop-built, 120 BPM electronic piece in B minor, under amateur colour film of a Frisian winter and a Dutch pre-race voice, whose form is announcement (0:41 to 2:36), endurance (2:36 to 4:36) and withdrawal (4:36 to 5:04), with a drop at 1:28.6 that works by subtracting first.
2. The picture is cut by image and phrase, not by beat (no significant beat or bar locking, on either grid), with perhaps two or three cuts (0:28, 2:36.6, probably 4:48.96) set near section changes by an editor who knew the track.
3. The frames taken alone are mostly ordinary and often cheerful; the grave tone comes from the minor key, cold pad and ending (maker's choices) and from the date and churchyard (received by anyone who knows the 1963 race), set against a festive, unwavering pulse, so the piece is suspended between memorial and celebration rather than resolved.
4. The voice is probably a pre-race voice (its numbers match the official entry figures), so the hindsight, if one hears it, is supplied by the viewer; the film itself never states any cost, and it ends on thaw and water, not on people, which I read as withdrawal (H) and only tentatively as mourning (L-M).
5. Several attractive claims should be dropped (held-up skater at 4:28, addressed stare at 4:10, "voice becomes breath", tricolour at 4:45, "tour skaters" from blurred bibs), and the panel should be allowed to keep its feelings as readings, not findings.

**The single moment that best captures the piece:** 1:24.3 to 1:30 (1:24 to 1:28.6, then the drop): the static road, the narrator's last pre-race sentence ("Waar iedereen zich voor opmaakt, dat is de Elfstedentocht"), the drums and bass falling away under it, then everything returning on one downbeat while the picture does not move. It holds in one place a voice that knows nothing, a machine that is determined and a picture that simply watches, which is the piece's whole balance. It is also well attested in the data (H on the events), unlike the moments built on one blurred frame.
