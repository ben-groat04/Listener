# t_birdwalk: how it feels and what it means

## What this most probably is

A bass-heavy, deadpan club-rap boast about a dance, cut together as a daft, yellow, bird-obsessed collage. The caption names the act as "Soulja Unit", the song as "BIRD WALK", and says "AVAILABLE NOW on iTunes", so this is almost certainly a promotional video for a rap single, and "bird walk" is the dance the words keep offering. The pictures are home-made-looking and recycled: a trio dancing in a pale room, a dancer in a yellow bedroom, a person in a yellow poncho with a box on their head, a giant bird puppet, a bird costume, small children in beaked hoods, a grainy crowd clip, and a young man in a striped hooded towel. It is cheeky, affectionate and funny. Place, year and intent are left to the uncertainty section.

## The music

There is no tune. There is a heavy one-note floor, a chanted hook, and a voice that talks rather than sings, and that is the point. The sub-bass sits on a single note, A, and it is physical: boomy, round, felt in the chest more than heard. Over it a dry, close voice swaggers through a boast, with a sparse drum hit that keeps the groove slow and bouncy, a nod-along rather than a dance-floor rush. The mood is playful, cocky and bodily, over a slightly dark floor. Almost none of it comes from harmony; it comes from delivery, weight and rhythm.

The shape is a count. For the first twelve seconds there is no bass at all: a voice grabs the microphone ("Aye, man, we got this new dance") and one drum hit lands every second and a half. The missing low end is something you feel, a held breath, and a falling sweep seems to lean towards the drop. At 0:12 the weight arrives, the mix gets a little louder and narrower, and the groove locks in. After that the bass is taken away and put back on a rigid twelve-second grid, and the last return is at 2:35. Loudness barely moves within each state, so the drama is spatial and bodily rather than a swell: when the bass goes the picture opens wide and weightless and exposed; when it returns it snaps narrow and heavy. It is the sound of subtraction.

The voice defines the sections as much as the bass. The refrain, "that bird walk, watch me do that bird walk", is low and wide, and it is the only thing you could hum. Verse one jumps up into a flat, near-monotone recitation, dead centre and very close, which can feel hypnotic and a little cramped. Verse two settles lower. Drums thicken before the later cuts, so you hear a rush into a wall, and the returns are bare: a hit, a gap, then the bass. Once you have learnt the count, each drop becomes anticipation rather than surprise. The tempo feels slow and swaggering.

## How the picture works with it

The cutting is loose and by eye. Pictures do not move with the beat, and the sound drops carry the structure. What holds the film together is one colour world, yellow, orange and pink, and the bird returning in different registers: studio puppet, car-park costume, children in beaked hoods, the poncho and box, the little logo in the caption. The cutting is busiest in the first full groove and slowest in the first two breaks. Around the big edges the picture is often a little early, as if an editor knew the count; the dark crowd clip arrives just ahead of the first drop and the towel is lifted before the slam at 2:35. It is suggestive, not strong, and viewers largely supply the sync themselves: whatever is on screen when the floor falls takes the credit.

Where words and pictures meet, the effect is comic. "We just bird dance at home" plays over the trio in their pale room. "I'ma show you how it go" plays over flailing and shuffling feet. A boast about money and birthday cake plays over a puppet in front of a children's alphabet wall. The box-headed poncho figure, with its drawn mouth, is simply funny.

## Meaning and context

The strongest reading is that a dance is being offered to you. The words promise to show you how it goes, the picture shows flapping arms and bent knees, and the caption turns the offer into a sale. The home-made clips look like bodies dancing for nobody, among themselves, while the packaged layers (puppet, hooded man, caption) look out at the viewer.

The human side is what makes it endearing (interpretation). Kids in beaked hoods with their elbows bent like wings, a man in a towel standing perfectly calm, a figure in a poncho miming a microphone against a drawn mouth: these are ordinary people throwing themselves into a daft idea with total commitment, and a crowd of strangers flapping together is a small joy. The film is a loving, DIY promo, and the sincerity of "available now" is part of why the gags land. The comedy is deadpan: children's-programme imagery against a club-rap boast.

The sound is consistent with club rap of the 2000s to 2010s without separating crunk-era from trap-era; the spoken tag at the start, the one-note boast and the fade-out are genre conventions, which is a guess. No older record is demonstrably sampled.

## What it is like to listen (interpretation)

All of this was heard through measurements, not by ears, so each "feels" is a reading tied to a number.

I sit forward for the first twelve seconds. One drum hit at a time, nothing underneath, and the longer it lasts the more my chest waits. At 0:12 the bass lands as a small physical shove and my shoulders drop into a nod. I smile at the dark grainy clip that arrives with it, the heaviest sound on the lowest-fi, grainiest picture.

Verse one narrows my attention. The voice sits on one flat note, very close, and I stop hearing words and just ride the pulse. Then at 0:47 the floor goes and I feel it in my breath: space, exposure, children in yellow hoods with their elbows bent like wings. In a track that is otherwise not tender, that is the tenderest picture in the film. At 0:59 the weight returns and I settle, and from then on I know what is coming.

Knowing is the problem and the pleasure. Through the long second verse, nearly forty-five seconds of one note and one rhythm, mostly over the same trio footage, my mind drifts, and I notice the drums getting denser rather than anything changing. The bass flickers out around 1:43 and slams back, and that small stumble wakes me. When break two arrives as a pasted copy of the first, I feel a flash of déjà vu and a touch of impatience: "again".

Then 2:23 changes my mind. The bass and drums leave together, the voice walks on over plucked stabs with nothing beneath it, a riser seems to build, and on screen a man pulls a towel low over his face, lifts its edge, and stands calm. The slam at 2:35 arrives on a face that has already done its joke. That is the moment I would replay. After it the voice is chopped into urgent little bursts, and when it vanishes near 2:47 I feel relief and a trace of unease at once, a machine left running without its host. The fade-out leaves me with no arrival, as though a volume knob were turned down. The silent picture afterwards is odd: people fling their arms at no sound, and the last image is a tilted, blurred orange face glancing away. It feels forlorn to me.

## The moments that stay with you

- **The towel and the slam (both).** The picture moves first and the sound arrives on a finished gesture. Most readers would name this the best moment.
- **The first drop at 0:12 (sound).** The whole method in one event: twelve seconds of want, then weight.
- **The refrain (sound).** "That bird walk, watch me do that bird walk" sticks because it is low, wide and repeated until it becomes a chant. It is the nearest thing to a tune.
- **The first break (both).** The floor falls away and the picture shows children with bent arms. It teaches the trick the rest of the track repeats.
- **The beaked-hood children at the return (picture).** The weight comes back while they are still on screen and the hooded man arrives just after.
- **The solemn child (picture).** A face in a yellow fluffy hood with googly eyes, the funniest in the film.
- **The poncho figure's sign-off (picture).** A hand held to a drawn mouth like a microphone, as the caption ghosts away.
- **The silent tail (picture).** The last glance away, with no sound.

## What remains uncertain

Place, year and intent are open: a MySpace address and an iTunes line point to a time when both were in use, but the year is not established, the settings look suburban and English-speaking without fixing a country, and it cannot be shown from the frames that the makers meant parody. Whether the instruments are what they sound like, whether one voice or several is rapping, and what exactly is said (the words after 1:56 are not recoverable) are open. It is unknown whether the hooded man is the act, a fan or a stand-in, and where the archive clips come from. The silent ending is most probably a mismatch between audio and picture lengths, but that is unproven.

**Where sources disagree.**
- The dossier says the hooded man is on screen at all three bass returns; the interpretation corrects this to two of three, with one late, within chance. The report follows the interpretation.
- The dossier says the last frame has "one corner lit, then black"; the interpretation shows a fully lit last frame. The report follows the interpretation.
- Tempo: tracker about 152 (locally about 161) versus felt about 78 to 80. The report states both and prefers the half-time feel (M-H).
- Hook width: leakage from the "other" bed (earlier round) versus the voice's own width (later). The report treats it as the voice's own (M).
- One voice or a crowd chant: one voice with ad-libs and a widened hook (M-L); a crowd is neither shown nor excluded (L).
- Top-end ceiling: windows say 6 to 7, 8 to 10 and 16 kHz; unresolved, so the report says only "band-limited".
- Whether the hooded man is the voice: never established; the report does not claim it.
- The dossier says the picture "locks loosely to the big structural edges"; the interpretation says only a suggestive link. The report uses the weaker wording.

**Detector caveats.** Shot and cut counts (about 50 shots, 51 cuts in the sources, and the per-section cut rates) come from automatic detection with known misses and false cuts.
