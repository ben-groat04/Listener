# t_birdwalk: how it feels and what it means

## What this most probably is

This is almost certainly a low-budget promotional video for a rap single about a dance called the "bird walk". The caption names the act as "Soulja Unit", the song as "BIRD WALK", and says "AVAILABLE NOW on iTunes". The music is a programmed, bass-heavy club-style rap track, probably in the snap- or crunk-leaning family of programmed club rap, though place and period cannot be fixed from what was measured. The film is a collage of recycled and home-made-looking clips: a trio dancing in a pale room, a dancer in a yellow bedroom, a person in a yellow poncho with a box on their head, a giant yellow bird puppet, a person-sized bird costume, small children in beaked hoods, a dark grainy crowd clip, and a close-up young man in a yellow, orange and pink striped hooded towel. It is a cheeky, daft, affectionate boast. The era is possibly the late 2000s, since a MySpace address and an iTunes line were both in common use then, but the year is not established. The country is not established either; the settings look suburban and English-speaking. Whether the makers meant it as a joke cannot be told.

## The music

Start with the floor, because the whole track is about the floor. For the first twelve seconds there is no bass at all. A voice grabs the microphone ("Aye, man, we got this new dance") and a single drum hit lands about every second and a half. The missing low end is something you feel, a held breath, and a falling sweep just before 0:12 makes you want the weight back. At 0:12 it comes: a deep, near-pure sub-bass tone on one note, A, enters in about a second, the whole mix gets a little louder and narrower, and the track turns into a bouncing nod-along groove.

That is the method of the piece. There is no chord progression and no tune. The instruments sit on one note, with only brief visits to a fourth above it, and nothing in them settles whether it is major or minor; the only hint of a darker, bluesy colour is in the voice. What the music has instead is a count. The bass is taken away and put back on a rigid twelve-second grid (off at 0:47, back at 0:59, off at 1:59, back at 2:11, off at 2:23, back at 2:35), and loudness barely moves within each state. The drama is spatial and bodily rather than a swell. When the bass goes, the stereo picture opens wide and the track feels exposed and slightly weightless; when it returns, the picture snaps narrow and heavy again. It is the sound of subtraction.

The voice is speech, not song, and it defines the sections as much as the bass does. The refrain ("that bird walk, watch me do that bird walk") is low and wide. Verse one jumps up by more than an octave into a flat, near-monotone recitation, dead centre and dry, which feels hypnotic and a little claustrophobic, as if the rapper were standing very close. Verse two settles lower, on a steadier, more confident pitch. The sound is dry and close, with a band-limited, slightly boxy top, and the deep bass is a smooth, synthetic-sounding tone that is probably 808-like, though that is inference.

The rhythm is felt at a slow, swaggering pace, about 78 to 80 beats a minute, though the automatic tracker reports roughly double that, and which is "right" depends on how you count. Drums thicken before every cut, so you hear a rush into a wall, and the returns are bare: a hit, a gap, then the bass. After the first break the listener has learnt the count, and each later drop becomes the pleasure of anticipation rather than surprise.

Overall the mood is playful, swaggering and bodily over a heavy, slightly dark floor: neither sad nor tender. Almost none of that comes from harmony; it comes from delivery, timbre and rhythm. This listening was done through measurements (stems, spectrograms, chords, levels), not by ears, so every "feels" here is a reading tied to a number.

## How the picture works with it

The picture does not follow the beat. Cuts avoid the tracker's pulse more often than chance would, with only a weak, uncorrected hint of landing between beats, and there is no lock at the bar or at the twelve-second level. Yet the film holds together because of one colour world, yellow, orange and pink, and because the bird keeps coming back in different registers: studio puppet, car-park costume, children in beaked hoods, the home-made poncho and box, the little logo in the caption.

The cutting is fastest in the first full groove (0:12 to 0:47) and slowest in the breaks. Around the big edges the picture is often a little early rather than exactly on them. The dark crowd clip arrives just ahead of the first drop, and the towel is lifted before the slam at 2:35. That looks like an editor cutting by eye to a count he knew. It is a loose link, suggestive rather than strong, and viewers largely supply the sync themselves: whatever is on screen when the floor falls or returns seems to take the credit.

Where words and pictures meet, the effect is comic. "We just bird dance at home" plays over the trio in their pale room, though that word is disputed in transcription. "I'ma show you how it go" and "show you what it do" play over flailing and shuffling feet. A boast about money and birthday cake plays over a puppet in front of a children's alphabet wall (1:16). The box-headed poncho figure, with its drawn mouth, is simply funny whatever anyone intended.

## Meaning and context

The strongest reading is that a dance is being offered to you. The words promise to show you how it goes, the picture shows flapping arms, bent knees and a shuffle, and the caption turns the offer into a sale. The home-made clips look like bodies dancing for nobody, among themselves, while the packaged layers (puppet, hooded man, caption) look out at the viewer; the film negotiates between the two.

The comedy is deadpan: children's-programme imagery set against a club-rap boast, framed by a perfectly sincere sales message. It is affectionate and funny to watch, but it cannot be shown from the frames that the makers meant it as parody, and the sincerity of "available now" is part of why the gags land.

For lineage, the sources offer little beyond clues. The spoken tag at the start, the one-note boast and the fade-out are conventions of the genre, but that is a guess from outside the measurements. The sound (a falling-glide sub-bass, a sparse beat, a dry voice) is consistent with mid-2000s to 2010s club rap without separating crunk-era from trap-era, and the MySpace and iTunes text points to a time when both were in use. No older record is demonstrably sampled.

## What it is like to listen (interpretation)

I would sit forward for the first twelve seconds. One drum hit at a time, nothing underneath, and the longer it lasts the more my chest waits for something. When the bass lands at 0:12 it is a small physical shove, and my shoulders drop into a nod. I would like to think I would smile at the dark grainy clip that arrives with it, because the heaviest sound lands on the oldest-looking picture.

Verse one is where my attention narrows. The voice sits on one flat note, very close, and the repetition hypnotises a little; I stop hearing words and just ride the pulse. Slightly cramped, but not unpleasant. Then at 0:47 the floor goes, and I feel it in my breath before I think about it: space, exposure, children in yellow hoods with their elbows bent like wings. I find that stretch the tenderest in the film. At 0:59 the weight returns and I settle again, and from then on I know what is coming.

Knowing is the problem and the pleasure. Through the long second verse, nearly forty-five seconds of one note, one rhythm and a trio dancing in the same room, my mind drifts, and I notice the drums getting denser rather than anything actually changing. The bass flickers out around 1:43 and slams back at 1:47, and that small stumble wakes me for a moment. When break two arrives as an exact copy of the first, I feel a flash of déjà vu and, honestly, a touch of impatience: "again".

Then 2:23 changes my mind. The bass and drums leave together, the voice walks on over plucked stabs with nothing beneath it, a riser builds, and on screen a man pulls a towel low over his face, lifts its edge, and stands calm. The slam at 2:35 arrives on a face that has already done its joke. That is the moment I would replay. After it the voice is chopped into urgent little bursts, and when it vanishes near 2:47 I feel relief and a trace of unease at once, a machine left running without its host. The last fade at 3:01 leaves me with no arrival, as though a volume knob were turned down.

The silent picture afterwards (3:08 to 3:21) is odd. People fling their arms at no sound, and the last image is a tilted, blurred, orange face glancing away. Some of me finds that forlorn; some of me thinks the audio simply ran out. I cannot settle which.

## The moments that stay with you

- **2:33 to 2:36, the towel and the slam.** The picture moves first and the sound arrives on a finished gesture. Most readers would name this the best moment.
- **0:12, the first drop.** The whole method in one event: twelve seconds of want, then weight.
- **0:47 to 0:59 and its return.** It teaches the trick the rest of the track repeats.
- **0:59 to 1:01.** The floor returns while two children in beaked hoods are still on screen and the hooded man arrives half a second later.
- **2:44, the solemn child** in a yellow fluffy hood with googly eyes, the funniest face in the film.
- **3:01 to 3:05, the poncho figure** holding a hand to a drawn mouth like a microphone, a wry sign-off as the caption ghosts away.
- **3:12 to 3:20, the silent tail,** and the last glance away.

## What remains uncertain

Whether the instruments are what they sound like, whether the track really has a bar of one and a half or three seconds, and what exactly is said (the words after 1:56 are not recoverable; "pub" and "club" at 1:10 cannot be told apart) are all open. The same goes for whether one voice or several is rapping, whether the hooded man is the act, a fan or a stand-in, where the archive clips come from, and whether the silent ending is a gesture or an accident. Most probably it is a mismatch of lengths, since the audio is about 188 seconds and the picture about 201, but that is unproven, and the effect on a viewer is real either way.

**Where sources disagree.**
- The dossier says the hooded man is on screen at all three bass returns; the interpretation corrects this to two of three, with one late, within chance. The report follows the interpretation.
- The dossier says the last frame has "one corner lit, then black"; the interpretation shows a fully lit last frame. The report follows the interpretation.
- Tempo: tracker about 152 (locally about 161) versus felt about 78 to 80. The report states both and prefers the half-time feel (M-H).
- Hook width: leakage from the "other" bed (earlier round) versus the voice's own width (later). The report treats it as the voice's own (M).
- One voice or a crowd chant: one voice with ad-libs and a widened hook (M-L); a crowd is neither shown nor excluded (L).
- Top-end ceiling: windows say 6 to 7, 8 to 10 and 16 kHz; unresolved, so the report says only "band-limited".
- Whether the hooded man is the voice: never established; the report does not claim it.
- The dossier says the picture "locks loosely to the big structural edges"; the interpretation says only a suggestive link. The report uses the weaker wording.

**Detector caveats.** Shot and cut counts (about 50 shots, 51 cuts in the sources, and the per-section cut rates) come from automatic detection with known misses and false cuts.
