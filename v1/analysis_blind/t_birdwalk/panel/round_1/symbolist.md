## 1. What it is about

On the surface, a promotional clip for a novelty dance. Beneath that, I read it as a piece about **the borrowed body**: how a dance, a joke and a self travel by costume, copying and repetition rather than by authorship [interp]. Nobody in the film is the bird; everyone is wearing it. A puppet is a hand inside yellow feathers; a "person-sized costume" is a person inside; a poncho with a box on the head is a child's idea of a bird; children wear beaked hoods; a man wears a striped towel as a crest. Six registers of the same yellow figure [dossier, seen at 0:00, 0:09, 0:16, 0:18, 2:44]. The song's boast ("I show you how it go") is a promise to demonstrate, and what is demonstrated is imitation. The dance is something you do by becoming something else for three minutes.

A second theme sits under that: **being seen** [interp]. The hooded man keeps looking at the lens, mouthing, waving a hand across his face, as if wiping the glass (3:06-3:11 [seen]). The lyric is a boast about money, "cake", a birthday, a bad day for haters and a best tomorrow [read, heard-ish]. It is the oldest wish there is: to be looked at, and to be looked at as winning. Yet the video is home-made, low-resolution, reused clips on a loop. The gap between "so icy, so nice" and a bare bedroom with a striped bed is the poignancy, and the comedy.

## 2. The central metaphor: the floor that is taken away

The music's whole drama is subtraction: the sub-bass leaves and returns on a rigid 12-second count (off 0:47.25, on 0:59.25, off 1:59.25, on 2:11.25, off 2:23.5, on 2:35.5 [read]). Loudness stays flat within each state; what changes is the floor and the space. I take that as the piece's one real idea: **the ground goes, and you keep dancing**. In the breaks the voice and the "bird walk" refrain walk across nothing, like a cartoon character over the cliff edge before looking down [interp]. Then the floor comes back and the body is glad.

The picture rhymes with this almost too well. Every dark crowd shot (0:12, 0:47, 1:58, 2:36) is the oldest, murkiest footage, with a chequered floor [seen at 0:12-0:13], and it arrives at the first drop and at two of three break entries [dossier; I checked only 0:12]. The chequered floor is literally a floor. [interp] The bass comes back each time on or beside the hooded man (0:59.8, 2:11, 2:35 [dossier]): the face returns as the ground returns. That is a human claim, not a mechanical one: the thing that holds you up is being looked at.

## 3. Motifs and visual rhymes

- **Yellow as one colour for everything.** Bird feathers, poncho, bedroom walls, hood stripes, caption lettering [seen, sheet 01]. The caption strip itself (yellow-and-white, with a bird logo from 0:14) makes the commercial frame the same colour as the joke. Even the advertisement is wearing the bird. [interp]
- **Hood as beak.** The striped hood peaks above the forehead like a crest; his open mouth in the 0:18-0:19 frames sits where the puppet's beak sat a second earlier [seen]. Whether the editor intended it, the edit asks us to see a man becoming a bird in the way the puppet earlier "speaks" in a rap voice (beak opens 0:03-0:04 [seen]; voice from 0:00.9 [read]). [spec as to intent]
- **The covered face.** Hood, box, towel pulled low (2:33), covered lower faces on dancers [dossier]. In a piece about display, a lot of faces are hidden: you may perform only if you are masked. The box on the poncho figure has a drawn face [seen, 3:01-3:05]. A face on a face.
- **Feet.** Legs on asphalt (1:04), sneakers (1:47) [dossier]. The dance is finally just feet. The lyric about "all-white" and "feet" lands on sneakers 3-5 s late [dossier]: the picture is always a little behind the words, like imitation.
- **The solemn child (2:44).** A small child in a fluffy beaked hood with googly eyes, not smiling [dossier; not opened by me]. If the frame is as described, it is the most honest face in the film: a person who has been told to be a bird and is considering it.
- **The A-B-A bird at 1:16-1:29.** Puppet in front of an alphabet and number board, then the car-park costume, then the puppet again [dossier]. A children's lesson set under a boast about money: learn your letters, then watch me get paid. [interp]

## 4. How it makes me feel, and which moments do it

1. **0:00-0:04, the deadpan gag (strong).** A close-up of a giant yellow puppet bird against flat blue, the beak opening at 0:03-0:04 [seen], while a rap voice says it has a new dance [read, heard-ish]. Tender absurdity: you are laughing before you know it is allowed. The held breath of the intro (no bass, a drum hit every 1.55 s [read]) keeps the smile slightly nervous.
2. **0:12.25, the drop onto darkness.** The bass lands as the picture goes murky green-amber with a bare-chested crowd and a yellow bird-costume figure [seen 0:12-0:13]. The sound gets heavier while the image gets older and dirtier, which feels like a door opening into a cellar party. Dread and delight together.
3. **0:47-0:59, break 1.** The floor drops, the image widens [read]; I feel exposed, then, at 0:54, children in beaked hoods flap their elbows [dossier]. Innocence set into the gap where the weight was. Best idea in the piece, as I read it [interp; frame not opened by me].
4. **2:23.5-2:35.5, the beatless hook.** A tower of foam blocks tumbles as the bass and drums cut together [dossier]; the hood man pulls the towel over his face (2:33) and, as the slam returns at 2:35.5, lifts it with both hands [dossier]. This is the film's one real gesture of meaning: hide, then reveal, on the beat of the return. A peek-a-boo, which is how every small child first learns that things persist when unseen. That is, to me, the emotional core: the bass is object permanence. [interp; the moment is from the dossier, not opened by me]
5. **2:47-3:01, the voice leaves.** The mouth shuts at 2:46-2:48 as the vocal stem goes silent [dossier]; the dancers keep moving over a drum-led, voice-free stretch; the glide falls like a deflating whoop (2:57-3:01, low confidence [read]). I feel something close to tenderness: the boast is over and only the body is left.
6. **3:01-3:21, the bookend and the silence.** The poncho figure holds a hand to its drawn "mouth" like a microphone [seen, 3:01-3:05], then the hooded man wipes at the lens [seen, 3:06-3:11], then the trio again, faster, with more people [seen, 3:12-3:18], then an extreme close-up of the face, tilted, filling the frame [seen, 3:19], then black. The audio ended at 3:08.15 [dossier, decoded]. Thirteen silent seconds of people still dancing is the most moving thing in the piece: the music was only ever an excuse for the bodies, and when it is withdrawn they carry on regardless, like the cartoon character who has already walked off the cliff and still has not fallen. [interp]

## 5. What is clever, what is weak

**Clever**
- The whole structure is one trick (take the floor away, put it back) and the 12-second count teaches it within one cycle [read]. The first break makes the body learn it; later returns pay off the learning.
- Picture sits close to this structure at the big edges though not to the beat: five of seven edges have a cut within 0.9 s [dossier, his arithmetic; suggestive, n = 7]. Measured cuts avoid locking to the tracked beat (21.6 per cent within 80 ms against 42 per cent expected [dossier, sync data]). A video that obeys the architecture and ignores the pulse reads to me as a *loose* hand: someone editing by the feel of the section, not the bar. Consistent with home-made; I will not call it a design decision [spec].
- The silent tail. Whether intended or not, ending the sound at 3:08 and leaving the picture running is the best formal gesture in the piece.

**Weak, or at least unproven**
- Reuse at heavy rates: trio 11 times, hooded man 15 [dossier]. Repetition is thematic, but the effect is also thin: I cannot tell the difference between a deliberate litany and a shortage of footage. Both are legitimate readings; the evidence does not choose.
- The caption layer present for nearly the whole film [dossier; seen at 0:14-0:19 and 3:00-3:05] removes any chance that the piece is just a joke: it is an advert, and says so repeatedly. It cuts against any symbolic reading I make. I should hold that honestly: the symbols may be accidents of cheap material, and the audience's pleasure in them is real regardless of authorship.
- The music itself supplies no harmonic story (one tonic A, speech not melody [read]); all the poetry is in subtraction and in the picture. A reader who wants tune will find the piece bare, and I think that is part of why the jokes and faces carry so much.

## 6. A note of caution about my own reading

I am working blind from a dossier plus two contact sheets. The strongest claims above (peek-a-boo at 2:33-2:35, the children at 0:54, the solemn child) rest on the dossier's description and were not opened by me. The shapes I find (borrowed body, object permanence) are interpretations; there is no evidence that anyone intended them. I also cannot hear: "the bass lands" is from measurements.

## 7. Three questions for the other panellists

1. **To the music/sound panellists:** the breaks are subtraction, but the *voice stays on*, even in the beatless hook (2:23.5-2:35.5 [read]). Does that change what the voice means in a break, boast or reassurance? And does the measured width (voice and bed opening wide, bass mono) support the reading that the break is "exposure" and the groove is "enclosure"?
2. **To the picture and structure panellists:** are the three bass returns on the hooded man (0:59.8, 2:11, 2:35) a pattern a viewer would feel, or an artefact of his having 15 appearances in 201 s? Please test: how often would a random edge land on him?
3. **To the context and meaning panellists (blind, so from the frames only):** is the hooded man the performer, a fan, or a stand-in? If his mouth is in time with the voice (tested only by seeing it, not measured), the "bird costume" reading changes; if it is not, the film is *about* lip-syncing someone else's boast. Which does the picture support, frame by frame?
