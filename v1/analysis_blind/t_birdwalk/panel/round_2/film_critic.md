## 1. Concessions first

**The Sceptic is right about the hooded man at the bass returns, and I over-sold it.** In round 1 I called "the bass comes back to a face" the one recurring editorial idea. I now count the hooded man's shots from the dossier timetable: about 47 s of 201 s, so roughly 23 per cent of the running time [calc; the shot edges are the dossier's, so treat it as +/- 2 points]. Checking the three returns strictly:
- 2:11.25: he is on screen, mid-shot [dossier, and the Sceptic saw it on sheet 07].
- 2:35.5: he is on screen [seen on sheet 08, 2:35 frame].
- 0:59.25: he is **not**. The shot at that moment is the children in beaked hoods (0:54-0:59.8) and the hooded man arrives 0.54 s after the bass. The dossier's "on screen at all three" is true only if a half-second late counts.

So it is two of three, not three of three. With a 23 per cent share, getting two or more of three by chance is about 12 per cent [calc: 3 x 0.23^2 x 0.77 + 0.23^3]. That is nothing. The other four edges (0:12.25, 0:47.25, 1:59.25, 2:23.5) are all landed on something else, three of them on the dark crowd clip or the puppet. My honest statement is now: there are two kinds of edge in this film, and the dark clip owns the entries, but I cannot show that the hooded man owns the returns. The pattern is weak, and I withdraw "the one recurring editorial idea". What I can still defend is narrower: the crowd clip arrives at the first drop and at two of three break entries (0:11.85, 0:46.98, 1:58.39), and at all three it is early by 0.3 to 0.9 s [dossier]. n = 3 again, so I call it a habit worth noting, not a proof.

**The Sceptic is also right that the dark/dim contrast is partly free.** The green tint and the blown-out cream of the hooded man come with the sources. I said "the archive is placed, not handled", and that was already a concession. What I will still credit is sequencing: the editor chose where the dark clip goes.

## 2. Answering the questions put to me

**Music critic: does the picture treat the drops as events?** Mostly no, with exceptions. A cut within 0.9 s of an edge happens at five of seven; but that includes the 0.86 s miss at 1:59 and the 0.5 s late cut at 0:59. At 2:11.25 and 2:23.5 the picture ignores the edge by cutting and lets a running shot carry it. Two things the editor does that read as an answer to the music:
1. **Contrast at entries**: the picture goes dark as the bass goes (0:47, 1:58) and goes dark as the bass arrives (0:12). So the dark clip is not a mirror of the bass; it marks "a change is happening" whichever way [interp]. The music critic's finding that cuts into breaks are preceded by drum thickening, and returns are bare, suggests the editor could have known the break was coming but not the return. That fits the picture's pattern: the early cuts (-0.27, -0.86, -0.4) sit at the thickened entries, and the late one (+0.54) sits at the bare return. It is a small asymmetry, and I like it, but it is five data points [calc/interp].
2. **The hold on a running shot**: at 2:11 and 2:23.5 the editor does not cut. Not cutting is a choice at a drop only if the editor was aware of the drop. The 2:23.5 case is the best argument for awareness (see section 3).

**Sceptic: what would a random edit look like, and how often would a return land on the hooded man?** See above: about one in eight for two of three. As for the foam blocks (2:21.5-2:26.65), I have now looked at sheet 08. At 2:22 the tower stands with the puppet to the right; at 2:23 the puppet's head is up beside it; at 2:24 the blocks are tilting; at 2:25 and 2:26 the tower is down and the puppet is among the rubble [seen]. The break is at 2:23.5 [read]. So the collapse begins within a second of the cut. That is either the luckiest alignment in the film or an editor choosing a clip because it falls over. I cannot separate those. A scorer's test: any random 5 s clip of a source with a single big event has about a one-in-three chance of putting that event within a second of a given edge. I will not claim intent.

**Sceptic: the single frame that shows the makers are in on the joke.** I will answer, but carefully. Not the puppet (blind mode forbids me relying on what it is), and not the caption (which is earnest). The best candidate is 3:01-3:05 [seen, sheet 10]: a person in a poncho with a **box on the head and a face drawn on it**, one hand held up to the box where a mouth would be, bobbing in front of a garage door. That is a costume made for being funny and the filming does not hide it. A serious promoter does not end the music with that. Against that reading: the strip in sheet 10 at 3:00-3:04 is as earnest as ever, and it fades at 3:04, only as the shot changes. So my answer: the makers are in on it **and** selling. These are not opposites; the caption strip is the sales pitch of someone who knows the pitch is funny. What would count against me: any shot where an actor is obviously supposed to look cool and is filmed straight. The hooded man comes closest (3:06-3:11, deadpan stare, hand wiping the lens [seen]), and I read him as cool and aware, not earnest. That is [interp].

## 3. The silent tail: new evidence from sheet 10

The Sceptic asks whether the 13 s of silence is a decision or a leftover, and I said "export accident" in round 1. Opening sheet 10 changes my view a little.
- The credit card **returns at 3:06** [seen], while the music is still fading (audio ends 3:08.15 [read/dossier]). The card text is half-faded in at 3:06 and fully there at 3:07.
- It then **stays over three different shots**: the hooded man 3:06-3:11, the trio 3:12-3:18, and, as far as the frames show, up to the last close-up [seen; the 3:19 frame has no visible text, so the card seems to be gone or off-frame by then].
- At the start it ran 0:02-0:11 over three shots too. So the film has a **planned book-end card of similar form at both ends**, and the end card starts on a cut (3:05.8) two seconds *before* the sound ends.

What this tells me: the picture was cut to a planned length and an end-card section, and the audio file is simply shorter (188.15 s against 201.4 s [dossier, decoded]). The editor could have placed the end card to cover the fade and then stopped, but chose to run another shot after it. So silence was not "forgotten"; the editor kept cutting (trio at 3:12, face at 3:19) with no sound to cut to. That raises one question I cannot answer in blind mode: was the video cut to a longer version of the song and the soundtrack was later trimmed? The one thing I can say is that the tail has an editorial structure (card, trio, face), so it is not just a held frame. I now lean to "picture planned, sound cut short" and drop "export accident" as too glib. The Symbolist's "most moving thing in the piece" is [interp] and I would say silent bodies are moving only if you decide the silence is meant; I cannot.

## 4. Where I agree, push back or add

- **Agree with the First-timer** that 2:33-2:35 is the best picture-and-sound teamwork, and with the Insider's "the towel is a curtain". But sheet 08 adds a precision none of us gave: the lift happens at 2:34 (his hands raise the towel edge, mouth open [seen]), and by the 2:35 frame he is already still, hood up, calm. The bass is at 2:35.5 [read], and the cut to the dark clip at 2:36.2 [dossier]. So the **reveal is a beat before the slam**, not on it, and the slam lands on a face that has already finished its gesture. That is better, not worse: the face anticipates the music (like the 0.4 s early cut at 0:12) and the sound then arrives on his stillness. An editor who cut the reveal ON the slam would be matching; this one is preparing. That fits a consistent habit I now see: **the picture moves first, and the sound follows**. Three early cuts into the drops, the 0.7 s reveal before the slam. The exception is the late cut at 0:59.
- **Push back on the Symbolist's "object permanence" reading** only as to authorship: it fits the towel moment beautifully, but sheet 08 shows he hides at 2:33, lifts at 2:34, and is calm at 2:35, with 2:36 the first dark frame. Peek-a-boo is there; the music then has to arrive. It does not arrive "as" the reveal; it arrives after the child has stopped playing. The structure is "hide, show, wait, floor returns" and I think "wait" is more interesting than "reveal".
- **Agree with the Sceptic** that the beak-opening sync was never tested and one frame at 0:03-0:04 is thin; but the first-spoken-line gag works with or without sync because the voice comes from 0:00.9 and the beak is open by 0:03. A viewer's eye does the sync for free (that is the whole mechanism of a dubbed puppet). I would call it a viewer-made sync [interp].
- **Add to the First-timer's boredom point**: the trio clip is used for about 54 s. On sheet 10 (3:12-3:18) the same room shows more people, a red-cap dancer in front, faster moves [seen]. That does not look like a new angle on the same take; it looks like a different take. So the dossier's "same clip cut 11 ways" is partly false: there are at least two takes, probably more. I cannot say how many, and I withdraw "the trio clip is cut into different lengths" as a description of one take.

## 5. The thread I would develop: the picture is always early

Pulling the numbers together [dossier/calc]: the crowd-clip cuts lead the bass edge by 0.4, 0.27 and 0.86 s; the 2:35.5 slam follows the reveal by about 0.7 s; the 0:59 return is the only late one (+0.54 s). Mean cut phase within a beat is 0.51 and the Rayleigh test is only just significant [dossier, sync], so the beat-level story is noise; but at the scale of the 12 s blocks the picture seems to lead. Mouth, pointing and arm gestures then run **ahead of or across** the lines: sneakers 3-5 s after the "on your feet" line [dossier], picture energy lagging the drum rise by 2-3 s at 1:34 [data], cut rate lagging loudness by about 4 s [data]. So the picture **leads at the section edges and lags inside the sections**. That is a plausible description of someone editing by eye in a timeline: placing big cuts before the section change so they register, then letting the material run. It is [interp], and the sample is too small to defend as a rule. A test I would like: for every cut in the film, compute its signed distance to the nearest "other"/vocal/bass change, not only the bass edge, and see whether the early bias is real.
