## 1. The question that came to me most: earnest promotion or knowing joke?

The film critic, first-timer, sceptic and music critic all asked a version of this. The sceptic asked for "the single frame or second that tells me the makers are in on the joke", and for what would count against it. I will try to answer that properly, and I will start by conceding ground.

**Concession.** In round 1 I called the piece "a joke built on collision" and said I could not tell whether the puppet footage was tribute or parody. The sceptic is right that the on-screen text is earnest on its face: the strip "Soulja Unit 'BIRD WALK' AVAILABLE NOW on iTunes" (about 0:14-3:04) and the credit card with a MySpace address are straightforward sales copy [seen, sheet 10 at 3:00-3:11]. Nothing in the text winks. So "the makers are in on a joke" cannot be proved from the caption layer, and I withdraw any suggestion that the frame as a whole is ironic.

**But there are two different things being called "the joke".** (a) Whether the *act* knows the images are funny. (b) Whether the *packaging* is sincere. These can both be true. Craze-song promo of this kind (my [infer], low-medium: the shape of a dance-instruction single sold through MySpace and iTunes) is normally sincere about the product and happy to be silly in the pictures. A silly picture is not a sign of insincerity; it is a way of being shareable.

**Frame evidence that bears on (a):**

- **3:01-3:05, the poncho figure.** Someone has drawn a face on the box (eyes and a beak-like shape, yellow card) and holds a hand to it like a microphone [seen, sheet 10]. A drawn face on a box is a handmade gag by whoever filmed it. That is evidence the *footage* was made playfully. It does not say whether the act made it or a fan did.
- **3:06-3:11, the hooded man.** He looks at the lens, hand sweeps up across his face, tilts his chin, eyebrows level, no smile [seen, sheet 10]. His expression is deadpan, and the gestures (a "wipe" across the lens, a finger flick) are the vocabulary of someone addressing a webcam or phone camera. Deadpan to camera is, in my reading, the one place where the performer seems to know he is being funny. Against that: deadpan is also simply what an ordinary non-actor does when a lens is a few inches from his face [infer].
- **2:44-2:46, the unsmiling child in the beaked hood.** The film critic and I both lean on this. It is a found-looking clip (soft, warm, older-looking), so the child's solemnity is not the makers' performance; the *editor's choice* to place it there is [infer, weak].

**What would count against the joke reading:** if the hooded man were visibly sincere (beaming, singing along, hands working a choreography) in every appearance. He is not smiling in most of the frames I opened (3:06-3:11), but the dossier has him shouting and grinning at 0:25-0:33, so he varies. I cannot call it settled. **My verdict: the caption is sincere, the picture is deliberately cheap and good-humoured, and the two are not in conflict. "Warm joke in a sincere frame" is my position; I rate it medium-low, not high.**

## 2. Symbolist's question: performer, fan or stand-in?

I cannot test lip-sync from still frames and will not pretend to. What I can add from the sheets:

- **He is shot like a self-made clip, not a music-video performance.** Tight, off-centre, tilted, close enough that his hand blocks the lens at 3:06-3:11 and his face fills the frame at 3:19 [seen]. Lit by a flat cream wall and what looks like available light [infer]. There is no stage, no second camera angle, no crew-style framing.
- **The towel hood has no obvious performance logic**: it is a bath-towel stripe pattern worn as a cowl [seen]. This looks like a home prop, matching the cardboard-and-poncho figure. I would say "someone in the same DIY world as the poncho" is better supported than "polished lead artist".
- **The caption names an act, not him.** So "fan, member of the act, or the act's own comic mascot" are all open. I would put "affiliated clip-maker or crew member" as slightly likelier than "random fan" only because the credit card closes on him (3:06 onward) and the film ends on his face (3:19-3:21): an editor gave him the last word. That is an editor's decision, not proof of who he is [infer, low].
- **Pick the frame that decides it.** The test the symbolist wants is mouth shape against the vocal. The one pair I can see by eye is the dossier's 2:46-2:49 (mouth closed as the voice stops). I agree with the dossier that this is a coincidence worth testing, not a test.

## 3. Sceptic's and symbolist's numerical challenge (I did the arithmetic)

Both asked how often a returning bass would land on the hooded man by chance. I summed his appearance intervals from the dossier timeline (15 appearances) and get about 47 s of 201 s, so about 23 per cent of screen time [data, my arithmetic from dossier times, +/- a few seconds]. For three bass returns:

- Probability that all three land on him by chance: 0.23^3, about 1.3 per cent.
- Probability that at least two of three do: about 14 per cent.
- Probability at least one does: about 55 per cent.

So "all three" is unusual (about 1 in 80) if the three edges were drawn independently. Caveats I owe the room: (1) the dossier itself says the 2:11 return is mid-shot and the 2:35 one falls near the end of his shot with the cuts at -0.7 and +0.7 s either side, so "on him" is partly generous, with the 2:35 edge only just inside; (2) three cases is tiny and I picked this pattern after seeing it, so a one-in-eighty is not a clean p-value [interp]; (3) the dark crowd clip landing on 3 of 7 edges is a separate coincidence, and the sceptic is right to keep the two apart. I would say: **suggestive, probably partly intentional given the number of such coincidences, not proven.** I would not put it in the "what the film means" column.

## 4. The sceptic's silent ending, and the question of what the world tells us

The sceptic asked whether the 13 s of silence is planned. From sheet 10: the credit card is on from 3:06 and still on at 3:12-3:18 over the trio [seen], so the last 15 s are a *credit-card ending*, not a loose leftover; the caption design supports a planned "end card" more than a missing soundtrack. Against that: the audio stops at 3:08.15 [data], while the card keeps running. A reasonable [infer, medium-low] reading is that the music's length and the video's end card were set separately (the song was faded to its length; the video was padded with end-card footage). That fits promo practice where a song's length and a video's length are independently fixed. So I read it as **planned frame, careless seam.**

## 6. Where I disagree with a colleague

The symbolist reads the dark crowd clip landing at the drops as "the thing that holds you up" (chequered floor as ground). It is an attractive reading, but it needs the pairing to be intended. Three of seven edges is within what a random edit gives (the first-timer's 37 per cent per edge would predict about 2.6 of 7), so the pairing could be chance. I would treat the "floor" image as a good *reception* reading (it is what a viewer might feel) rather than an authorial one.
