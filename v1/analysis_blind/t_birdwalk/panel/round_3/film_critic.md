## 3. The thread I most want to develop: the film is a re-use machine, and the re-use is the craft

About nine sources fill 201 s in 52 shots. Of the 11 trio shots, 5 bedroom shots and 4 crowd shots, that is 20 of 52 shots from three sources [dossier counts]. A viewer expecting a "music video" sees a promotional reel, and the editing intelligence is mostly in what it refuses to do: it does not cut to the beat (confirmed at two grids), it does not build a picture climax, and it does not use the best clip (the hooded man) at the hardest moments. What it does is alternate *registers of the same gag*: bird as puppet, bird as costume, bird as a box with a drawn face, bird as a child in a hood, bird as a man in a stripy towel. Colour does the editing the cuts do not: yellow, orange and pink recur in the hood, the poncho and the bird's feathers, so a cut between the two never feels like a change of film. That is also why the fast alternation of dim archive and bright cream faces reads as texture, not as noise. The dim clip arrives as a different tone of the same colour world, not as a rupture, and I call that careful sequencing even if I cannot show it was intended.

This also changes how I read the structural moments. The music does its job with the 12 s template. The picture's job is to make the repeat feel new and to give a face to the quiet. It does this three times, and only the third (2:21.5-2:36: tumbling blocks, loosest trio, towel gag) is clearly well made. First-timer said picture did the work the music had stopped doing at the third pass; I agree, and add that this is the only place the film's pacing, gag and music thicken together.

## 4. Settled reading (5 sentences)

1. t_birdwalk is a promotional reel cut from about nine reused sources over a programmed 12-second-block rap track, with a credit card at each end and a caption strip throughout, and it knows it is a joke without ceasing to sell.
2. The editing does not follow the beat at the tracked or the felt pulse (tested both), locks only loosely to the 12 s block edges (five of seven within 0.9 s, which is not strong evidence), and gets its coherence from a controlled yellow-orange-pink palette and from the alternation of bird "registers" instead.
3. The one clearly crafted passage is 2:21-2:36, where falling blocks, a trio take that loosens, a towel pulled down and lifted, and the return of the bass produce a small comic structure of hide, show, wait, slam.
4. The picture ends on a long run of planned shots (3:01-3:21) while the sound finishes at 3:08.15, so the 13 s of silence is most probably a production gap that the picture happened to make touching; I read it as unintended and effective.
5. What the viewer takes away is affection: a daft dance, a deadpan hooded face, children in beaked hoods, a box with a drawn mouth, all of it handled with more care of colour than of rhythm.

## 5. The single moment

**2:33-2:36**: the hooded man pulls the towel down over his face at 2:33, lifts the edge with both hands and opens his mouth at 2:34.8, stands calm and finished by about 2:35, and the bass slams in at 2:35.5 on a face that has already done its gesture. In three seconds it holds the film's habits (a face, a hood, a delayed return) and its sense of humour.
