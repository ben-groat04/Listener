## 3. The thread I think is most interesting: a promo that works on a viewer without a plan

Every panellist has circled one fact from different sides. The music is built by subtraction on one note, with a 12 s count the body learns after the first break [data]. The picture follows loudness loosely, ignores the beat, cuts nowhere near two of the three returns [data], and was probably laid over an audio file that was 13 s too short. Yet it holds together for a first-time viewer, and I think I can say why. Two things. First, the yellow bird costume everywhere is a cheap way of making disparate clips look like one world (I will not call it a symbol). Second, the music's repetition acts as a frame the viewer fills with whatever appears: a deadpan toddler, a puppet, a man in a hood. When the bass returns, whatever is on screen gets the credit. That is the most honest way I can state what the panel has been arguing about: the pairing is largely made in the viewer's head, and the film exploits that by being full of faces and gags that can take credit for the drop. Nobody needs to have planned it for it to work, and nobody should claim they did.

What bores me stays the same: verse 2 (1:10-1:56), where one note, one rhythm and one trio clip go on for 46 s. What moves me is small: the children at 0:54 and the towel moment before 2:35.5. What I would tell a friend: watch 0:00-1:10 and 2:20-2:50, skip the middle, and turn the sound off for the last 13 seconds because there is none.

## 4. Settled reading (five sentences)

1. This is a cheap, cheerful dance-craze promo: a spoken boast about a "bird walk" over one note, with a card advertising the single on screen throughout [seen/data].
2. The music's whole drama is subtraction on a rigid 12 s count (bass out, image wide, bass back and narrow), loudness flat within each state, and it is the music, not the picture, that gives the piece its shape [data].
3. The picture is a loose, human edit that speeds up and slows down with the sound (r = 0.27, about 4 s lag), mostly ignores the beat and the drops, and gets its charm from deadpan children, a puppet and a yellow-everything look, not from synchrony.
4. The first break is the best idea, the second is a literal repeat that I felt as padding, the third (hook alone, foam tower falling, towel lifted a beat before the bass returns) is the best made; verse 2 is where I drift.
5. The ending is a fade that finishes at 3:08 followed by about 13 s of silent picture ending on a blurred face, which reads as an unfinished edit rather than a gesture, and I hold the tone to be playful and slightly eerie but not sad.

**The single moment that best captures the piece:** 0:59.25 to 1:01, the first return of the bass after twelve bare seconds, with two children in yellow beaked hoods still on screen and the hooded man arriving a second later: the floor comes back and the picture, unplanned or not, has two deadpan faces ready to take the credit.
