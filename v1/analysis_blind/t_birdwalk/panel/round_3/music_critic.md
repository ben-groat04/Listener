Blind mode. I cannot hear; sound claims are the music reading's measurements. Tags: [read] music reading; [data] sync/shots files; [calc] a test I ran this round on `scenes/shots.json`; [interp]; [spec]. Times m:ss. British spelling.

## 1. A test I ran, because three of you asked for it

The Film critic and the Sceptic lean on the tracker's beat grid (median interval 0.394 s, about 152 BPM), and I said in round 2 that cuts avoiding a double-time grid tells us little about the real pulse. So I re-ran it [calc]. I took the 51 cut times from shots.json and tested their phase against grids of 0.75 s (the felt beat), 1.5 s, 3.0 s and 12.0 s, anchored on the bass edges (0:12.25, 0:59.25, 2:11.25), with 2,000 random-cut permutations as the null.

- 0.75 s grid: resultant length R = 0.12, permutation p = 0.46 to 0.48; cuts within 80 ms of a beat 18 to 25 per cent against 21 per cent expected.
- 1.5 s: R = 0.14, p about 0.36. 3.0 s: R = 0.06, p = 0.82. 12.0 s: R = 0.06, p = 0.85; only 2 per cent of cuts within 80 ms of a 12 s grid point, 1 per cent expected.

Result: at no pulse level, and from no anchor, do the cuts lock. I concede my own suspicion that the "not dance-literate" finding might be an artefact of the double-time tracker: it is not. The picture ignores the pulse at 80 BPM as well as at 152. Caveat: the 0.75 s pulse is inferred from bass spacing and block arithmetic [read, medium-high], and its phase from a bass edge good to 0.1 to 0.25 s, so an 80 ms window is tight for a 0.75 s grid; I put weight on the permutation p-values, not the window counts. That also answers the First-timer: nothing cuts on 12 s multiples.

## 2. Questions put to me

**Symbolist: does anything in the measurements make the voice at 2:23.5 different from breaks 1 and 2?** Honestly, little. Width in break 3 (0.5 to 0.66) sits inside the break 1 and 2 range (0.37 to 0.71) [read]; hook voice width is the voice's own [read]. What differs is not the voice's level or spread but what has gone: in breaks 1 and 2 a drum crack every 1.4 to 1.57 s keeps a pulse alive and the voice is chopped onto those cracks; in break 3 the drums go to silence (-53 dB, then none) and the voice keeps the whole refrain, with plucked stabs on a 6.2 s figure (medium) [read]. So the voice in break 3 is not doing something new; it is the only thing doing anything, with no pulse to hang from. That is a structural fact, not a vocal one. I can therefore say "the piece's most exposed twelve seconds" with data; "emotional centre" is the picture's contribution (the towel gag, the collapsing blocks) and my own reading, marked [interp]. I accept that this is picture-assisted, and I will not claim it for the sound alone.

**First-timer: would the three blocks feel like a form, and does the paste change the shape?** Played blind, I think of it as a form of 1 + 1 + 1 with the second a copy. Break 1 sets the rule, break 2 is its twin (72.00 s lag, r 0.95 [read]) and is the one passage a listener may feel as "again" rather than "more". Break 3 changes the rule (drums go too), so the three give statement, repeat, variation: a small A, A, B shape. The paste does not damage that shape; it makes it ritual, since a copy exactly 72 s on, six blocks later, is what a loop-based arranger does. It is padding in the sense of adding no information, and groove in the sense of the body welcoming it. Both, as I said; break 2 is where padding is nearest.

**Film critic: which edge is easier for an editor, entry or return? Does drum thickening explain the early cuts?** Entries are the findable edge: the drums thicken (13 onsets in the last 3 s before 1:59.25, 8 before 2:23.5 [read]), whereas returns are bare (hit, gap, bass, no fill). An editor working from a waveform sees a dense patch then a drop at entries, and a quiet gap then a bass onset at returns, so entries are easier to anticipate and returns easier to react to. That fits your asymmetry: three early cuts (-0.4, -0.27, -0.86 s) and one late (+0.54 s). I agree with your rule of thumb, with its sample of five. But 1:59.25 is 0.86 s early, wider than the 0.25 s frame uncertainty and than the drum burst's own length, so I would not stretch the explanation to cover it.

**Sceptic: is the 2:47.5 voice exit a pasted loop ending or a mixing choice, and what separates the two?** I cannot separate them from this data and I withdraw "eerie" and "wry deflation" as claims; they are mood words only. Evidence for a mix choice: the vocal stem drops cleanly to about -70 dB from 2:49 while drums rise about 10 dB from 2:50 and the bass continues [read]. Evidence for a loop that ran out: the late hook repeats at 12.09 to 12.21 s intervals, so the last repeat, from 2:35.5, would have ended about 2:47.6 to 2:47.7, matching the exit. So a loop that ran out is the cheaper reading, and I now lean that way (medium-low): 2:35.5 to 2:47.5 is the last block, and the voice does not continue into the next because there is no next block of voice. That makes the voice-free stretch a backing-track bar count completing, not a gesture. The 2:57 to 3:00 glide, at 15 dB under the chant with a stem level of -33 to -56 dB, is probably bleed or a sweep and I will not read it as the voice letting go (medium-low).

**Insider: is the intro voice a hype-man drop recorded separately?** I can only offer measurements: the opening voice is dry, close and mono-ish against a wide quiet bed, 3 to 18 dB above the other layers [read]. That is the same dry, close treatment as verse 1 and 2. Nothing in the data says it was recorded separately from the rest; a voice that opens a track in the register of a spoken tag is a convention of the genre [spec; outside the data]. Nothing measured shows the song and video opening were made as one product, and I will not claim it.

## 4. The thread I would develop: subtraction as the arrangement, and why it needs the voice

The most interesting finding of this panel, from the music's side, is that the piece has almost no content in the usual sense: one note, no progression, a rigid 12 s grid, and flat loudness within each state [read]. The form is made from three things that can be taken away: the sub (47 per cent of the energy below 60 Hz), the drums, and the width of the bed. Because the sub is mono and the bed is wide, taking the sub away opens the image from 0.04 to 0.35 up to 0.37 to 0.71: a single act changes level, weight and space together, and the listener feels all three as one event. This is why the break works so reliably at 0:47, and why, once the listener has learnt the count, the return is a pleasure of prediction rather than surprise. Then the voice has a particular job: in the grooves it is the thing to nod to (verse 1 on D4 near-mono, verse 2 on G3 dry); in the breaks it holds the refrain so the track keeps a shape while the floor is out. The voice is the continuity layer, the bass is the punctuation. That is a well-known dance-floor logic, and the piece is a plain, even naive, use of it: it does not try to be more.

What the data cannot give me, and I say so for the final time: I cannot hear whether the bass hits like a club system or sounds thin; kick versus snare; or whether the voice is one person. The "808" and "crunk-era" labels are inference and stay at low-medium.

## 5. Settled reading (five sentences)

1. A programmed, one-note (A) rap track whose whole form is a 12 s count in which the sub-bass is taken away and put back, flat in loudness inside each state, so the drama is bodily and spatial (the sub leaves and the image opens wide) rather than melodic (high for the structure, medium for the feeling).
2. The voice carries the continuity: low, wide hook; near-mono recited verse 1 on D4; dry verse 2 on G3; and it holds the refrain through the breaks so the track keeps its shape when the floor is gone (medium-high).
3. Break 2 is a literal paste of break 1 and break 3 is the variation (bass and drums cut together, the voice alone over nothing), so the three breaks read as statement, repeat, variation, and the repeat is where groove tips into padding (high on the copy, medium on the feeling).
4. The picture does not lock to the music at any pulse level (my own re-test at 0.75, 1.5, 3.0 and 12.0 s, p 0.35 to 0.86), so the sense that the film "follows" the music comes from the sound's own clarity and from a few coincident edges, not from editing to the beat; the silent 13 s tail is a length mismatch more probably than a gesture (medium-low).
5. It is cheeky, swaggering and playful over a dark, heavy floor, comic in its single note and plain in its means, and its charm lies in how completely it commits to one trick.

**The single moment that best captures the piece: 0:12.25, the first bass drop** (-71 to about -19 dB in one second, the whole mix about 5 dB louder and narrower, after twelve seconds in which a voice and one drum hit every 1.55 s have made the listener need it). It is the piece's whole method in one event: absence built up, then weight delivered, and everything afterwards is the same trick repeated until it becomes ritual. The picture's contribution, the darkest, grainiest clip arriving 0.4 s early, adds to the effect but is not needed for it.
