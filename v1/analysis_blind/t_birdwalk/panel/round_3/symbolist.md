## 3. The thread I want to develop: the face that is looked at and the face that looks away

Everything survivable from my earlier motifs reduces to one thing I can see without hearing. The film sets two ways of being seen against each other. Home-made clips (the trio, the bedroom, the driveway) show bodies doing a dance for nobody: they look at each other, at the floor, at the wall screen. The packaged layers (the puppet at 0:00, the hooded man at 0:39, 1:29, 3:07-3:10) look *at us*. The caption strip then speaks to us in the same direct way ("AVAILABLE NOW"). So the film is a negotiation between the dance as a thing people do among themselves and the dance as a thing offered to a camera for sale. Then, after the sound has gone, it gives the room back to the first kind: the trio at 3:12 flings its arms with nobody's voice to follow, and the last face, at 3:20, is turned slightly away, eye up and to the side [seen, f_0201]. The last face is not performing at us.

I put this forward as interpretation: the data cannot test it and it rests on three or four shots. The sceptic can fairly answer that a man turning his head at the end of a take is just that. I would say only that the editor kept the shot, and the *placement* of the last shot is the one structural decision the picture alone owns.

I also want to be plain about what I am dropping. Drop: object permanence, "the face returns as the ground returns", "borrowed bodies", "best formal gesture", "wry deflation", the lit-corner reading. Keep: the gaze as a two-way structure; the humour that the film shares with the viewer, shown by the home-made bird-box with a drawn mouth held up like a microphone at 3:01-3:05 [seen, sheet 10], which someone made on purpose.

## 4. Settled reading (5 sentences)

1. t_birdwalk is a promotional single built from borrowed and home-made bird images, whose structure is almost entirely the music's twelve-second removal and return of the bass, with the picture following loosely, by feel and a few seconds late.
2. Beneath that, its recurring subject is *how a dance gets offered to others*: bodies doing it for themselves in rooms and driveways, and a hooded face and a puppet doing it to the lens, with a caption turning both into a sale.
3. The feeling is affectionate and comic, not parody: the box with a drawn mouth, the solemn child, the puppet before a lettered wall are funny whether or not anyone meant them to be, and the film's sincerity about being "available now" is what makes them so.
4. The sadness some of us feel in the last minute comes from departures that the film does not reverse (voice at 2:47.5, caption at 3:04 and 3:19, sound at 3:08), and I hold that as an effect on the viewer, probably produced by a length mismatch rather than by design.
5. What remains certain, being seen and measured, is the repeated yellow, the repeated bird, the repeated hooded face, and the fact that the last person we see is looking slightly elsewhere.

**The single moment:** 3:12-3:20, after the sound has ended: the trio flinging their arms with nothing to dance to, and then the close-up of the hooded face turned slightly away, one eye glancing off to the side, still lit and orange in the frame at 3:20.
