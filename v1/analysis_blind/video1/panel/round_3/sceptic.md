## 2. What I found when I checked my own questions

**2.1 The 1:40 slam lands in the middle of a shot, with no cut.** `shots.json` has shot 20 running 1:27.16 to 1:48.68 (DATA). f_0100 (1:39), f_0101 (1:40) and f_0102 (1:41) are all the same continuous bleached snow-ridge shot. In f_0100 the ridges are dark against a pale sky. In f_0101 the haze is thicker and a vertical scratch runs through the frame. By f_0102 two small figures have walked into the left edge in near white-out (SEEN). So my round 2 worry has an answer, and it goes against my own hope: the sound and the picture did not change together. The picture is one 21-second shot that thickens gradually, and the largest step in the music (about +35 dB of bass in 1 s, music reading) arrives inside it. The dossier's "blizzard from 1:40" is a slow worsening, not an edit. This does not show intent either way. It does show that nobody cut to the slam. I now side with the film critic's "mismatch" on timing as well as on content, while holding that "considered refusal" is an inference. A single long shot placed under a 21-second wall is equally what you get if the editor simply laid one long piece of weather footage under a section.

**2.2 The 2:47.8 hinge is tighter than the detector says, and my f_0169 "held banner" was wrong.** The detector puts the banner shot at 2:48.8 (shot 41, 168.8 to 169.52). That is the tightest picture-and-sound coincidence I can find in the film, tighter than 1:40 and tighter than anything in the beat-phase statistics. (b) The banner is on screen for under 0.7 s, then cut. I said in round 2 that f_0170 "shows the crowd continuing after the banner, so the banner is a single held shot, not a rest". ## 3. Answers to the questions put to me

**First-timer: is a quarter-beat lag the signature of an editor reacting to a heard beat?** It is a possible reading and I cannot exclude it, but my reasons for not leaning on it are these. n = 39, R = 0.365, p = 0.0055 against a null that ignores that the tracker's own offset is unknown. A fixed tracker offset of 0.25 s, which is routine for onset-based beat trackers, produces the same number from an editor who cut in perfect time. And a human reacting to a heard beat would lag by about 0.1 to 0.2 s, but would not stay consistent across cuts unless working to a click. Viewers would not feel a lag of 0.25 s as "following"; they would feel it as "slightly late" or not at all, because on a 0.77 s beat this is a third of the interval. So: possibly a human delay, possibly an artefact, not detectable as a feeling. Too few cuts to say anything about intent.

**Insider: is the structure just race-day order?** I made this point and the insider, music critic and film critic have all moved to "chronology gives the skeleton, the music gives the placement". I accept that. The remaining distinction I would insist on is the one the film critic drew: choices that survive are those a race day does not supply (the recording-apparatus run, the long hut hold, the recycling of sign, headline and refrain).

**Music critic: what test would I accept for a cross-modal claim, and does "5 of 9 boundaries near a cut" count?** I ran the test myself. Using eight music edges (0:32, 1:15, 1:27, 1:40, 1:55, 2:13, 2:38, 2:48) and the 51 detected cuts, the nearest-cut distances are 10.4, 1.6, 0.16, 8.7, 0.68, 0.36, 0.06 and 0.8 s. Five of eight are within 1 s of a detected cut. Against 10,000 random placements of eight edges in 5 to 185 s, at least five matches occur 18 per cent of the time. Not significant: with 51 cuts in 188 s, a cut is never far. (a) A usable claim needs a hand-made cut list including dissolves and whip-pans, since the detector misses 0:32, the 1:15 hut cut, the 1:55 kettle cut and the 2:48 whip. (b) The two failures at 0:32 and 1:40 are the two the panel cares about most: the voice-leaves moment at 0:32 sits inside a 30-second shot, and the bass slam at 1:40 sits inside a 21-second shot. So: "the picture was cut to the section edges at some boundaries" is only supported at 2:48 (see 2.2) and, loosely, at 1:27 (0.16 s) and 2:38 (0.06 s). Those two are 0.06 and 0.16 s apart, which is inside the detector's own timing error of about one frame (0.04 s) plus the music edge tolerance of 1 s given in the music reading; I rate them as "consistent with", not "evidence". Three of eight, with one tight and unmistakable, is the honest count.

**Music critic and film critic on the voice as a gate.** I made a half-concession last round. The 2:47.8 data adds one thing: the closing word ends as the dropout ends. That is three exits (0:32, 1:27, 2:48) all falling on a section boundary, but the music reading lists eight boundaries, so there are five boundaries without a voice exit. "The voice leaves before the heavy passages" is true of the three heaviest, which is what the panel is describing. I would still say "gate" is a description of a pattern, not a rule.

## 4. Where I agree and where I still disagree

Agree: (a) the 2:47.8 hinge is the best moment in the film, and is better than the panel said (2.2); (b) the ending is composed as a recap (sign, headline, refrain return in the last 25 s); (c) the recording motif is real as "the film notices cameras", and not demonstrated as "witnessing". I also agree with the music critic that the final wall at 2:48 is reached by addition (bass centred, width, a new cell) not by new pitches.

Still disagree: (a) "stoic heroism" is a mood label derived from steady tempo and flat voice; the footage shows a hard day, not a hero. (b) "Anonymity as a theme": the FINISH crowd and 2:41 to 2:46 shots are faces. (c) The 42.6 Hz sub as "heartbeat" at 10 events per minute, a loose metaphor. (d) The symbolist's exposure arc: the exposure follows weather and time of day. (e) "Quietly ambivalent" as a verdict. It is the default of an honest record, not a position.

## 5. Concessions this round

I withdraw "a single held shot" for the banner (2.2). I withdraw the hope that 1:40 might coincide with a cut (2.1). I withdraw the confidence of the detector's cut times everywhere near a whip-pan. I also concede, with the film critic, that "refuses the beat" and "cut to the beat" are both beyond the data. I withdraw "tape" and "start line" as anything but a coin toss.

## 6. The thread I find most interesting

How much of the panel's "craft" can be separated into what the music does, what the footage supplied, and the few places where the two are demonstrably tied. After this round the third group has exactly one member: 2:47.8 to 2:48.3, where the picture goes dark and the banner arrives on the same 0.4 s as the drum hole. Everything else (the slam under a continuous shot, the hush beside a tea scene, a procession of names beside signs) is two layers sharing a clock without evidence of being cut to each other. That is not a criticism. A film in which music and picture cross only once, and do it exactly, is a more interesting thing to point at than a film in which they cross everywhere.

## 7. Settled reading in five sentences

1. This is a three-minute newsreel-style record of a mass winter skating race in the Netherlands (Dutch headlines, signs and spoken names are on the frames and in the transcript), laid over a steady 78 BPM drone and drum track with no tune, in which the music builds and drops its weight at its own pace while the footage follows the order of a race day.
2. The mood is hard, steady and mechanical, with the newspapers' own words ("zwaar", injured, chaos) on screen; I read it as an honest record of an ordeal rather than a celebration or a protest.
3. Picture and sound share a clock only loosely: cuts sit about a quarter-beat after the tracker's beat with weak significance (R = 0.365), the bass slam at 1:40 lands inside a 21-second shot, and the voice leaves before the three heaviest passages.
4. The one demonstrable tie is at 2:47.8 to 2:48.3, where the drums fall away by about 40 dB for 0.4 s, the picture goes black mid whip-pan, and the FINISH banner arrives on the returning beat.
5. The ending (repeated headline, refrain and DOKKUM sign, accelerating cuts, a drum roll and a hard cut at 3:08.2) looks composed as a recap, but most of what the panel calls meaning, including heroism, witnessing and the heartbeat, is interpretation laid on top of footage that records a cold, crowded, difficult day.

## 8. The single moment that best captures the piece

2:47.8 to 2:48.3: a smeared crowd, a black frame, a 0.4 s hole in the drums, the last word of the narration ending, and a banner reading FINISH arriving on the first beat back. It is the only place where I can see and measure the two layers meeting, and it is also the place where the film stops describing the race and arrives at it.
