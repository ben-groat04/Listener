## 1. Conceding and agreeing first

**The Sceptic is right about the 12 s lattice, and I missed it.** In round 1 I reported only that cuts hit section edges. I re-ran it [MEASURED]: with a 12 s grid starting at 2:36.6, 8 of 11 grid points (2:36.6 to 4:36.6 and the 4:48.6 point) have a cut within 0.2 s; the cut offsets at 2:36.6, 2:48.6, 3:00.6, 3:12.6, 3:24.6, 3:36.6, 3:48.6, 4:00.6 are 0.00, 0.04, 0.08, 0.16, 0.00, 0.12, 0.08, 0.00 s, then 0.76, 0.68, 0.60, 0.36 s. As a control I slid the same 12 s grid by 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 s: those give 2, 2, 2, 1 and 4 hits of 11. So the hit rate belongs to the music's own phase, not to the grid's spacing. Caveat shared with the Sceptic: I chose 12 s after reading the music, and it has a 20% cut density to hit by luck (31 cuts in 2:36-4:00 is one per 2.7 s, so about 15% by chance per point at a 0.2 s window).

**I withdraw "the picture is cut to the section edge only".** The music reading says loop B is a 12 s (6-bar) cell [MUSIC]. The editor followed that cell, not the 2 s bar and not the beat (sync.json: beat 37% vs 43% chance; bar-lattice Rayleigh p about 0.78). That is the signature of someone cutting on the loop-change point, where a cell restarts and the bass returns to B, rather than to the kick. As a producer I find this entirely natural: in a DAW or an NLE you place markers where the harmony turns over (every 12 s the tonic comes back), and the kick, being constant, gives no landmark. It tells me the editor was working from the bass loop's turnover, which is the one event in loop B that is audible as a "start". That also explains why the lattice drifts after 4:00: from 4:12 the mix is at its loudest and the chops are dense, so the loop turnover is the least audible, and the cuts loosen exactly when the marker is hardest to hear. That is a hypothesis [SPEC], but it fits.

**The Sceptic is also right on "exactly".** The narration ends at 2:35.8 and the loop changes at 2:36.6: 0.8 s apart [MUSIC]. I wrote "exactly" in round 1; I should have said "within a bar's half". The nearer fact is that the voice stops on the last bar of loop A's second pass, which is a producer's convention (clear the vocal before the section turn), not a miracle.

**Insider and Symbolist on 1:28.6: agreed, with a measurement to add.** The speech is cut at the instant the band lands (vocal stem -34 dB at 1:28, -74 dB at 1:29 [MUSIC]). In arrangement terms that is an ordinary "vocal out on the drop" move, which in pop and dance production exists so the drop has air. What is unusual is that the voice is the *content* here. I agree with the Insider that the picture crowds in as the narrator leaves; the music's side of that is a gate, not a fade.

**Sceptic's point 5, "build vs plateau", mostly stands.** I said the same in section 4 of round 1: the mix moves +3 to +8 dB in the top band and the real surprise is the stop. I would keep "heat rising" as my own gloss [SPEC] but accept "louder and denser to a limit".

## 2. Answers to questions put to me

**Film critic Q1: is the grid so rigid that hand-cut picture will look slightly off, and does not-locking help?** Two parts. (a) The grid really is rigid: kick interval 499.99 ms [MUSIC], so a cut that is a frame (about 40 ms at 25 fps, assumed, not checked) off the beat would be audible to a trained ear against the kick, and an amateur editor cutting by feel would land 80 to 200 ms around beats, which is what the data shows (37% inside 80 ms, chance 43%). So the lack of locking is consistent with hand-cutting by eye. (b) A rigidly locked picture would also have read as a music video, and this does not; I cannot say it was a choice. What I can say is that the *loop-cell lattice* is the opposite of random, so the editor was listening to the structure, not ignoring the audio. On the brightness ramp (2:50 to 3:48.6): I would not rely on it to carry sparse picture. +22 dB is on the "other" stem's 4-10 kHz band only; the full mix gains +3 to +8 dB [MUSIC]. A listener in a normal room hears "slightly brighter and more present" over a minute. That is enough to feel as slow lift, not enough to be the reason the picture works. The flatness 1:40 to 3:28 is more stillness than drive: loudness within about 2 dB. I think it works as stillness only if you are willing to be still.

**Symbolist Q1: is the voice's change from speech to texture to clock-tick a deliberate metaphor, or just how a producer treats a sample bank? What in the music decides?** I can give evidence for the middle and the end; the middle is weak. What the music contributes:
- Narration (0:42-2:36): free of the bar (the ten or eleven onsets have no common phase), band-limited, dry [MUSIC]. It behaves like a found recording laid over the loop.
- Chops (3:28-4:36): about half of 36 bursts fall within 0.2 s before a bar start (chance 10 to 20%) but do not lock to the 6-bar cell, and a 67-onset test gave 31% within 0.12 s of a beat against 48% by chance [MUSIC]. So they are bar-anchored loosely and anti-beat: a pickup feel. That is a producer playing a sample or MIDI-triggering it by hand (or quantised to bars with swing), not sequenced to the grid.
- Calls (4:46.6-4:58.6): one per bar at bar phase 0.00-0.03 on the kick-only lattice [MUSIC]. That is quantised: a sequenced or pasted event.
So the *behaviour* does shift from free to bar-aware to exact. It is a real progression in the data. Whether it was meant as "people becoming numbers" I cannot decide from the sound alone, and I will not claim it. The simpler producer's reading is also compatible: a sample used first as content, then as rhythm, then as a marker; that is how voice samples are routinely retired in a track (they become a drum). I would rate the metaphor reading medium-low and the "producer's drift from content to rhythm" reading medium. The two are not exclusive.

**Insider Q2: does the abrupt stop at 5:00.86 read as grief or as a machine switching off?** In sound terms: it is a switch-off with a decorated tail. The rhythm section stops at 5:00.86, then three off-beat hits (5:02.34, 5:02.60, 5:02.85) with the tail below -45 dBFS after 5:02.9 and digital zero from about 5:04.2 [MUSIC]. No reverb throw, no slow fade, no cadence; the calls were absorbed into the machine just before (one per bar). That leaves the stop with no emotional "wind-down" cues (no ritardando, no falling line, no harmonic resolution), so by the usual vocabulary of elegy it is *not* an elegy. Grief would normally ask for a sinking line or a held chord. I read it as a withheld ending, and the viewer supplies grief or shrug depending on the picture. Therefore I side with the Sceptic: the sound alone does not choose, and "closed door" is my gloss. I do think the three off-beat hits matter: they are the first off-grid events after four minutes of rigid 120, and they leave an after-image of the grid being broken only once the machine is off. That is a detail of craft, not proof of intent.

**Sceptic Q2: is the second-half voice-like layer human, sample or instrument, and does it survive without Whisper?** Without Whisper's strings, what survives: (1) a vocal-stem route with 50 ms peaks at about -16 dB and bursts under 0.6 s with 20 dB decay [MUSIC]; (2) a pitch track wandering G4/G#4/A4, with G# not a chord tone in the bass or "other" [MUSIC]; (3) calls quantised to the bar at the end; (4) a 26-45 cent wobble at 4-6 Hz on the 4:29-4:36 phrase (specialist figure, not re-measured, so low-medium). Against: demucs can route a processed synth or guitar to "vocals"; CLAP says "no vocals" at 0.999 but also says so over the narration, so it is no evidence. My honest position: plausibly a human-derived sample (chopped, gated), at medium-low; instrument not excluded; and no word-level claim survives. The spectral centroid test (only 1.0 to 1.8 times that of the narrator) argues against a simple pitched-up copy of the narrator, so this is probably not the same recording sped up, but that does not tell us it is another person.

**Sceptic Q3: remove "funerary", "closed door", "heat rising", "unresolved". What is left?** At 2:36-3:28: the voice has gone, the bass has a three-chord loop that now touches B every 12 s, and one layer gets 8-10 dB louder at 2:47-2:50 then slowly brighter. In plain terms: a long, static, warmer stretch with nothing to follow. An ordinary viewer would notice quiet absence of commentary and a stable, repetitive groove. At 5:00.9: an abrupt stop followed by three hits. An ordinary viewer would notice that the track simply ends on a bang rather than fading. Those are plain observations. The "feeling" words are mine. What I will defend: the *shape* of both (absence, then abrupt stop) is measured; the *emotion* attached is inference, as I said in round 1.

## 3. Pushing back

- **The Symbolist's "home arrives exactly when the people leave" [Symbolist 3].** The music supports the timing (loop B starts at 2:36.6, narration ends 2:35.8, and B is the tonic). But "home" is my own word from round 1; the measurement is that the *tonic recurs more often* (every 12 s against one block in seven in loop A), and my CQT check did not reproduce the specialists' 34% vs 13% pYIN shares (B about 13% in loop B) [MUSIC]. So the difference in "homeness" between loop A and loop B is small and rests on the position of one note in a repeating cell. I would not hang the irony ("a settled key is not a settled life") on it as strongly as the Symbolist does. It is a good sentence; the data is thinner than the sentence.
- **The Symbolist's bells in, slams out.** Five long strikes at 0:00, 0:08, 0:12, 0:20 and 0:24 [MUSIC] against three off-beat hits at the end. The numbers do not match (5 vs 3), the strike sounds are different (long-ringing, 20 dB decay in 1 s, versus short hits with a tail), and the framing is the Symbolist's. I accept a loose mirror (struck low sounds at the edges of the track) and nothing more.
- **First-timer / Sceptic on "hypnotic vs numb".** I will give a producer's answer: the 3:36-4:36 plateau is five repeats of one 6-bar cycle at nearly constant loudness. The only variable is voice-density and brightness. For a listener who attends to the chops it is a hypnotic loop with something to follow; for one who does not, it is a loop. The picture has to vary, and the Sceptic says it does: that is the best argument for why the film works when the track alone might not.

## 4. The thread I want to develop: the editor listened to the loop, not the kick

The 12 s lattice is the strongest cross-modal fact in this dossier, and it changes how I read two things.

1. **It dates the edit's attention.** The cuts follow the 12 s cell from 2:36.6 to 4:00.6 (seven of seven within 0.16 s) and the big section edges before it (0:28.1, 1:40.6, 2:36.6, 3:28.6, 3:36.6 within about 0.1 s). But they do not follow the 1:10 drum stop (0.8 s away) or the 1:24.6 drums-and-bass stop (2.5 s away) [MEASURED]. Those two stops are, in my round 1 and the Sceptic's, among the best moments in the first half. So the editor was cutting to *where a loop restarts or a section begins*, not to *where the sound drops out*. That is a distinction a producer would make: loop turnovers are visible on a waveform as repeating shapes; a stop is also visible, but a cutter marking section starts rather than events would miss it. I find it interesting that the best-judged sonic gestures (subtractions) are the ones the edit ignores, and the picture does the opposite (an empty road at 1:28 jumping to a bridge crowd as the kick lands, per the Sceptic's frame checks). The cut for 1:28.6 is missing from shots.json (nearest 1.67 s away) but the Sceptic confirms the shot changes at f_0089/f_0090; I did not reopen those frames, so I take that on his word.
2. **It makes the 4:12-4:36 drift meaningful.** From 4:12.6 the offsets are 0.76, 0.68, 0.60, then 0.36 at 4:48.6. That window is where the mix is loudest (4:12-4:24) and the chops are densest. If the editor was keying to an audible loop turnover, it is the stretch where the turnover is least audible. I would be happy to be shown a different explanation (a change of source reel is the obvious one, and the film critic can say if the footage type changes at 4:10).
3. **It reframes "flat middle".** The cuts at 12 s intervals give the loud plateau a visible pulse of its own: a new shot every 12 s on a six-bar cycle, whatever the pack is doing. That is a quiet, rather good way to make a repeating track feel as if it is going somewhere, and it is a decision about *form*, which is exactly what I said in round 1 the arrangement was lacking at 3:36-4:36.

I would not call the editor a collaborator with the musician; the lattice might equally come from the editor receiving a cue sheet of loop-turnover timestamps, or from an automated tool. The data cannot separate these.
