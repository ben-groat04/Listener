## 1. Answers to the questions put to me

**Sceptic: does black at about 5:04 and digital zero at 5:04.24 change my view of the fade?** Partly, and I have to correct both of us. I opened the last frames. f_0304 (5:03) is a floe on plum water at full strength; f_0305 (5:04) is a very dark plum-grey frame, not black; f_0306 (5:05) is black [SEEN]. So the picture is not at black at 5:04; it is nearly black at 5:04 and fully black about a second after the sound reached zero. The two channels finish within about 1 s, not 0.2 s. That is still a close landing, and I concede the Sceptic's wider point: the sound hits its last hit at 5:02.3 to 5:02.85 and its tail dies by 5:04.24, while the picture is dimming across those same seconds. The fade is therefore timed to the *tail*, not to the *slam*. What the slam gets is the picture still on the floes at full strength (f_0304 at 5:03 shows no dimming yet). So the picture "sighs" only after the hits have landed. I withdraw my round 2 remark that the fade is the "least interesting choice" in the sense of arbitrary: a default fade-to-black at the end of a timeline would also look like this, and I cannot tell the two apart [SPEC]. What I can say is that the sound's decay and the picture's dimming overlap, so the ending reads as one gesture. I do not claim more.

**First-timer: the burn holes in the ducks at 4:52, kept on purpose?** I opened f_0293 [SEEN]. Two large, bright, ragged-rimmed discs sit on the right of the frame over a rough circle of open water with ducks and gulls; the rims are dark and crinkled, like holes burned or bubbled through the emulsion. They are the same kind of mark as the three white discs I described at 2:37 (f_0158). Whether the editor kept them on purpose I cannot know; the footage plainly has them. What I can say is that a picture editor with a different roll to choose from would have had to *choose* to leave a shot with two blown holes in it at the film's penultimate scene, and the film also lets in the 3:37 burn flash and the 2:08 whiteout. So the evidence favours an edit that tolerates damage, which is what "handled with respect" meant in my round 1, and I would not have trimmed it: the holes are the only place in the last minute where the film itself is visibly failing, and that is the right place for it. That is a judgement, not a finding.

**Symbolist: does 2:36.6 make the second half a different kind of film, and name one frame that shows a person as a person.** I keep the claim narrow: the *method of cutting* changes at 2:36.6 (shot length 5.2 s before, 2.7 s after; cuts on the 12 s cell for seven of seven points [MEASURED, Sceptic]). I do not claim a different kind of film, because the people do not turn into numbers all at once. Frames after 2:36.6 that show a person as a person: f_0178 (2:57) is a blurred woman at the left, half eaten by a flare, a farmhouse behind her [SEEN]. She is a person but barely, and the flare is eating her; I would not offer it as a face. The better answers are f_0251 (4:10, the young man in the fur collar, accepted by the Sceptic's own check) and, new this round, **f_0273 (4:32)**: a red bobble-hat from behind and a shoulder in a heavy, hairy jumper, blurred and very close, against blue shadowed snow [SEEN]. There is no face, but the hat is a particular object belonging to one particular body, and the camera is close enough to feel its warmth. Bibs make people into numbers; a knitted hat with a bobble does the opposite. So the film's later people alternate between number (bibs 201, 529) and particular (hat, collar). That corrects my round 2 line that "face-to-number run side by side": it is better as *number and particular, interleaved*.

**Music critic: do the shots at 4:12 to 4:36 differ in kind from 2:36 to 4:00, so that source explains the drift?** I tested the pace argument first and it fails. Mean shot length from 2:36.6 to 4:00 is 2.7 s (31 shots); from 4:00 to 4:36 it is 2.7 s (13 shots, shortest 0.72 s) [MEASURED]. The pace is identical, so "fast cutting loses the 12 s habit" (Sceptic, round 2) is not supported on this data, though the detector under-splits whip-pans and the real rate in 4:00 to 4:20 is probably higher. What does differ is the footage: from 3:09 to 4:00 backs and bibs on dull ice; from 4:06 faces, head-on packs, a bleached backlit wide, sunglasses, a helper steadying a skater [dossier], plus the red-hat close-up (f_0273) that is the most saturated and nearest in the film. That is a change of camera position and of subject, and my [SPEC] reading from round 2 stands: the editor let the picture lead where the material became richer. The 12 s habit and the loudest music (4:12 to 4:24 [MUSIC]) do not coincide with any musical event I can name; the Music critic's own reading gives nothing at about 4:10. I therefore treat the drift as footage-led, not music-led, and I now think that is also the answer to "dialogue or own clock": at the section joints the picture is in dialogue with the music; at the loudest bars it keeps its own clock.

**Music critic to the Symbolist (picture at 4:46 to 4:58).** Not addressed to me, but I opened it. f_0287 (4:46) is a low-angle view of a pole, insulators, wires and a red-and-white banner against cyan sky, and f_0291 (4:50) is legs on scored ice [SEEN]. ## 3. Agreements, pushback, concessions

- **Sceptic on faces: conceded.** Faces occur at 2:36, 2:57 (poorly), 4:10 and 4:35 and are fewer where the camera follows from behind. My round 1 "withholding" was an effect of geometry plus the editor's decision about how much of it to keep, and I will say "ordered", not "withheld" (First-timer's word, and a better one).
- **Sceptic on "exactly" at 2:36.6: conceded.** The picture cut is on the section edge; the voice ended about 0.8 s earlier. I stop saying the voice "leaves exactly" at the loop.
- **Sceptic on colour: conceded in full.** Picture warmth and brightness do not track the music (r about 0.2 and 0.03 [sync.json]).
- **Insider on the demographic: accepted.** 575 racers to about 9,500 tourers means a crowd of mostly ordinary people; the anonymous mass is not a distortion. I withdraw any reading of the bibs as coldness.
- **Pushback on the Sceptic: "mechanically organised but not rhythmically felt."** The cut density does speed up when the voice leaves, and the handheld pack footage is not mechanical. A cut that holds a 12 s habit for 84 s *and* lets a skater's lunge and a hat run free inside the cell is a decision about priorities: the grid governs the joints, the material governs the interior. That is not "not felt"; it is how a cutter treats a long loop he cannot hum.
- **Pushback on "closed door" and "heat rising"** is mine and I have already stripped them. What remains, stated without adjectives: five strikes then a groove; a four-second hollow then a drop with the crowd arriving on the cut; a voice that goes, then a cut to a road without people; a hard stop with a picture that is still bright and then goes dark over the next second.

## 4. The thread I develop: the film's order is a dialectic of number and particular

Over the second half the film alternates between things that count and things that cannot be counted. The narration counts (575, 9,500, 200 km, 16 below). The bibs count (201, 529, 157, 170, 48). The 12 s lattice counts, and the track ticks one call per bar. Against that: a woman half-erased by a flare, a hat with a bobble, a fur collar, panting mouths, a stooping skater against a bleached sky, duck-holed emulsion, floes with no shape. I would not claim a design, only a pattern of a kind a viewer registers without being able to say why: the machine supplies the framework (cell, tick, numbers), and the film stock and the hand-held camera keep leaking particulars through it. The edit obeys the framework at the joints and lets the particulars through between them. The end goes further: the last image of a person is legs (4:50), the last image of anything living is gulls and ducks, and the last frames have no one at all. The framework survives (the track ticks to 5:00.9) and the particulars are gone.

## 5. Settled reading (five sentences)

1. This is a handheld, small-gauge home-movie record of an ice-skating event in a hard winter (the title board gives 1963 and Friesland; the narration's figures give 200 km, 575 racers and 9,500 tourers), cut to a machine-steady 120 BPM loop by someone who tied the main changes of picture to the music's section edges (the 1:28.6 drop, 1:40.6, 2:36.6, 3:28.6, 3:36.7) to within about 0.1 s while ignoring the beat.
2. The most skilled thing the edit does is to hold back people and then give them, in order: no one until 0:53, a crowd on the cut with the band at 1:28.6, anonymous packs with bibs from 3:09, a few faces and a red hat at 4:10 to 4:35, and nothing at all at the end.
3. From 2:36.6 to 4:00 the picture is cut to the music's own 12 s cell (seven of seven within 0.16 s) at an average of 2.7 s a shot, the point at which the voice has left and the editor stops cutting to events and cuts to the machine; after 4:12 the grid loosens as the footage turns to faces and the loudest bars, which I attribute to the material, not to tiredness.
4. The colour drift, flares, burn holes and whiteouts belong to the stock and are tolerated rather than designed; they carry the "archival" feel and the music does not drive them.
5. The ending is a plain, well-timed landing, not a statement: the track stops dead at 5:00.9 on floes at full strength, and the picture dims across the tail to black a second after the sound reaches zero, which I read as craft and as a stopping place, not as grief.

**The single moment that best captures the piece: 1:28.6**, where the cut from f_0089 (an empty road with four tiny walkers) to f_0090 (a railing full of onlookers) falls within 0.05 s of the kick landing on every beat after four seconds of no drums and no bass; the whole film's method (subtraction, arrival of bodies, edit keyed to the music's formal edge and not its pulse) is in that one instant.
