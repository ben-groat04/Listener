Elfstedentocht, Parts 1 and 2

Public Service Broadcasting's two-part score for the frozen 1963 race.

Listening accounts written by Claude from a close analysis of the sound and picture. No one at the keyboard heard the music. The first-person voice is how it would land on an attentive listener, and every fact in it has been checked.

Nothing happens, and I couldn't look away

I came out of this one braced and a little hollow. Five minutes of an unhurried beat under pictures of people skating through a Frisian winter, and by the end I felt the way you do when someone has been in a room with you for a long time without speaking, and then leaves.

The moment that stays is the quiet one just before the drums come back. The music had been walking along with a narrator reading over it. Then the drums dropped out, then the bass, and there was only the dark hum of the pad and that one flat voice saying that this, what everyone is getting ready for, is the Elfstedentocht. On screen, a long still shot of a snowy road with footprints in the foreground. Only the little knot of people moved, slowly, towards me. It went on a beat longer than was comfortable, and then everything slammed back at once, the drums far louder than anything before them, and the picture jumped to a bridge so crowded the people were a smear. It was like a door kicked open on a quiet house. I actually sat back. After that the drums held steady right up to the final stop. The piece does get louder and brighter over the next couple of minutes, but nothing else hits like that.

It opens so quietly I thought something was wrong. A dark board with white capitals, De Winter van 1963 in Friesland, and one low thud that took ages to fade. Then frost on branches against a grey-blue sky, held far longer than a modern edit would dare, with a few slow thuds drifting over it. I liked that. It asks you to settle. When the beat finally arrives it lands exactly on a cut, from a bright blue park to bare black trees against a lilac sky, and the whole thing starts to walk.

The voice is the other thing I keep thinking about. It does not sing and it does not keep time with anything. It is dry and even and a bit thin, the sort of voice you might half-remember from an old film. It goes through dates and distances in its own rhythm while the music goes about its business underneath. "Eighteen January 1963." Sixteen degrees below. More than two hundred kilometres. Then, about half-way through, it reads out the numbers: 575 racers, about 9,500 tour skaters, and a list I could not make out. The voice stays dry and even throughout, so the feeling has to come from you. When it finished, the music shifted underneath and the picture cut to the first shot from a moving car, along a road lined with frosted white trees. After that the voice never spoke in sentences again, and I missed it more than I expected.

The music itself is a steady, patient pace with a slight limp in it. It put me in mind of a slow march, or of the long middle stretches of those minimal electronic records where the point is that nothing changes and you stop waiting for it to. I will admit that for a stretch in the middle I was bored. Skaters on dark glossy ice, farmhouses, a telegraph post, low light; the same groove, nothing announced. But somewhere in there it had been getting brighter and louder without ever speeding up, and by the time the camera was in among the skaters, close behind dark figures bent forward with their arms held behind their backs and white numbers on their backs, the cuts coming quick, I was leaning towards the screen. It pulled forward without hurrying, like being towed.

There are faces in there, and I looked for them. A young man seemed to glance towards the camera in a flurry of other faces and was gone. Coated onlookers stood at a barrier by a road sign. A figure in a long coat walked away across blue ice. Nobody is followed, nobody is named, and nobody wins. The pictures are mostly ordinary, and the faces that can be seen mostly look cheerful or relaxed. If it is sad, and I think it is, the music is doing that, not the pictures.

The last stretch is what I will remember. The bright shimmer on top falls away and the beat goes on a bit bare. A little later the low notes are cut into short jabs, a short voice-like sound lands on each bar, and the hum underneath turns hollow. The camera drops to a skater's legs over scratched ice, close and unsteady. Then the picture ends on ducks and gulls on grey-blue water, then standing on broken ice, then cracks, then slabs of ice on rose-brown water. The ice was not a floor any more.

And then the groove stops dead. The hum rings on alone for a second and a half or so, which felt much longer. Two knocks, close together, with a low note under them. The picture starts to dim as they sound and is near black about a second after the sound has gone. It felt less like an ending than like something being taken away.

Afterwards I looked it up: nearly ten thousand people set out that day, and 127 finished. I did not know that while watching, and it did not change what I felt. This is a very patient piece of music laid over a lot of people out on the ice, and it never tells you how to feel about them. I did not like it all the way through; the middle asked a lot of me. But I would sit through the middle again for the room going quiet at the road, and for the knocks at the end.

Cold, and a banner that says FINISH

I came out of it braced, the way you are after standing in a wind for a while. Not sad, not lifted. Tired for the people on screen. The drums run from the first second to the last with hardly a stumble, and over them sits a thick, grinding, heavy-booted wall of sound with no tune to hum and no chorus to wait for. It is stern. It put me in mind of a long march, or of a factory heard from the pavement outside, something enormous going on whether or not you are paying attention.

The picture is old black-and-white film of a skating race across frozen Friesland, and for the first half minute I did not know what I was looking at. A smear, a dark band over a pale cloud. Then a crowd at night in front of a lit building, nearly black, all heads and blur. A newspaper front page tilts into view announcing the Elfstedentocht for Friday; another calls it "superhuman hard". Somewhere in there a dry Dutch voice comes in and reads out a date, 18 January 1963, and later an hour that sounded like half past five in the morning. It does not tell you a story. It reads facts out, like a bulletin on a station you half-tuned in. The band does not make way for it. The voice drops into the spaces the music happens to leave and then gets swallowed again.

What I felt first was the cold. Frosted faces on a dark path. Someone stares straight into the camera; a few seconds later someone runs at the lens, rime on their clothes. A skater lurches. Later the voice gives the temperature, sixteen degrees below zero, and the frost on those faces had already said it.

The first moment that stayed with me is a small one. The voice finishes its sentence, what everyone is preparing for, that is the Elfstedentocht, and stops, and the music opens out, wider, as if a door had been pushed back. The picture is a hazy windmill, a wooden platform, a telegraph pole, then a line of skaters, tiny and black on a white plain. It felt like being let out into the open and finding it enormous and empty.

Then the first real surge: loud, bright, the pictures coming fast. Numbers on backs filling the frame one after another, a blur of ice and legs. I was swept along with it.

And then the floor went. The deep rumble that had been holding everything up simply vanished, and what was left was lighter and edgier and not at all comfortable, like a stair that is not there when your foot comes down. A registration table, hands writing on forms, the voice giving figures that sounded like 575 racers and about 9,500 tour skaters. Paperwork. I drifted. The sound was thin, the picture was forms and gloved hands, and my attention loosened.

What woke me was the stretch after the voice goes quiet. For about twenty-five seconds there is no bass, and when the band comes back at 1:27 it is wide and loud, and the picture opens out to match: tiny skaters in a white void, then a single skater small beside reeds and a fence. One slow, stepping line floats up top, not quite a tune, more like someone walking carefully across thin ice. Then the bass came slamming back. Not louder so much as heavier, like a hand laid flat on your chest after you had got used to nothing there. It was the best moment in the film for me, relief and weight together, over a pale ridge of windblown dunes with two specks of skaters coming into it. The picture had changed a breath before, which made it feel almost inevitable.

After that it is loud and busy and sprawling: rooftops seen from high above, a skater climbing a wooden ramp, a rough handheld scramble of collars, legs, crowds lining a street, people heaving one another over rough ground.

Then the thing I did not expect. The rumble leaves again, the music shrinks to something thin and high, and the picture is a kettle pouring, cups and saucers on a patterned cloth. Four or five people on a bench in hats and hoods, drinking from white cups, one of them looking straight at me. It was the quietest, most exposed place in the whole piece, and into it drop half a dozen very slow, very deep thuds, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. A face in a snow-crusted knitted cap, mouth open. A hunched figure. Someone swigging from a bottle. A skater kneeling in the open snow, then getting up again. This is where the film turned human for me. Everything before was a crowd and a plain; this was people drinking from cups in the cold. It was the one place I felt tenderness, and it caught me off guard in something this hard-faced.

The voice comes back and reads the towns of the route one by one, a little higher and slower, with signs flashing up near the names: IJLST, WORKUM, DOKKUM. A small aeroplane over a white plain, a helicopter. It has the cadence of a litany, and the music walks along underneath it. The bass returns, the opening sentence comes round again, the same words if not necessarily the same take, and I felt everything closing in on a point.

Then a hairline flicker where the drums and bass drop away, and a white banner cuts in with black capitals: FINISH. A crowd in hoods behind it. And the sprint begins, wide and steady and relentless. It does not build. It holds one level of effort and will not let go, while the pictures race past: numbers, a skater bent double on the ice, a blank white plain, the "zwaar, zwaar, zwaar" headline again, cracked ice and a skate.

What I wanted was a skater crossing a line. In the pictures I looked at, nobody is seen crossing one, and no winner is named or shown. The banner says FINISH and the music says arrival, but the picture gives people in peaked caps (role unclear), a crowd, blur and glare. I found I liked that more than I expected. The music promises an ending and the film steps round it.

The ending itself is a shrug. The sound tapers away in about half a second and stops, with no cadence. The road sign for Dokkum again, an indoor hall with a banner announcing a report on the race, then black.

I would call it brave more than beautiful. It is relentless and a bit stubborn, and in the paperwork stretch it tried my patience. But when the bass came back after that thin wait, and in the hush of the bench with its cups and slow thuds, it got to me.

1. The set as one work

Public Service Broadcasting's "Elfstedentocht Part 1" (3:09) and "Part 2" (5:04) were commissioned for the 50th anniversary of the race of 18 January 1963 (Explore the North festival, Leeuwarden; footage and voice-over from the Fries Film Archief; context.md) and released together as a two-sided single (MusicBrainz; the mapping video1 = Part 1, video2 = Part 2 is high confidence, see section 8). Together they tell one account twice. The words are one Dutch archive script (the date, the hour, the cold, "more than 200 km", 575 racers and about 9,500 tour skaters), the pictures draw on one pool of Frisian film, and the music is the part that changes: a loud, flat, band-led piece on a D pedal at about 78 BPM in Part 1, and a quiet-to-loud, loop-built electronic piece on a B pedal at 120 BPM in Part 2. Part 1 gives the event as a record (print, paperwork, signs, a banner); Part 2 gives it as a place and an effort (frost, pack, dusk, water). Neither film shows a winner, the finish or the finishers. The pair is about the run-up and the effort, and it ends twice without arriving. The best one-line description from the team is continuous in material, restarting in narration, re-set in music (story and music, round 3; INT, M). Its clearest finding about the work is that, with script and footage held roughly constant, the sound decides what the same material means (music, round 3; INT, M-H).

3. How the two parts talk to each other

What they share (OBS unless marked). - One script, in the same order. Both give the date, then the marathon and cold lines, "twelfth", the title sentence, "16 degrees below zero", "more than 200 km" and the 575 / 9,500 line, in much the same sequence (Whisper medium on both mixes; Part 1 also has a second "twelfth" line at 0:40.6, and the town list). It is the same words, not shown to be the same take: cross-correlation of the title sentence between the parts is at control level, though stems are separated and processed (music, round 2; M that the words are shared, L on the take). - One footage pool. Four shots match across the two films, grey in Part 1 and in colour in Part 2: bib 170 (video1 1:05, video2 3:34), bib 145 (1:08, 3:30), bib 157 (3:00, 3:51) and a striped-headband skater (3:02, 4:44). Picture confirmed them by eye; four is a floor, found at 1 fps with low recall (H on the shots, M on the order). The order is the same apart from one swap, and the spacings differ (the last two are 2 s apart in Part 1, 53 s apart in Part 2), so Part 2 is neither a stretched copy nor a shuffle: INT, M-L, "a recut from the same rolls". No narrated sentences sit under any of the four in either film, but Part 2's voice fragments (3:28.6 on; Whisper "Eerst" 3:30.6, "Slop" 3:34.5 to 3:35.9) overlap the 3:30 and 3:34 shots (story, round 3, corrected on check). - Same method of drama. Both are pedal pieces with no tune and no cadence; the feeling is carried by layers, level and timbre. Both put the title sentence directly before a rhythm-section hole (video1 2:47.8, 0.4 s; video2 1:24.3 to 1:28.6, about 4 s). The team settled on "habit of the mix, the same stage direction used twice", not "signature" or "rehearsal" (SPEC); the hole is sound-led. Part 2's picture holds one static dyke-road shot (1:22.1 to 1:30.3; round sign, footprints, two or three tiny walkers) through the hole and the drop; the picture does not mark the drop, and the next cut is 1.7 s later. Part 1 has no cut at its hole either. - Shared dry, band-limited narrator, centred against a wide band (music, round 1).

What they do not share. No riff, melody or bass line (searched; none found, H on absence), no key (D against B), no tempo (78 against 120), no drum behaviour, and no narration after 2:36 in Part 2. The old "same pulse" idea (a 0.385 s hat in Part 1, a 0.375 s bass figure in Part 2) was withdrawn by its author as coincidence.

Does one answer the other? Not as an argument. Part 2 opens about 15 dB under Part 1's end, which is relief in level, but it restarts the script at the date and re-sets key, tempo and kit, which is restart in content (music, round 2; M). The only real hand-over is footage: Part 1's last race burst (video1 2:55 to 3:02) is the kind of material Part 2 spends about two minutes on (listener: "Part 2's plateau returns to the part of Part 1 that was over too soon", INT, M). Note that the shared shots are not held longer in Part 2 (two longer, two shorter); the time is gained by more shots (listener R3 check).

4. The arc across the set

There is no single arc, and the team dropped "the pair rises then falls". What can be said:

  1. Two loudness shapes. Part 1 sits flat and loud; by K-weighted loudness its end (2:48 to 3:08, about -7.0 to -6.4 LUFS) is level with 1:07 to 1:15 (peak about -6.6 LUFS at 1:07), not louder. Part 2 builds from a quiet pad, peaks about 4:12 to 4:36 and is dismantled from 4:36.8. Only the order of release links them; the "staircase" across both is a description, not a design (music and listener, round 3; M).
  2. Two registers of picture. Documents first, then place and pack: Part 1 leans on print, desks and signs and ends on a banner and black; Part 2 leans on pack, crowd and landscape and ends on water. This is a tendency, not a division of function, because Part 1 also has empty frosted fields and Part 2 also has road signs and lorry lettering (picture and story, round 3; M).
  3. Neither film arrives. Part 1 lists the route (2:16 to 2:36) and ends mid-effort on a banner. Part 2 withdraws to water. Research context only (elfstedentocht_1963.md, not from either film): 127 official finishers, the winner Reinier Paping (10:59) and 9,862 starters; none of these appears in either film (OBS on the absence; INT, M on its meaning).
  4. Pace of the telling. Part 1 is compact (facts, a list, a sprint). Part 2 states the same facts more slowly, then spends two minutes with no new facts and ends by subtraction. Read in order, the pair goes from telling to staying with it (INT, M-L).

5. Disagreements and open questions

These are real and unresolved, not smoothed over.

  • "Variation" or "second telling". Music prefers "variation" (the sound is what differs); story prefers "second telling" because the narration restarts at the date, which is a narrative device. Both can hold (round 3).
  • Which part works better. Listener: Part 1 alone (events spread through the run, ends level with its loudest K-weighted stretch), Part 2 as the one that stays after it (the long subtraction, the clean joint at 1:24.3 to 1:28.6, the probable bar-lock). It is a reasoned expectation, M to M-L (round 3).
  • Does Part 2's picture follow its music better? Listener and picture said yes (bar-start cluster; round 3); on check the drop itself is not marked by a cut, so only the cluster remains. The cluster is post hoc, the window was chosen after looking, the 0.1 s lead is within detector and mp3 error, and the earlier team line was "no lock" (corrected in round 3). I reproduced 18 of 66 (section 8). Treat as M-L. Part 1 is not shown to be less aware: it has a beat-rate lagged cluster in two flurries, and the tracker may be offset, so edit habit against tracker phase is unresolved (music and picture).
  • Are Part 2's voice fragments town names? Story's hypothesis (an echo of Part 1's eleven names) was downgraded to L, leaning against. Re-weighed on check: Whisper medium (mix) does give nine short segments at 3:25.9 to 3:48.1 (Stief, Eerst, Slop, Stapen, In de lopen, In de banken, In de bonswacht, Vrijdiger, Vrijdiger) whose order loosely echoes Part 1's list (eerst, slotten/Slop, Staveren/Stapen, in de lopen), and nine equals the "nine main bursts" counted by music. That is mild evidence for the echo (SPEC). Against: the music reading finds the ASR passes mutually incompatible and calls these probable hallucinations; the bursts are longer (0.4 to 1.0 s against 0.03 to 0.58 s) and bar-quantised against free; no legible place-name sign was found in the picture (a road sign appears at 4:28). Now open, L-M, neither leaning; only a Dutch listener can settle it. Still excluded from the meaning of the pair.
  • Why was it split? A two-sided release is the only documented reason; "one account given two jobs" is INT, M-L; a pure format effect is not excluded (SPEC, L). No source says how the two were commissioned.
  • The hole. Whether the 0.4 s dip in Part 1 registers at all on a first hearing is open; the listener thinks it may not, with the banner covering it. The 4 s hole in Part 2 is hard to miss (INT).
  • Same image, effort or routine? Bib 157 under Part 1 (loudest run, glimpsed 2 s) is expected to read as effort; under Part 2's plateau as routine. Faster or slower is genuinely mixed (Part 1 slower in tempo, denser; Part 2 twice the beat rate, calmer). The listener calls for a viewer test.
  • Agreement is weaker than it looks. All colleagues read the same dossiers, and Whisper on this material has been wrong before (story, round 3; ERRORS). Part 1's Whisper line "Bedankt voor het kijken!" (3:07) is not used anywhere: it falls at the "Reportage" banner (3:07) in a stretch where no colleague reports speech, so I treat it as a likely ASR artefact (my note, SPEC).

6. What a human takes away

All of this is INT, expected rather than reported.

  • Same words, different weather. The strongest impression is how much the score decides: the pair is a demonstration that identical facts and near-identical footage can feel like a bulletin in one film and a vigil in the other (music, listener, story; M-H on the facts, M on the meaning). It is best met in order, because only then does the constancy show.
  • Part 1 is the telling, Part 2 the room. Part 1 is urgent, stern and eventful; Part 2 lets a viewer who already knows the facts stay with the cold, the pack and the ice.
  • What lingers is a subtraction. The end of Part 2 (bright layer off, bass gated, groove cut, a short drums-and-bass tag from 5:02.34, then water) is the passage most likely to stay, and it rests on the order of removals, not on colour (listener; M). Next, for a viewer who notices it, a small private recognition of bib 170, grey then blue (SPEC, L-M), and Part 1's bass return at 1:40.0 on speakers that carry it.
  • No arrival. One leaves with the run-up and the effort, not the result. A Dutch viewer would add hindsight (the last such winter for 22 years; context.md, round 2 limits); it is not in either film as an observed property.
  • The script is noticed before the shots. A viewer is more likely to notice the date coming round again (video2 0:41.7) than four recurring shots (listener, picture, story; SPEC, M-L).

"Elfstedentocht Part 2" (Public Service Broadcasting): a report

About five minutes. Timestamps are m:ss on our file (305.9 s). The official track is 5:04, so times may sit about 1.9 s from the release. Tags: OBS = measured or seen; INT = interpretation.

How this music was heard

No one has listened to this audio with ears. Everything about the sound comes from measurement: separated stems (drums, bass, voice, everything else), spectrograms, chord and pitch estimates, and loudness. So "dark", "hushed" or "brittle" below are readings of those measurements (INT). The measurements are solid (OBS); the feelings are a reconstruction.

The music

This is a loop-built electronic piece at a steady 120 beats per minute. It has no sung vocal, no tune and no cadence. The same seven notes (B C# D E F# G A) are used throughout. B minor is the simplest name, because the pad sits on B; the first-half bass spends most of its time on E, G and D, so an E-centred hearing is equally possible (INT). It never changes key or resolves. The drama is made by layers, loudness and the human voice, not by harmony or tempo.

The opening (0:00 to 0:28). A single dark, hushed pad, with isolated low thuds and long tails at 0:00, 0:08, 0:12, 0:20 and 0:24. There is no pulse, bass or voice, and almost nothing above 4 kHz. It is very quiet. It feels like a held breath, or a cold morning heard through glass (INT).

The stride begins (0:28). Bass and a sparse drum pattern walk in under the unchanged pad, and the overall level steps up about 7 dB. These are the first footfalls, still muted and dark.

A voice, then a band that holds back (0:42 to 1:24). At about 0:42 a dry, band-limited Dutch archive voice begins, "18 januari 1963". It is the first human sound after 42 seconds of pad. The sudden brightening at that instant is the narrator's own consonants, not a new instrument. The voice leads and the band frames it. The bass plays one long note per four seconds (E, G, D, E, G, D, then a B). The drums are intermittent and nearly vanish around 1:01 to 1:03 and 1:10 to 1:15. The effect is spacious, documentary and a little solemn.

The hole and the drop (1:24 to 1:28.6). The drums vanish at about 1:24.3 and the bass at about 1:25, for roughly four seconds. Only the pad and the narrator remain (the mix drops only 2 to 3 dB), and the narrator names the subject: "Waar iedereen zich voor opmaakt, dat is de Elfstedentocht" ("what everyone is preparing for, that is the Elfstedentocht"). Then everything returns on one downbeat at 1:28.6, with the drums about 50 dB louder, a four-on-the-floor kick and no fill. Its force comes from the gap before it and the lack of any warning (INT); it is the biggest single event in the piece.

The locked stride (1:28.6 to 2:36.8). From here to 5:00 the pulse never changes. The drums sit at a flat level with the kick on every beat. The bass plays a figure grouped in threes against the kick, so it lands with the kick only on beat 3 and never on beat 1. That gives a steady foot-tap with a slight limp (INT). It also fools an automatic beat tracker, which reports 161 BPM instead of 120. The narrator still speaks over the top in free time, a human voice carried by a machine.

The last sentence and the clearing (2:36.8). The narrator's last word, at 2:36.1, closes a head-count of 575 racers and about 9,500 tour skaters, and the voice never speaks in sentences again. A new loop begins, with the bass changing note every 12 seconds (B, A, D), and one layer partly drops out. It sounds like a sudden clearing in which the music must carry the scale of the crowd alone (INT).

The trance and the plateau (2:48.8 to 4:36.8). For about 47 seconds nothing new happens and the loop is hypnotic. Then pressure builds with no single moment announcing it. One layer brightens by roughly 22 dB in its top frequencies (about 3 dB in the full mix), fastest between about 3:26 and 3:50. Its notes and rhythm stay the same, so it sounds like a filter opening or the layer being pushed harder, not like new instruments; how it was made cannot be recovered. Overall level creeps up to a peak at about 4:24 to 4:36, roughly 15 dB above the opening.

In the same stretch the voice becomes something else. From about 3:28.6 it returns as short fragments, possibly wordless, that seem to recur once per two-second bar and grow longer (the bar timing comes from the dossier and was not re-tested here). Whether they are sliced archive speech, a vocal-like synthesiser or leakage from another layer cannot be decided. Brightness, loudness and fragment length rise together, so the intensity climbs without anyone being able to say when. It feels settled but straining: a long, determined effort over a pulse that never speeds up (INT).

The unmaking (4:36.8 to 5:04). The track ends by taking itself apart, in this order.

  • At 4:36.8 the bright layer falls away (about 13 dB at the top). The pulse goes on, so the energy seems to drain while the machine continues.
  • At about 4:48.6 the bass is cut into short stabs, and a single unpitched voice-like call lands on each bar. The pad loses its third and goes hollow, so the texture is brittle and lurching.
  • At about 5:00.8 the groove stops dead, and the pad rings out alone for about one and a half seconds.
  • The closing tag, 5:02.3 to 5:02.9. Two drum hits (5:02.33 and 5:02.58), measured. Under them the bass sounds as one sustained tone from 5:02.33 to about 5:02.86, with a brief dip at 5:02.6; the music analysis hears this as three notes a quarter-second apart (5:02.33, 5:02.58, 5:02.83), but the signal alone resolves only the dip and the end (INT). The drum hits are as loud as the groove. Then silence, with digital zero from about 5:04.25.

It reads as withdrawal and a stop, not arrival (INT). "Calm" fits the surface and the gesture, not the energy.

How the picture works with it

The film is colour archive footage of the Frisian winter and the race, credited to the Fries Film Archief; the exact reels and the narrator are not identified. It opens on a title board, "De winter van 1963 in Friesland", then frost and snow over the pad. A snowbound churchyard appears as the date is spoken at 0:42, which turns a mood into a dated day. Colour arrives at about 0:48 with a pink-gold ice road, followed by small solitary figures: a lone coated figure at a footbridge (about 0:57) and a woolly-hatted face in purple light (about 1:00).

The picture does not follow the beat as far as measured. Against the 120 BPM beat grid (fitted to the kick drum), 36% of the 86 cuts fall within 80 ms of a beat, against 32% expected by chance, and cut timing within the beat (p about 0.09) and within the four-beat bar (p about 0.2) is not distinguishable from chance. The bar test assumes a four-beat bar counted from the drop and is untested beyond that. (The tracker's 161 BPM grid gave 37% against 43%, but that grid is wrong and is not used.) In the first 42 seconds only 10 cuts exist and they lean towards one point in the bar (p about 0.01); with so few, and several tests run, I treat that as a hint only. Cut rate and image motion rise and fall loosely with loudness (correlations about 0.2 to 0.3, which are weak, and the cut-rate result did not survive a shuffle test). That cuts follow the narrator's phrase boundaries before 2:36 is an impression (INT), not tested. A few joints are tight:

  • At 0:28.08 a cut to cold, low-contrast light lands within about 0.05 s of bass and drums entering. This is the tightest match in the piece.
  • At the drop (1:28.6) the picture does not cut. One static shot of a snowy road (trampled footprints, two or three tiny walkers) runs from 1:22.1 to 1:30.3 straight through the hole and the drop; the cut to the first crowd, a bridge with spectators, comes 1.7 s after the drop. So the picture ignores the biggest moment in the music.
  • At 2:36.6 a cut to the first shot from a moving vehicle, on cleaner, whiter stock, lands about 0.5 s after the narrator's last word and about 0.2 s before the new loop at 2:36.8.

From about 3:13 to 4:33 the camera sits inside or directly behind the pack: backs, bibs, hands behind backs, a few faces. Nobody is followed. Cuts are quick (median about two seconds). Relief comes in a few long holds: the wide ice (about 3:00 to 3:09), the bright canal and sidecar (3:46), and a backlit golden skater bent double (about 4:10.5 to 4:12). Shot by shot the pictures are mostly ordinary or cheerful, so any sadness comes from the pairing with the music (INT).

At the end the picture turns away from people. From about 4:52 there are ducks and gulls on open water, then broken floes on rose-brown water. The closing tag sounds at 5:02.3 to 5:02.9; the picture starts to dim at about 5:02.5, is about a third of full brightness when the audio reaches digital zero (5:04.25), is near black from 5:05 and fully black about 5:06. This edit shows no finish line, no winner, no named person and no credits.

Meaning and context

The race was held on 18 January 1963, the twelfth Elfstedentocht, about 200 km through Friesland. Published sources give about 570 racers and about 9,300 tour skaters starting, and 127 finishing. The narrator's figures (575 and about 9,500) are close, but whether the voice speaks from before the race or looking back is unknown. The piece was commissioned for the 50th-anniversary Explore the North festival in Leeuwarden and released in autumn 2013; no band statement about it is known. The 2012 tour was cancelled, and 1963 remained the benchmark for the hardest race. A viewer who knows this will hear the piece differently from one who does not.

The shape is announcement, endurance, withdrawal. The voice speaks in sentences until 2:36. A long loudness-led stretch with no explanation follows. Then layer, low end, people, sound and picture are removed in turn. A free-timed human voice sits over a rigid modern grid and lines up with it only where its sentences end, at 1:28 and 2:36 (OBS). The recurring subject is a place: a road receding into white, with small figures on it and sometimes none.

Elegy or celebration is genuinely open. The minor key, cold palette and subtraction ending point towards mourning. The festive four-on-the-floor kick and the forward-looking words point towards communal effort. The best-supported word is withdrawal; "mourning" is weaker (INT). "Thaw" or "the end of winters" is a cultural reading carried by the 2012 cancellation, not by the pictures, which show only ice that is no longer a floor (INT). A fair short description is an unspoken afterword: a confident voice recites its figures and leaves, and a steady modern pulse carries anonymous bodies across the ice, brightens, and is taken apart until only water and the closing tag remain, two drum hits at 5:02.33 and 5:02.58 over a bass tone that lasts to about 5:02.86. Whether you feel grief, pride or a quiet leaving depends on what you bring.

The moments that stay with you

  1. 1:24 to 1:30, the hole, the drop and the empty road. A gap, a named subject, then force with no preparation. The picture stays on the empty road until 1:30.3, then cuts to the first crowd.
  2. 2:36, the last sentence. The head-count ends, the loop changes, the layer thins, and the voice never returns as speech.
  3. 0:42, the date over the churchyard. The first human sound after 42 seconds of pad.
  4. The last 25 seconds. The bright layer gone, bass stabs, ducks and floes, the stop at about 5:00.8, the closing tag (two drum hits, 5:02.33 and 5:02.58, over a bass tone to about 5:02.86), the fade.
  5. 4:10 to 4:12. A young man glancing at the lens, then the golden skater: a glance within a flurry, not a held close-up.
  6. 0:28. The stride begins, and sound and picture turn together within a few hundredths of a second.

What we could not settle

  • The instruments cannot be recovered from the measurements; no banjo or guitar is claimed.
  • How the late build is made, and what the late voice fragments are, are open.
  • The narrator and source films are unconfirmed. The trusted words are the date, the title sentence, 16 degrees below, over 200 km, and 575 and about 9,500. The time of day near 1:00 ("half zes" against 6.30 or afternoon in other passes) is contradictory.
  • Whether the closing tag (5:02.3 to 5:02.9) was meant as a deliberate full stop is not decided by the signal.
  • The tonal centre (B or E) is a hypothesis.

Elfstedentocht Part 1: a cold dawn, a heavy step, and a bass that comes back

Checked against: v1/data/video1/music_views/per_second_levels.json, v1/data/video1/music_views/chords.json, v1/data/video1/music_views/summary.json, v1/data/video1/stems/stem_rms_per_second.json, v1/data/video1/lyrics/ (Whisper Dutch and translate transcripts), v1/data/video1/sync/sync.json, v1/data/video1/frames_1fps/f_0024.jpg, f_0121.jpg, f_0168.jpg and f_0170.jpg (opened full size), dense 12 fps frames cut from videos/video1.mp4 at 2:45 to 2:50 and 2:58 to 3:08 (viewed as a contact sheet; scratch, not stored), v1/logs/ERRORS.md, 2026-11-17, status: checked (accuracy pass T1)

Public Service Broadcasting, "Elfstedentocht Part 1" (3:09). Black-and-white archive film of the Frisian ice race of 18 January 1963, scored by a loud, relentless instrumental rock track.

The short version

A calm Dutch voice gives the date, says it is half past five in the morning and mentions a distance of about two hundred (the noun is unclear) for those strong enough, and sixteen degrees below zero follows. Underneath, a heavy, grinding step that never relaxes. For about a minute and a half the band builds; then at 1:15 the bass vanishes, and for about 25 seconds the music floats with no floor under it (bar one faint blip near 1:28). When the bass slams back at 1:40, it is the track's cathartic hit. It lands harder than its loudness suggests precisely because it was withheld. Everything after is a long run towards a bright, loud, cymbal-driven sprint that ends, at 3:08, with the effort still going. In the frames sampled (and in dense 12 fps frames around 2:45 to 2:50 and 2:58 to 3:08) I see no skater cross a line; at about 2:49 the frame shows a man in a flat dark cap seen from behind, a man in a peaked cap at his left, others around them and someone holding a camera-like object; no skater or bib is clear (f_0170 opened). The music tells you to arrive; the picture shows you cold, crowds, paperwork and tea.

How to read this report

I cannot hear audio or watch film as you do. The music was "heard" through measurements: separated instrument stems, spectrograms, chord and pitch tracking, loudness curves. The picture was read from sampled frames, about one a second. Feelings below are reasoned inferences from those measurements, not listener data. Where something is uncertain I say so in plain words, once.

The music

It is D-centred and static. The track sits on a D drone at about 78 beats per minute (the tracker's local tempo ranges from 78 to 152 BPM; median beat interval 0.766 s), with a fast flutter of drums over it. That the pattern never changes speed is inferred from the stems, not proven. The chord detector labels D for about 94 s and Dm for about 33 s of the 189 s, but its 143 chord changes are partly reactions to distortion rather than real changes (doubt: moderate). What the stems and chords do support is a D-centred drone, an A, D, G walk from about 2:15 (chords.json labels Am, D, G from 2:15.6), and chromatic friction in the low figure of the final sprint. There is no verse, chorus or tune. The third of the chord is almost never played, so the mode is not settled (D major and D minor score almost equally): it sounds stern and driven, not sad and not triumphant.

The drama is subtraction. Three things are switched on and off: the bass, a dense, distorted "wall" layer (probably guitars, though the stems cannot tell guitar from synthesiser), and the narrator. The drums keep going for the whole track; their only break is a 0.4-second hole at 2:47.8. The composer here is, in effect, the mixer.

The journey, in order:

  • 0:00 to 0:10, snap and gather. A swell and a hit at 0:00.7, drums already running, the wall joining at about 0:01, the bass entering at about 0:03 as a step (quiet at first) and then rising over roughly 17 seconds to full weight. Layers arrive one by one on a fixed pulse: anticipation.
  • 0:10 to 0:32, told a story. The narrator gives the date ("18 januari 1963"), the hour ("half zes in de ochtend") and a distance of about two hundred ("tweehonderd", noun unclear) for those strong enough, ending on the sentence "Waar iedereen zich voor opmaakt, dat is de Elfstedentocht". The band does not duck for him. A calm voice over an engine that never relaxes.
  • 0:32 to 1:15, momentum. The voice stops on what looks like a bar line and the band opens up, brighter and wider. Short bursts of narration ("16 degrees below zero", "a monster ride of more than 200 km"), the wall swelling markedly, the hi-hats opening from about 1:05, loudness climbing to a first peak around 1:07.
  • 1:15 to 1:27, the floor falls away. The bass cuts out at about 1:15, the mix gets noticeably quieter and the wall thins to bright upper lines. Light, suspended, slightly uneasy. The narrator comes in at about 1:19 to read out the numbers: 575 racers, about 9,500 tour skaters (transcribed as 575 and about 9,500: ASR, unconfirmed; research gives 568 and 9,294).
  • 1:27 to 1:40, lift without ground. The voice stops; the wall surges, the stereo picture doubles in width, loudness comes back, and a slow stepwise line floats over no bass at all. This is the track's one melody-like moment, and the nearest thing it has to yearning.
  • 1:40, the landing. The bass returns. This is the moment most listeners would point to. Loudness barely changes (about 0.2 to 0.5 LU on a later ebur128 measurement quoted in v1/analysis/video1/interpretation.md; the music reading's own K-weighting gives 0.1), but the bass itself leaps from almost nothing to full weight and the stereo image pulls into the centre. You feel weight and relief, not volume. It works because of the 25 seconds you waited.
  • 1:55 to 2:38, held breath, then setting off. The bass leaves again and the music empties to a thin, high, narrow block, one of the two thinnest passages of the piece (the other is the bass-less stretch at 1:15 to 1:27), and 1:55 to 2:13 is about as quiet as the bass-less 1:15 to 1:27 (within a fraction of a unit on the level meter). Into it fall six very low, identical thumps at 42.6 Hz, one every 6.15 seconds from 2:01 to 2:32, like a slow second clock over the unchanged flutter. On a phone speaker these largely disappear, and what you feel instead is the wall dropping. From about 2:13 the wall thickens, a walk through A, D and G begins, and the narrator reads the towns of the route one at a time.
  • 2:38 to 2:48, weight resumes. The bass comes back, the chords turn, and the opening sentence returns at about 2:44, so the line that began the piece comes back to frame it, and the band then answers it. Whether it is the same recording or a re-cut, the measurements cannot say. Then comes the hole: for about 0.4 seconds the drums and bass drop out while the narrator's last syllable decays and the guitar holds. A held breath that ends in release rather than shock.
  • 2:48 to 3:08, the sprint. A steady, bright and wide stretch (width 0.7 to 0.86), level in loudness with the first peak around 1:07 to 1:15 rather than louder than it. The loudest raw stretch of the track is 1:40 to 1:55 (peak about -9.5 dBFS at 1:52, per_second_levels.json). Mostly D by the chord detector, with a gritty, circling low figure (chromatic friction around D) and a cymbal-led kit. By feel it is the riff and the payoff: hard, grinding, relentless. The exact notes cannot be recovered from the recording, but the character is plain. It ends in a quick taper of about half a second, with no cadence, as if the effort carries on offscreen.

The voice. It sounds like one voice, band-limited and centred; whether the repeated line is a re-cut is unresolved. It supplies facts and nothing else; the band supplies the feeling. It enters into space the band has cleared by subtraction (1:19, 2:15) and leaves at changes of state. By the vocal stem, speech runs 0:09 to 0:32, 0:41 to 0:56, 1:19 to 1:27, 2:16 to 2:36 and 2:44 to 2:48; no speech is transcribed from 1:27 to 2:16 (the stem shows only brief, wordless blips near 1:03 to 1:05, 1:46 to 1:48 and 1:57 to 1:58) or from 2:48 to 3:08. The first long gap spans the band's return, the bass slam at 1:40 and the thin tea-table passage; the second is the sprint, so the last word goes to the band.

How the picture works with it

The footage is made largely of print, paperwork, signs and crowds. Skaters are mostly anonymous bent figures seen from behind or at distance, in bibs. I saw no clear smile in the frames sampled, though most faces are small, dark or blurred. The cold is everywhere: rime on caps and sleeves, frosted reeds, a ground blizzard at about 0:51 to 0:56.

Some of the joins are plain to see:

  • The towns. As the narrator reads the route from about 2:15, five or six of the eleven towns are heard and shown within about two seconds of each other (ASR timing varies by up to 3 s): IJlst is shown but its spoken name is unconfirmed; the others are the Vrouwe van Stavoren inn, "WELKOM IN WORKUM", BOLSWARD / BOALSERT, FRANEKER / FRJENTSJER, DOKKUM, then a signpost to Leeuwarden. Sneek, Sloten, Hindeloopen and Harlingen have no sign in the frames. This is the tightest word-to-picture match in the film. Language says where, the band says how hard, and the faces are left unlabelled.
  • The numbers. The registration count at 1:19 to 1:27 plays over a registration table, writing hands and bib 157.
  • The tea tables. At 1:55 to 2:14, under the thinnest music and the first thump, the picture goes indoors: a kettle, a row of young people drinking from white cups, one of the five looking at the lens (f_0121 opened; one or two others may), a frost-caked face, a van with a cross. This is the film's warmest and most exposed passage.
  • The ending. At 2:47 a whip-pan and a "FINISH" banner arrive within about a second of the hole. Then the sprint, with bibs, skaters on the ice, blurred crowds, the newspaper page again (2:57), a reused DOKKUM sign at about 3:06, a banner reading "Reportage ELFSTEDENTOCHT", and black.

Whether the cuts follow the music by design is not settled. Only 7.8% of cuts fall within 80 ms of a beat against 19.7% expected by chance (sync.json), so cuts are not on the beat. Their phase within the beat does cluster (single-beat Rayleigh p=0.0055, n=39), but that depends on the beat tracker's phase being right, the cut list misses fast changes in snow, and the half-beat and bar tests are no better than chance (p=0.95, 0.56). The fair description is two well-made layers joined at the narrator, with the picture clearly following the narration, rather than a proven counterpoint. Broadly, though, picture and sound do get busier together, but only weakly (r=0.27 for cut rate against loudness, weak and untested for significance, with the picture leading the music by a few seconds): the busiest cutting is about 1:06 to 1:16 and 2:58 to 3:08, the first at the first loudness peak.

What it means

The band gives stakes; the picture gives content. Drive, waiting, return and release come from the music. Faces, cups, cold and crowds come from the film. Put a cosy major-key bed under the tea scene and it would feel entirely different. Here it feels cold, effortful, collective and unglamorous.

Collective effort, not heroics. The film's units are places and documents: headlines, a registration table, tea, a control hut, town signs, the finish banner. No skater is named; the only print that could name a person is small newspaper print at 0:23, which I have not read (a name-like fragment sits beside the photograph, illegible at 1 fps and unreliable at full size). The narration reads out "575 racers, about 9,500 tour skaters" (ASR, unconfirmed; research gives 568 and 9,294), not a winner.

The subject is the effort, not the result. The sound has an arrival, the 2:48 sprint. In the sampled frames the picture never shows one; at 2:49 it shows a man in a flat dark cap seen from behind, a man in a peaked cap at his left, others around them and someone holding a camera-like object, and no skater or bib is clear. The panel agrees that viewers experience this; whether the makers intended it is undecidable. It is also only Part 1 of a two-part release, and archive cameras usually stood at control points, so the absence should not be over-read.

Context. The piece was commissioned by the Explore the North festival in Leeuwarden for the 50th anniversary of the 1963 race, "De Hel van '63". That year 9,862 started and 127 finished; Reinier Paping won in 10:59 (research context, not read from the film). The footage comes from the Fries Film Archief; which films were used, and who the narrator is, are not confirmed. The band's method is to let archive voices carry the story while the music frames them, and the archive hiss and grain are material, not flaws. Reception is thin but positive, centred on drive, physical effort and the fusion of image and sound. Readings of memory or ghostliness are the listener's, not demonstrated properties of the film.

The moments that stay with you

  1. 1:40, the bass slams back. The strongest contrast in the track, made by absence.
  2. 2:47 to 2:49. The closing sentence, the half-second hole, the FINISH banner, then the sprint, in which no crossing of a line is seen in the frames sampled.
  3. 1:55 to 2:14, the tea tables. The thinnest music and the film's humanest scene.
  4. 2:16 to 2:36, the town list, with five or six of the eleven towns shown within about two seconds of being heard.
  5. 1:15, the floor falling away, and the six slow thumps, if your speakers can reproduce them.

If you only watch one minute, watch 1:55 to 2:48, and watch it loud, on good speakers or headphones.

How this was made

The videos. videos/video1.mp4 is Public Service Broadcasting's Elfstedentocht Part 1 (3:09) and videos/video2.mp4 is Part 2 (5:04). Both were made in 2013 for the Explore the North festival in Leeuwarden, for the 50th anniversary of the 1963 race, using footage and voice-over from the Fries Film Archief. The part order is confirmed by the official track lengths on MusicBrainz.

How the agents "listened". No agent can hear. Each track was split into stems (vocals, drums, bass, everything else) with demucs, then measured: per-second loudness and stereo width, spectrograms in 20-second windows, chords, the melody line, tempo and beats, zero-shot audio tags (CLAP), and transcripts of the Dutch narration (faster-whisper, including a pass on the isolated voice). The picture was read from frames sampled once a second plus a keyframe per shot, and the link between music and picture was measured (cuts against beats, cut rate against loudness).

How the reading was built. 1. What a blind test showed. The whole pipeline was also run with no background facts and no web. It still worked out the subject (the 1963 Elfstedentocht) from the spoken date and the newspaper headlines in shot. Comparing the two runs showed that context biases as well as helps: the informed run "saw" a celebratory raised fist (in fact a raised hand) and a headline it could not actually read, both primed by the research. The blind run read more like a person responding. Those lessons are now rules in the toolkit's prompts.

Limits. The feelings described are reasoned from measurements, not heard. Instrument identity (guitar or synth) often cannot be settled from stems. Frames are sampled about once a second, so brief moments can be missed. Uncertain points are marked in the text.

Why the first version was rewritten. The first published reports read like an audit of a spectrogram: decibels, timestamps, confidence tags. You said, rightly, that this is not how a person listens.
